# The State of the Climate 2026: A Conversation with Zeke Hausfather

## 1. Episode Overview

**Guest Background:** Dr. Zeke Hausfather is a climate scientist and lead author of the upcoming IPCC 7th Assessment Report. Unusually for a scientist, his career has spanned the clean tech startup world, founding a company out of grad school before returning for his PhD. He currently serves as climate research lead for Stripe, deploying a billion dollars on permanent carbon removal technologies, while also working as a research scientist with Berkeley Earth, which produces one of the main global surface temperature records.

**Main Topics:** The conversation covers the latest 2025 climate data, the state of climate modeling, the political challenges facing climate science under the Trump administration, scenarios for future warming, and emerging technologies for carbon removal and potential geoengineering interventions.

**Key Thesis:** Despite political headwinds and uncertainty in climate sensitivity, the fundamental physics of warming is clear. The challenge now is navigating between mitigation, adaptation, and potentially controversial interventions like geoengineering and carbon removal, while dealing with the reality that **the 21st century's trajectory remains uncertain and highly dependent on policy choices made today**.

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### The 2025 Climate Data Release

**The Framing:** Host Bryony Worthington interviews Hausfather on the day the 2025 global temperature data was released, providing real-time analysis of what the numbers mean.

**Hausfather's Perspective:** 2025 was "quite warm" despite being a La Niña year (which typically produces cooler temperatures). It **tied with 2023 as the second warmest year on record** across eight different international research groups, coming in at about **1.44°C above pre-industrial levels**—just shy of the critical 1.5°C threshold.

**Key Insight:** The fact that 2025 matched 2023's temperature during a cooling La Niña phase indicates the climate system is "running quite hot." Hausfather predicts that with an El Niño developing in 2026, **2027 will likely be the next warmest year on record**, potentially breaking through previous records.

**Supporting Evidence:** The warming trend continues unabated despite natural variability that should have produced cooler conditions. This suggests the underlying human-driven warming signal is strengthening.

### The US Withdrawal from Climate Science

**The Question:** How is the Trump administration's hostility to climate science affecting the IPCC and research more broadly?

**Hausfather's Analysis:** The impact has been severe and immediate. **NOAA (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration) previously had the most scientists participating in the IPCC of any organization globally—now there is essentially one**, and that person only participated because they were retiring and "didn't really care" about the prohibition.

**Mechanisms Explained:** The person responsible for nominating US scientists to the IPCC was fired the week before nominations were due, so "the email bounced back" for anyone trying to apply. However, about 60 US scientists are still participating entirely from academic and private sector organizations, with **philanthropic funding stepping in to cover travel and logistics**.

**State-Level Response:** While states like California continue supporting climate research through their university systems, others like Texas are "policing what's on the syllabus" to ensure it's not "woke." The federal funding situation remains chaotic, with the administration proposing a **50% reduction in National Science Foundation funding** while Congress has been more resistant to cuts.

**Constitutional Conflict:** Hausfather notes that constitutionally, Congress controls spending ("the power of the purse"), but the Trump administration has set a precedent of "not spending money that Congress has allocated," leading to lawsuits with mixed results so far.

### Climate Models vs. Observations: How Well Are We Doing?

**The Framework:** Hausfather explains how climate scientists reconcile physics-based models with observational data to validate predictions and understand uncertainty.

**Key Research Finding:** In 2020, Hausfather and colleagues (including Gavin Schmidt at NASA) went back and collected **all early climate model predictions from 1970 through the mid-2000s**. Of 18 models assessed, **11 were "pretty much spot-on" with observations**, and when corrected for actual CO2 emissions (versus assumed emissions), **14 of 18 were accurate**.

**Critical Distinction:** Hausfather emphasizes that climate models are **"physics-based computations"—not curve-fitting exercises like in economics**. Models are deliberately kept separate from observational data so observations can be used to evaluate, not train, the models.

**Recent Performance:** The latest generation of models (accompanying the most recent IPCC report) generally perform well. Looking at the **last 15 years, the rate of warming is "slightly above but close to the middle of the range"** of model projections, though earlier model generations (2008, 2014) predicted somewhat less warming than observed.

**The Five-Year Question:** When asked if models predicted the anomalous warmth of 2023-2025, Hausfather cautions that **"it's hard to use a period as short as 5 years to tell us all that much."** However, models do expect some acceleration in warming even under current policy scenarios.

### The Aerosol Masking Effect: An Accidental Sunscreen

**The Revelation:** One of the most striking insights is that **sulfur dioxide emissions from fossil fuels have been masking about half a degree Celsius of warming**. Without man-made aerosols, warming would be closer to 2°C today rather than 1.5°C.

**Historical Context:** Starting in the 1980s-90s, regulations stripped sulfur dioxide from coal and heavy fuel oil to address acid rain. This solved one environmental problem but inadvertently removed a "sunscreen" that was cooling the planet. Hausfather describes this as **"a little bit of a termination shock."**

**The Uncertainty Range:** There's significant uncertainty here—the aerosol cooling effect ranges from **as little as 0.2°C to as much as 1.2°C** across different models. This massive range has profound implications for climate sensitivity.

**Recent Observations:** Over the past 15 years, there's been an **unprecedented decline in the reflectivity of low-lying clouds over oceans**—nothing like it in the satellite record going back to 1970. This appears to be one of the main contributors to accelerated recent warming.

**Three Possible Explanations:**
1. **Reduced aerosol emissions** (definitely part of the equation)—sulfur particles serve as nuclei around which clouds form
2. **A response to warming itself** (the more worrying possibility)—models showing the highest climate sensitivity also show the biggest reduction in low-lying clouds
3. **Natural variability** we haven't observed before (least likely, no clear physical mechanism)

### Climate Sensitivity: The Fundamental Uncertainty

**The Core Question:** If we double atmospheric CO2 from pre-industrial levels (280 ppm to 560 ppm), how much will the planet warm?

**The Physics:** CO2 itself would cause about **1°C of warming**. But this triggers feedbacks:
- **Water vapor feedback** adds another degree (every 1°C of warming allows ~7% more atmospheric water vapor, itself a powerful greenhouse gas)
- **Cloud changes** can amplify or dampen warming
- **Snow and ice cover changes** affect reflectivity
- Multiple other feedback mechanisms

**The IPCC Range:** The most recent assessment says there's a **66% chance (likely range) of 2.5-4°C warming for doubled CO2**, and a **90% chance of 2-5°C**. But even 5°C isn't a hard upper limit—it could be higher, which "would be really catastrophic."

**Putting 5°C in Context:** Worthington asks what 5°C means in practical terms. Hausfather's response is sobering: **"The last ice age was about six degrees cooler for the global average"**—with mile-high ice sheets across the northern hemisphere. Five degrees of warming represents an equivalent but opposite shift.

**The Equilibrium vs. Transient Distinction:** Hausfather clarifies that climate sensitivity refers to **equilibrium warming after 300-400 years** of constant CO2. By 2100, actual warming would be only about two-thirds of equilibrium warming because **oceans absorb heat slowly**. However, this also means warming continues for centuries even after emissions stop.

**The Ocean's Dual Role:** Initially, oceans are "our friend," buffering surface warming by absorbing 90%+ of trapped heat. But once emissions stop, they become "our enemy"—continuing to warm and driving further surface temperature increases. In climate models, **the cooling from reduced atmospheric CO2 and the warming from ocean heat uptake roughly cancel out**, meaning the planet doesn't cool for millennia after reaching net zero.

### Emission Scenarios: From RCP 8.5 to SSP 3

**The RCP 8.5 Controversy:** Hausfather addresses the debate sparked by Michael Liebreich and others about whether the high-end scenario (RCP 8.5) was realistic. This scenario was **the 90th percentile of no-policy outcomes** when published—intended as worst-case, not most likely.

**The Translation Problem:** The distinction between "worst case" and "business as usual" got "lost in translation" partly because all other RCPs used carbon pricing in their underlying models, even though some could represent no-policy outcomes.

**The Coal Question:** Critics argued RCP 8.5 assumed burning more coal than economically extractable reserves. Hausfather is "less convinced that was a strong constraint," noting that from 2007-2008, **global emissions were increasing 3% annually and China was building a coal plant every 3 days**—a coal-dominated century didn't seem far-fetched then.

**Current Reality:** Since 2010, RCP 8.5 has "very much moved out of the realm of possibility," largely due to **dramatically falling clean energy costs**. However, Hausfather notes a concerning development: **in 2025, the US burned more coal** due to higher gas prices, colder winter, and policies preventing coal plant closures.

**SSP 3: The Nationalism Scenario:** Hausfather highlights **SSP 3 as particularly relevant today**—described as "a world of resurgent nationalism and isolationism with fraying international ties, less priority on technological research and development." If this becomes the dominant framing of the 21st century, "we could end up in a much worse outcome."

**The Bifurcation:** Hausfather observes that **"the US has embraced its role as a petrostate while China has embraced its role as an incubator of the technologies"** of the 21st century—wind, solar, advanced nuclear, vehicle electrification.

## 3. Frameworks & Mental Models

### The Three-Step Climate Projection Framework

Hausfather outlines how climate projections work:

1. **Emissions** → How much CO2 and other greenhouse gases will humans emit? (This is economics and politics, not physics)
2. **Concentrations** → How much remains in the atmosphere? (Modulated by carbon cycle feedbacks—ocean and land sinks)
3. **Warming** → How much temperature increase results? (Physics-based climate feedbacks)

**Application:** This framework clarifies where uncertainty lies. We can model physics quite well, but predicting human behavior (emissions) is harder. Getting emissions wrong makes even the best climate model useless.

### The Aerosol Masking Mental Model

Think of aerosol pollution as **"accidental geoengineering"**—we've been inadvertently cooling the planet by about 0.5°C while simultaneously warming it with greenhouse gases. As we clean up air pollution (a good thing for health), we're removing this cooling effect, revealing the "true" warming from CO2.

**Application:** This explains why warming might accelerate even as CO2 emissions plateau—we're reducing the cooling aerosols faster than we're reducing warming gases.

### The Ocean as Thermal Battery

Oceans absorb **90%+ of trapped heat**, acting as a massive buffer that slows surface warming. But this creates a **"committed warming"** scenario—even after emissions stop, centuries of continued warming as oceans equilibrate.

**Application:** This framework shows why **"net zero doesn't mean zero warming"** and why carbon removal may be necessary to actually reduce temperatures, not just stabilize them.

### The CDR vs. Geoengineering Distinction

**Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR):** Treats the underlying disease by removing CO2 from the atmosphere. Expensive (~$300/ton for durable removal) but actually solves the problem.

**Geoengineering (Solar Radiation Management):** Treats symptoms by reflecting sunlight. Cheap (~$10 billion/year potentially) but creates dependency—**"like dropping a bigger and bigger ice cube in the ocean every year."** Stopping without CDR causes catastrophic termination shock.

**Application:** Hausfather and David Keith's framework: If geoengineering is ever deployed, it must be **strictly limited to replacing the cooling we currently get from accidental aerosol pollution** (~0.5°C). This bounds the commitment and keeps eventual CDR costs manageable.

## 4. Contrarian Takes

### Geoengineering Needs Upper Bounds, Not Blanket Opposition

**The Contrarian View:** Rather than opposing geoengineering outright, Hausfather argues we should **establish clear upper limits** on deployment if it's ever used.

**Reasoning:** The worst outcome isn't deploying geoengineering—it's **becoming addicted to it** by deploying too much. If we mask, say, 2-3°C of warming with sulfur injection, the CDR needed to ever stop would cost "trillions upon trillions of dollars" and take centuries. But replacing just the 0.5°C of aerosol cooling we're losing anyway creates a manageable exit strategy.

**The Nuance:** Hausfather acknowledges this is controversial: "I've seen a lot of people who were previously skeptical about topics like geoengineering...become more open to this idea because if you think that climate change is going to cause the end of the world, you're pretty willing to try anything."

### The Moral Hazard of Cheap Geoengineering Is Real

**The Contrarian Take:** Worthington suggests the moral hazard of geoengineering is declining because clean energy is becoming cheaper than fossil fuels anyway.

**Hausfather's Pushback:** He's less optimistic. While we're "picking the low-hanging fruit of decarbonization today," harder sectors remain (industrial heat, aviation, agriculture, buildings). **"If we don't include the cost of climate change in the cost of fossil fuels...through subsidies or taxes, we're still going to end up with a lot less mitigation than we need."**

**The Warning:** Technology can enable another "fracking-style revolution" for fossil fuels too—**"technology cuts both ways."** And if geoengineering costs $10 billion/year while mitigation costs $2 trillion/year, the political temptation becomes enormous.

### High-Quality CDR Doesn't Compete with Mitigation

**The Contrarian View:** Many argue carbon removal creates moral hazard by letting emitters off the hook.

**Hausfather's Response:** **Durable CDR costing $300/ton doesn't compete with mitigation** because 90% of emission reductions cost less than $100/ton. "If companies are spending $300 a ton on CDR instead of cutting their own emissions for less than $100 a ton, they're lighting money on fire."

**The Real Moral Hazard:** Low-quality offsets at $4/ton (like paying someone in Borneo not to cut down a forest). Hausfather notes wryly: **"If you pay people not to cut down forest, suddenly everyone wants to cut down their forest so they can be paid not to."**

### The Billion-Dollar CDR Bet Makes Sense

**The Contrarian Position:** Why spend a billion dollars on expensive carbon removal when that money could buy lots of mitigation?

**Hausfather's Defense:** These companies (Stripe, Google, Shopify, etc.) **are spending much more on mitigation**. The CDR investment is about **innovation and cost reduction**, not tons removed. 

**The Logic:** "Solar is a mature technology...I'm not going to move the needle much at all in the cost of solar panels globally." But when they started CDR work, **"the industry didn't exist. There was a shipping container in Zurich and that was the sum total."** A relatively modest billion dollars can have outsized impact on an emerging technology.

## 5. Practical Implications

### For Understanding Current Warming

**Key Takeaway:** We're experiencing accelerated warming not primarily because emissions are increasing (CO2 emissions have **plateaued over the last 10-15 years**), but because we're rapidly reducing planet-cooling aerosols while greenhouse gases continue accumulating.

**Implication:** Expect warming to continue accelerating in the near term even if emissions plateau, as we continue cleaning up air pollution. This isn't a reason to stop reducing aerosols (they cause acid rain and health problems), but we need to understand the climate implications.

### For Climate Policy and Politics

**The SSP 3 Warning:** The scenario of **"resurgent nationalism and isolationism with fraying international ties, less priority on technological research"** is no longer hypothetical—it's increasingly descriptive of current reality.

**Actionable Insight:** While Hausfather "tends to think the 21st century is long and climate isn't going away as an issue," the current political moment represents a genuine risk of policy reversal that could lock in higher emissions trajectories.

**The Bifurcation Opportunity:** The US retreat creates an opening for other nations. As Worthington notes, **energy security concerns may push countries toward domestic clean energy** precisely because the world order is breaking down. The "perturbance in the world order...may spread but it also may just cause everyone else to galvanize."

### For Technology and Investment

**The Three Long-Term CDR Technologies:**

1. **Direct Air Capture (DAC):** Most expensive today, but unlimited scaling potential if we achieve cheap, abundant clean energy (fusion by 2080?). No shortage of geological storage.

2. **Ocean Alkalinity Enhancement:** Adding alkaline materials to reduce ocean acidity, increasing CO2 absorption. **One-to-one relationship: every mole of alkalinity added removes roughly one mole of CO2.** Massive scaling potential—ocean bicarbonate buffer is hundreds of times larger than atmospheric carbon.

3. **Surficial Mineralization:** Exposing reactive rocks to air and moisture to form carbonates. Direct measurement of carbon in rock provides verification.

**Near-Term Limited-Scale Options:**

1. **Biomass-based removal (BECCS):** Using waste and residues (sewage sludge, agricultural/forestry residues). Limited to **~2 gigatons globally** before competing with food production.

2. **Enhanced rock weathering:** Spreading basalt/olivine on farmland. Benefits soil, increases yields, but limited to **~1 billion tons/year** (only so much farmland near rock quarries).

**Investment Thesis:** The billion-dollar Stripe-led coalition is betting that **this decade is for learning what works and driving down costs** through deployment, not for achieving massive scale. The impact is in innovation, not tons removed.

### For Near-Term Climate Expectations

**2026 Forecast:** Hausfather and three other groups (NASA's Gavin Schmidt, UK Met Office, Berkeley Earth) all predict 2026 will be **"somewhere between the second and fourth warmest year"** on par with 2025, due to La Niña conditions.

**2027 Outlook:** With El Niño likely developing in late 2026, **2027 has "a pretty good chance of setting a new record"** as warmest year. Even if not, "it's not going to be long before we do"—the underlying trend continues upward.

**The Certainty:** Regardless of year-to-year variability, **"global warming is marching continuously upward."**

### For Scientific Priorities

**Where to Invest in Reducing Uncertainty:**

**Top Priority:** Better measurements and models of **aerosols and clouds**—the two biggest contributors to the wide climate sensitivity range. Improved satellite instruments for measuring aerosol cooling effects would be "quite helpful."

**Secondary Priority:** Monitoring permafrost carbon release, though current satellite instruments detecting regional methane patterns show **"we don't see a huge flux yet from permafrost, thankfully."** Most methane is coming from tropical wetlands and fossil fuel systems.

**The Nuance:** Hausfather emphasizes that **"narrowing down the error bars is important, but I don't think they'd fundamentally change what we know and what we need to do."** The impetus to reduce emissions doesn't depend on climate sensitivity being 5°C—it's bad enough at current best estimates.
