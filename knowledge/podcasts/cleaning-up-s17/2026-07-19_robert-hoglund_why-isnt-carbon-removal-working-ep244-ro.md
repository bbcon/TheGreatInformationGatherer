# Why Isn't Carbon Removal Working? | Robert Hoglund on Cleaning Up Podcast

## 1. Episode Overview

**Guest Background:** Robert Hoglund runs multiple carbon removal initiatives including Marginal Carbon, manages the Climate Transformation Fund at Milkywire (a $17M philanthropic fund), and co-founded CDR.FYI, a data platform tracking the carbon removal market. He serves on the Science Based Targets Initiative (SBTI) expert advisory group.

**Main Topics:** The state of carbon dioxide removal (CDR) after five years of market development, why corporate climate action has stalled, and what needs to change for CDR to fulfill its promised role in climate mitigation.

**Key Thesis:** The CDR sector needs to shift **from "speed and scale" to "prove and learn."** The industry was built on assumptions that haven't materialized—particularly that corporate demand would drive exponential growth. Instead, the "net zero decade" (roughly 2015-2024) has ended, and the sector must recalibrate around proving technologies at relevant scales while being honest about what's actually achievable.

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### The Corporate Demand Problem

**The Question:** Why aren't more companies funding carbon removal despite widespread net zero commitments?

**Hoglund's Perspective:** The biggest problem isn't finding great projects to fund—**it's motivating companies to act in the first place.** During the "net zero decade," there was a bandwagon effect where companies wanted to appear best-in-class. That momentum has evaporated. Companies now only want to do things they can account for in their emissions inventories, and under SBTI rules, **carbon removal can't be counted toward reduction targets.**

**Key Insight:** The companies actually buying carbon removal share a specific profile: **relatively low emissions and high profit per ton.** These are tech companies, finance firms, and media companies—not steel, cement, or chemicals manufacturers. They can afford leadership positions without significant cost impact.

**Supporting Evidence:** The Climate Transformation Fund has 25 corporate contributors including Klarna, Spotify, and various Nordic companies. Frontier (the larger competing fund) shows similar patterns—backed by Stripe, Alphabet, Shopify, Meta, and McKinsey Sustainability, not heavy industry.

### The Measurement vs. Impact Paradox

**The Challenge:** The interventions with potentially the highest climate impact are the hardest to measure precisely.

**Hoglund's Examples:** 
- **Policy work:** The fund supported Human Rights Watch in postponing a coal power plant license in Bosnia—potentially avoiding millions of tons of emissions. But quantifying exactly how much their contribution mattered is nearly impossible.
- **Systemic interventions:** Working with African utilities to onboard renewable energy could unlock massive change, but attribution is murky and timelines are long.

**The Corporate Tension:** Companies increasingly demand measurable, accountable interventions they can report. This creates a **mismatch between what's measurable and what's impactful.** As Hoglund notes: "You have to basically buy into the theory of change and think that what we're doing here and how we're selecting projects and how we're following up on them is a good strategy even if we can't account for all of it."

**Host Liebreich's Observation:** This parallels broader challenges in climate action—companies are "ferociously" working on their own supply chains because it makes financial sense and is accountable, but struggle with beyond-value-chain interventions.

### The Scale Reality Check

**The Numbers Gap:** 
- **IPCC models call for:** 6-10 gigatons of CDR annually by mid-century
- **Currently removing:** ~1 million tons per year with durable solutions
- **Contracted (not yet delivered):** 44 million tons
- **The gap:** Three to four orders of magnitude

**Hoglund's Reframing:** While the absolute numbers are tiny, **the growth rate has actually been exponential**—the sector hasn't underperformed its trajectory. The problem is that growth is heavily dependent on a few actors (particularly Microsoft), and there's no evidence it will continue without broader policy support.

**The Conditional Nature:** Hoglund argues that **CDR scaling is conditional on climate policy and emission reductions.** Without comprehensive carbon pricing (like the EU ETS) globally enforced, CDR won't reach the gigatons discussed in models. The sector's realistic role now is to **prove out solutions at relevant scales (millions of tons) so the toolkit is ready when policy eventually catches up.**

### What Actually Works in CDR

**Biochar (The Current Leader):**
- **Process:** Heat crop waste to 500-600°C, creating stable carbon that's mixed with soil/fertilizer
- **Cost:** ~$150/ton (literature) vs ~$400/ton (reality including transport/storage)
- **Permanence:** Hundreds to thousands of years based on incubation studies and ancient biochar evidence
- **Scale potential:** 1-2 gigatons using existing waste biomass
- **Status:** "Bread and butter of removing carbon today"

**Enhanced Rock Weathering:**
- **Process:** Grind limestone, basalt, or olivine and spread on fields; reacts with rainwater, dissolved CO2 flows to oceans
- **Potential:** Could become cheap
- **Status:** Still ~1 year from clear answers on effectiveness, best rocks, and optimal locations
- **Challenge:** High quantification costs currently

**Direct Air Capture (DAC):**
- **Current cost:** Very expensive
- **Constraints:** High capex (bigger issue than energy), cost of capital
- **Example:** Stockholm Exergi plant costs €1.3 billion for 800,000 tons/year net capture capacity
- **Hoglund's view:** Some approaches could eventually reach biochar cost levels, but still far away. Over 100 companies trying different methods.
- **Liebreich's skepticism:** Thermodynamic challenges and the sheer volume of air that needs processing (potentially all air on Earth multiple times) make him doubt DAC will ever be meaningful at scale.

**BECCS (Bioenergy with Carbon Capture):**
- **Status:** Largest contracted volume of durable CDR
- **Cost:** Literature says $150/ton; reality is closer to $400/ton including transport and storage
- **Challenge:** Retrofitting facilities means each project is different, limiting learning-by-doing cost reductions
- **Scale:** Stockholm Exergi building capture unit; Drax in UK controversial due to biomass sourcing questions

**Ocean-Based Approaches:**
- **Direct ocean capture:** Similar to DAC but using seawater
- **Alkalinity enhancement:** Being explored; EU Commission holding workshops
- **Status:** Hoglund waiting for clearer science before significant funding
- **Liebreich's concern:** Mixing dynamics—if you decarbonize water and it immediately mixes with surrounding ocean, the CO2 pressure gradient to pull from atmosphere is vastly reduced

**Burial/Briquetting (e.g., Graphyte):**
- **Process:** Create biomass briquettes, seal in plastic, store in disused quarries/mines
- **Advantage:** Cheap—minimal processing required
- **Challenge:** Can you guarantee it stays buried? Lower permanence certainty than other methods
- **Hoglund's comparison:** Like a well-protected forest—low but not zero reversal risk

**Algae/Ocean Fertilization:**
- **Status:** Cyclical hype—currently in a new cycle with new companies
- **History:** Hot 1-2 decades ago, then debunked
- **Challenges:** Uncertainty about whether algae actually sink to ocean floor; potential ecosystem disruption; difficult to measure net carbon removal
- **Example:** Running Tide (kelp on buoys) went out of business, shifted to sinking wood chips, created local pollution issues

### The MRV (Measurement, Reporting, Verification) Challenge

**The Permanence Problem:** How do you verify carbon stays removed for hundreds of years when we've only been doing modern CDR for a decade or two?

**Solutions with Clear MRV:**
- BECCS and DAC: Can measure exactly what's pumped underground with small error rates
- These have "easy" measurement but face other challenges (cost, biomass supply)

**Solutions with Uncertain MRV:**
- **Biochar:** Incubation studies and ancient samples suggest hundreds/thousands of years, but can't rule out mechanical breakdown or biological degradation over very long timeframes
- **Enhanced weathering:** CO2 ends up in ocean decades later—what happens then?
- **Liebreich's evolutionary concern:** Just as fungi/bacteria evolved to digest lignin (ending the Carboniferous period), nature might evolve to break down biochar

**Hoglund's Pragmatism:** "If you want to be absolutely certain that the carbon is removed for thousands of years, then you put yourself a high bar and then there's only few solutions that could meet it." The sector must accept different levels of certainty for different solutions.

## 3. Frameworks & Mental Models

### The "Prove and Learn" Framework

**Old Narrative:**
1. Climate change is catastrophic and urgent
2. Hard-to-abate sectors need CDR
3. Therefore CDR will happen at scale
4. Scale will drive costs down through learning curves
5. Start scaling NOW to hit 6-10 gigatons by mid-century

**New Narrative (Hoglund's proposal):**
1. **CDR is conditional on climate policy** that isn't materializing at necessary speed
2. The sector's realistic role is to **prove out solutions at relevant scales** (millions of tons, not gigatons)
3. Create a **toolkit of validated approaches** ready for when policy eventually drives demand
4. Focus on **learning which solutions are cheapest, most sustainable, with best co-benefits**
5. Accept this is a longer-term project than the "speed and scale" narrative suggested

**How to Apply:** Funders and companies should shift from trying to immediately scale to gigatons toward supporting diverse experimentation, rigorous measurement, and honest reporting about what works and what doesn't.

### Conditional vs. Controllable Emissions

**The Framework:** Hoglund distinguishes between emissions companies can actually control versus those conditional on external factors.

**Controllable:** 
- Direct emissions (Scope 1)
- Purchased electricity/heat (Scope 2)  
- Some supply chain elements where company has agency and financial capacity

**Conditional:**
- Supply chain emissions where company lacks agency (can't dictate supplier choices)
- Emissions where solutions exist but aren't financially viable without policy support
- Long-term systemic changes requiring policy shifts

**The Net Zero Problem:** When companies set net zero targets covering all scopes, they're **making commitments conditional on massive external changes.** This varies by company—vertically integrated firms with high margins have more control; others have targets that are essentially unreachable without policy change.

**Application:** Companies should be explicit about what's conditional, focus accountability on controllable emissions, and use carbon removal/beyond-value-chain interventions specifically for the conditional portions. This creates honesty about what's actually achievable and what requires systemic change.

### The Accounting vs. Interventions Split

**The Emerging Model:** Hoglund sees a trend toward **separating physical emissions accounting from climate interventions.**

**Clean Accounting:** Report actual physical emissions without creative accounting (like renewable energy certificates for electricity used at night from solar bought during the day).

**Separate Interventions:** Report beyond-value-chain actions separately—carbon removal, policy work, technology funding, etc.

**Why This Matters:** It prevents greenwashing while still encouraging companies to fund important climate work. You can't claim renewable energy you didn't actually use, but you can separately report funding that helped build renewable capacity.

**Application:** Companies should maintain rigorous, honest emissions inventories while separately tracking and reporting climate interventions. This allows ambitious action without compromising accounting integrity.

## 4. Contrarian Takes

### Carbon Removal Can't Save Us (From Ourselves)

**The Contrarian View:** Despite running a carbon removal fund, Hoglund argues the sector **won't reach climate-relevant scale without comprehensive policy.** This challenges the dominant narrative from CDR advocates like Julio Friedman who believe market forces and corporate action will drive gigatons of removal.

**Reasoning:** Five years of experience shows that **corporate demand is fragile and conditional.** Without carbon pricing or regulatory requirements, only a narrow slice of companies (low emissions, high profit per ton) will voluntarily pay for removal. The "net zero decade" bandwagon has ended, and companies are reverting to doing only what's required or directly accountable.

**Implication:** The CDR sector should stop overselling its near-term potential and instead focus on proving technologies so they're ready when policy eventually creates real demand.

### Measurement Obsession Blocks Impact

**The Contrarian View:** The things with **highest expected climate impact are often hardest to measure,** and corporate obsession with quantification is blocking funding for crucial interventions.

**Examples:** 
- Policy advocacy that could prevent coal plants (millions of tons avoided, impossible to precisely attribute)
- Systemic work removing barriers to renewable energy deployment
- Long-term capacity building in developing countries

**Reasoning:** Companies want numbers to put in reports. But the most leveraged interventions—removing roadblocks, changing policy, building coalitions—inherently resist precise quantification. **Demanding measurability creates a bias toward less impactful but more accountable interventions.**

**Implication:** Funders need to "buy into the theory of change" and accept uncertainty about exact attribution if they want to support the most impactful work.

### Direct Air Capture Skepticism (Liebreich's Position, Partially Shared)

**The Contrarian View:** Despite massive investment and hype, **DAC may never be climate-relevant** due to fundamental physical constraints.

**Liebreich's Reasoning:** 
- Thermodynamic challenges of extracting very diffuse CO2
- Volume problem: To remove meaningful amounts of excess atmospheric CO2, you'd need to pass all Earth's air through machines multiple times
- Even at 10% capture efficiency per pass, removing 50% excess CO2 requires processing enormous air volumes
- Capex and energy requirements make this implausible at scale

**Hoglund's More Optimistic Take:** Some passive approaches might work; over 100 companies trying different methods; a few could reach biochar cost levels eventually.

**Implication:** The sector should maintain diverse approaches but be honest about DAC's challenges rather than treating it as the inevitable solution for gigatons of removal.

### The Renewable Energy Certificate Problem

**The Contrarian View:** RECs (Renewable Energy Certificates) represent **accounting fraud** that undermines climate credibility.

**The Issue:** Companies buy solar power during the day, use grid power at night (often fossil), but count their nighttime emissions as zero because they bought equivalent renewable certificates.

**Why It Matters:** This isn't just technical accounting—it's **fundamentally dishonest** about physical reality. A company running on coal at night hasn't reduced emissions by buying solar certificates.

**The Trend:** Hoglund sees movement toward "clean accounting" where physical reality is reported separately from interventions. The Greenhouse Gas Protocol revision process is addressing this.

**Implication:** Better to honestly report high emissions and separately report renewable energy funding than to use creative accounting to claim false reductions.

## 5. Practical Implications

### For Carbon Removal Companies/Entrepreneurs

**Realistic Scaling Expectations:** Don't build business models assuming exponential growth to gigatons. The "speed and scale" narrative has failed. Instead, **focus on proving your technology works at relevant scales** (thousands to millions of tons) with rigorous MRV.

**Target the Right Customers:** The realistic market is **low-emission, high-margin companies** in tech, finance, and media—not heavy industry. Design offerings for customers who can afford leadership positions without existential cost impact.

**Embrace Diverse Approaches:** With over 100 DAC companies alone, **differentiation matters.** Hoglund's fund supported multiple methods across biochar, enhanced weathering, ocean approaches, etc. Don't assume one technology will dominate.

**MRV is Make-or-Break:** Companies that can provide **clear, honest measurement** will win corporate customers increasingly focused on accountability. But also recognize that some uncertainty is inherent—communicate it clearly rather than overpromising precision.

### For Corporate Climate Leaders

**Separate Controllable from Conditional:** Be **explicit about which emissions you can actually control** versus which depend on policy, technology availability, or supplier choices. Set aggressive targets for controllable emissions; be honest about conditional ones.

**Beyond-Value-Chain Interventions:** If you're a low-emission, high-margin company, **consider funding climate work beyond your inventory**—carbon removal, policy advocacy, technology development. But report it separately from emissions accounting.

**Demand Clean Accounting:** Push for **honest emissions reporting** rather than creative use of RECs or offsets. Physical reality in the inventory, interventions reported separately.

**Theory of Change Over Precision:** For highest-impact interventions (policy work, systemic change), **accept that precise attribution is impossible.** Evaluate based on theory of change and execution quality rather than demanding exact ton counts.

**Long-Term Commitment:** The "net zero decade" bandwagon has ended, but **the climate problem hasn't.** Companies that maintain commitment despite reduced peer pressure will be the ones that actually drive change.

### For Policymakers

**Policy is the Bottleneck:** Hoglund's key finding is that **CDR scaling is conditional on climate policy.** Without comprehensive carbon pricing (like EU ETS) globally enforced, voluntary corporate action won't reach necessary scales.

**Support Proving, Not Just Scaling:** Rather than immediately trying to drive gigatons, **fund diverse experimentation** at relevant scales (millions of tons) to build the toolkit for when policy creates real demand.

**Address the Hard-to-Abate:** Focus policy on **removing barriers to already-cheap solutions** rather than obsessing over the last 10-20% of emissions. As Liebreich notes, there's enormous potential in solutions that are already economically viable but face non-economic roadblocks.

**Honest Accounting Standards:** Support the **Greenhouse Gas Protocol revision** to eliminate creative accounting with RECs and other mechanisms. Clean accounting creates pressure for real action.

### For Investors

**Realistic Timelines:** The path to gigatons is **decades, not years.** Investment theses based on near-term exponential growth are likely to disappoint. Look for companies focused on proving technologies rather than immediate scaling.

**Diverse Portfolio:** With fundamental uncertainty about which approaches will ultimately work, **diversification across methods** (biochar, enhanced weathering, DAC, ocean, etc.) reduces risk.

**Policy Dependency:** Recognize that **CDR is ultimately a policy play.** Returns depend on governments eventually creating demand through carbon pricing or mandates. Factor this political risk into valuations.

**The Microsoft Problem:** Current growth is heavily dependent on a few large buyers (particularly Microsoft). **Concentration risk is high.** Look for companies building diverse customer bases or positioned for eventual policy-driven demand.

### For Climate Advocates

**Honesty Over Hype:** The CDR sector has **oversold its near-term potential.** Advocates should focus on what's actually achievable (proving technologies, building capacity) rather than promising gigatons that aren't materializing.

**Measurement Paradox:** Recognize that **demanding precise quantification can block the most impactful interventions.** Policy advocacy, systemic change, and barrier removal are crucial but resist exact measurement.

**Beyond Inventory:** The SBTI rule that **carbon removal can't count toward reduction targets** may be counterproductive. It prevents companies from funding important work. Consider frameworks that separate controllable reductions from beyond-value-chain interventions.

**The Pragmatic Decade:** Liebreich's framing of entering a "pragmatic decade" after the "net zero decade" is apt. Focus on **differentiating between cheap/controllable solutions and expensive/conditional ones,** prioritizing the former while honestly addressing the latter.
