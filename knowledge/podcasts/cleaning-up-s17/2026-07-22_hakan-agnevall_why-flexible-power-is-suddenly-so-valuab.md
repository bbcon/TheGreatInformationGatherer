# Why Flexible Power Is Suddenly So Valuable | Ep259: Hakan Agnevall

## 1. Episode Overview

**Guest Background:** Håkan Agnevall is CEO of Wärtsilä, a 190-year-old Finnish company that manufactures large piston engines for both marine vessels and power generation. With a 60/40 split between marine and energy businesses, Wärtsilä specializes in **flexible, modular power generation** in the 50-500 MW range and holds market leadership in four-stroke engines.

**Main Topics:**
- The explosive growth of AI data centers and their energy demands
- How flexible gas engines enable renewable energy integration
- The delayed IMO shipping decarbonization agreement
- The evolution of data center power strategies from grid-connected to off-grid solutions

**Key Thesis:** The AI data center boom is creating unprecedented demand for flexible power generation that can operate off-grid initially, then transition to grid-balancing roles while eventually integrating with renewables—validating Wärtsilä's core business model of flexible thermal generation supporting renewable energy systems.

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### The Data Center Power Revolution

**The Transformation:** Just 2-3 years ago, Wärtsilä wasn't even in the data center market. Average data center size globally was **11 MW**, and developers would simply sign power purchase agreements (PPAs) with utilities and buy backup generators from Caterpillar or Cummins.

**What Changed with AI:** Modern AI data centers now require **200-1,000 MW minimum**, with some planned facilities reaching 2-5 GW. When developers approach utilities requesting 600 MW, the typical response is: "Come back in 8 years" due to permitting, new power lines, and new power plant requirements.

**The Off-Grid Solution:** This timeline mismatch has created the **off-grid data center market**—facilities that generate their own power rather than drawing from the grid. This falls directly into Wärtsilä's sweet spot of modular, flexible generation using 20-25 MW engine units.

**Market Scale:** Agnevall reports Wärtsilä has now signed orders for **2.4 GW of data center capacity**, with predictions for US data center power growth ranging wildly from 40 GW to 220 GW by 2030. Texas alone has **400 GW worth of connection requests**.

### The Three-Stage Data Center Power Evolution

Agnevall describes how customers are thinking about data center power in **three sequential stages**:

**Stage 1 - Off-Grid Thermal:** Build dedicated gas engine power plants to serve the data center immediately, bypassing the 8-year grid connection wait. This is happening now and driving Wärtsilä's 80% capacity expansion from 2025-2028.

**Stage 2 - Grid Export:** Once the utility eventually builds the connection (8+ years later), add more engine modules and become a **grid resource**, exporting power to the broader system while continuing to serve the data center. The flexible engines can now provide balancing services.

**Stage 3 - Renewable Integration:** Layer in wind and solar (because they're the most affordable power sources) while using the thermal generation and battery storage for **balancing and stability**. The engines run less frequently but remain essential for grid reliability.

**Key Insight:** Liebreich notes this validates his "pragmatic climate reset" thesis—that regardless of the path taken, the destination is the same: **maximum renewables with flexible thermal backup**, because that's the economically optimal solution.

### Political and Social Pressures

**Consumer Cost Concerns:** There's growing political pressure around data centers driving up electricity prices for consumers. Several hyperscalers made pledges to President Trump that their developments would **not increase costs to consumers**, pushing them toward off-grid solutions.

**Power Quality Issues:** Beyond cost, data centers can create technical problems like harmonics that have caused blackouts (Spain, Portugal, Chile mentioned as examples).

**The Hyperscaler Preference:** Agnevall notes that hyperscalers clearly prefer green energy and renewables, but "with the pace they are having, that's not the focus or the core focus." Speed of deployment currently trumps sustainability goals, though economics will eventually drive renewable integration.

### Shipping Decarbonization Delay

**The MEPC 83 Decision:** The International Maritime Organization's Marine Environmental Protection Committee was set to vote on a **global carbon pricing mechanism** for shipping—a revolutionary step for an entire industry. However, strong US advocacy led to a **one-year postponement**.

**Implications:** The delay risks creating a **"patchwork of different regulations"**—the EU will keep its Emissions Trading Scheme (ETS) for marine, China may develop its own framework, creating complexity for a fundamentally global industry.

**The Fundamental Drivers Remain:** Regardless of regulation, Agnevall argues shipbuilders must consider that vessels have **30-year lifetimes**. It's reasonable to assume regulations will evolve and fuel will become more expensive (either fossil fuels through levies or green fuels through production costs). This makes **fuel flexibility and fuel efficiency** the critical strategic imperatives.

**Strategic Risk of Inaction:** "Not doing anything is also a strategic choice, which increases your strategic risk over time. If you only bet on one fuel and that fuel becomes financially not viable anymore, then you have a big problem."

## 3. Frameworks & Mental Models

### The Balancing Power Framework

**Core Concept:** As renewables grow (now 50% of global electricity generation capacity), their intermittency creates system instability. **Flexible thermal generation** can "balance" renewables by ramping up and down quickly to maintain grid stability.

**The Math:** Anders Lindberg's previous episode showed you can replace **25% inflexible coal** with **4% flexible gas engines** while adding renewables, achieving better economics and lower emissions.

**Why Modularity Matters:** Wärtsilä's engines come in 20-25 MW modules. Rather than ramping one giant turbine up and down, you can **switch individual engines on and off**, following wind and solar generation patterns more precisely and efficiently.

**Application:** This framework explains why flexible gas isn't competing with renewables—it's **enabling** more renewable deployment by solving the intermittency problem.

### The Supply Chain Bottleneck Model

**Lead Time Reality:** Wärtsilä has **3-year delivery times** for engines (compared to 5-7 years for large gas turbines). This creates a fundamental constraint on how fast data center capacity can actually grow.

**Capacity Expansion:** Wärtsilä is expanding capacity by **80% from 2025 to 2028**, but Agnevall emphasizes "this is heavy engineering"—huge castings and complex supply chains that can't be doubled overnight.

**The Skeptical View:** Liebreich challenges the 220 GW prediction, noting the US currently adds only 2.5 GW of data centers annually. Even with aggressive expansion, getting to 8-10 GW annually by 2030 would be pushing limits, suggesting **40 GW total by 2030 is more realistic** than the upper-range predictions.

**Other Bottlenecks:** Beyond engines, data centers need transformers, cooling systems, chillers, electricians, plumbers—creating bottlenecks "almost across the whole data center industry."

### The Fuel Flexibility Strategy

**The Principle:** In an uncertain regulatory and fuel price environment, the winning strategy is to **maintain optionality** across multiple fuel types rather than betting on a single solution.

**Wärtsilä's Approach:** Their four-stroke engines can run on various fuels (natural gas, LNG, biofuels, eventually hydrogen/ammonia blends), providing customers with **strategic flexibility** as regulations and economics evolve.

**Application for Shipowners:** With 30-year vessel lifetimes and uncertain future regulations, fuel-flexible engines reduce risk. If one fuel becomes economically unviable or regulated out of existence, the vessel can switch rather than becoming a stranded asset.

## 4. Contrarian Takes

### Data Centers Will Eventually Use Renewables (Not Stay on Gas)

**The Conventional Worry:** Many fear the data center boom will lock in decades of fossil fuel generation, undermining climate goals.

**Agnevall's Counterargument:** The three-stage evolution means data centers will **naturally migrate to renewables** because "renewables is the most affordable source of energy." The initial gas generation is a **bridge solution** driven by speed requirements, not a permanent state.

**The Economics Drive It:** Once grid connections arrive and renewable capacity can be added, hyperscalers will find it cheaper to run engines less and use wind/solar more, with engines providing balancing. "It's economically viable because we need development that is sustainable but it needs to be financially viable as well."

### The US Shipping Position May Not Matter Long-Term

**The Concern:** US opposition to IMO carbon pricing could derail global shipping decarbonization.

**The Counterpoint:** Even without global regulation, **market forces and regional regulations** (EU ETS, potential Chinese framework) will drive change. Shipbuilders can't ignore 30-year asset lifetimes and the likelihood of evolving regulations.

**The Practical Reality:** Fuel flexibility and efficiency remain the right strategy regardless of whether global carbon pricing materializes, because fuel costs will rise through some mechanism (levies on fossil fuels or higher costs for green fuels).

### Operational Optimization Beats Technology Innovation

**The Surprising Claim:** When asked about efficiency improvements, Agnevall says the biggest potential is **10-20% from operational changes** with existing equipment, not from new engine technology.

**The Explanation:** "There is still a lot of behavioral aspects on how you operate equipment and there is huge potential in that." Human decision-making and operational practices create inefficiencies that AI and data analytics can address.

**Technology Improvements:** Engine efficiency itself (currently 48-50%) can only improve by "a couple of percentage points more" due to thermodynamic limits (Carnot cycle). But **system-level innovations** (like hybrid engine-battery systems for LNG carriers) can still achieve 10% savings.

### China Isn't the Competitive Threat (In This Market)

**Expected Concern:** Chinese competition dominates many manufacturing sectors, particularly in energy equipment.

**Agnevall's View:** In marine, "more than half of the world's shipbuilding capacity is in China. We are in China" both selling and manufacturing. Rather than seeing China as a threat, Wärtsilä operates **within the Chinese ecosystem**.

**The Real Competition:** For data centers, the competitive pressure comes from **gas turbine manufacturers** (Siemens, GE, Mitsubishi Heavy Industries) and potentially from **battery cell manufacturers integrating forward** into energy storage systems, not from Chinese engine manufacturers.

## 5. Practical Implications

### For Data Center Developers

**Immediate Action:** Off-grid power generation using flexible gas engines is the **fastest path to deployment**, avoiding 8-year grid connection timelines.

**Plan for Evolution:** Design systems with the three-stage evolution in mind—ensure capacity for adding modules later and integrating renewables. Don't treat the initial gas generation as the permanent solution.

**Consider Location:** The conversation implies data center location decisions should factor in not just fiber connectivity and land costs, but **power availability and regulatory environment** (particularly around consumer cost concerns).

### For Power System Planners

**Flexible Capacity Is Essential:** As renewables grow, the grid needs more, not less, flexible thermal generation for balancing. The ratio might be small (4% flexible vs. 25% inflexible in the earlier example), but it's **critical for stability**.

**Modularity Matters:** Large combined-cycle gas turbines are less useful for balancing than smaller, modular reciprocating engines that can be switched on and off individually.

**Plan for Hybrid Systems:** The future grid will be **renewables + batteries + flexible thermal**, not renewables alone or thermal alone. System planning must integrate all three.

### For Investors

**Supply Chain Constraints Are Real:** 3-year lead times and 80% capacity expansions that take until 2028 mean supply will lag demand significantly. Companies with existing capacity and established supply chains have **pricing power**.

**Service Revenue Dominates:** More than 50% of Wärtsilä's business is service, not equipment sales. The **installed base creates recurring revenue** through maintenance, optimization, and digital services.

**Battery Storage Faces Headwinds:** Wärtsilä's energy storage business is "struggling" due to intense competition, EV market slowdown pushing battery manufacturers into stationary storage, and US tariff uncertainty. This segment is **much more challenged** than the engine business.

**Marine Uncertainty:** The IMO delay creates regulatory uncertainty, but the fundamental drivers (30-year asset lives, likely future regulations, fuel cost trends) still favor fuel-flexible, efficient engines.

### For Climate Policy

**Don't Demonize Gas Engines:** Small amounts of flexible gas generation **enable much larger amounts of renewables** by solving intermittency. The climate math favors this combination over inflexible baseload fossil fuels.

**Speed vs. Perfection:** The data center boom shows that when deployment speed is critical, **perfect climate solutions give way to pragmatic ones**. The key is ensuring the pragmatic path still leads to the right destination (renewable-heavy systems).

**Global Coordination Matters:** The IMO delay shows how **lack of global coordination** creates patchwork regulations that complicate decarbonization for global industries like shipping.

### Actionable Takeaways

1. **For energy buyers:** Consider off-grid flexible generation as a **speed-to-market strategy**, not just a backup option, particularly for large loads.

2. **For equipment buyers:** Prioritize **fuel flexibility and efficiency** over betting on a single fuel type, given regulatory and price uncertainty.

3. **For grid operators:** Plan for **4-10% flexible capacity** to balance growing renewable penetration, using modular solutions that can ramp quickly.

4. **For technology developers:** Focus AI applications on **operational optimization** (10-20% gains) rather than just incremental equipment improvements (2-3% gains).

5. **For manufacturers:** The data center boom creates a **3-5 year window** of strong demand, but supply chain expansion takes time—start now if you want to participate.
