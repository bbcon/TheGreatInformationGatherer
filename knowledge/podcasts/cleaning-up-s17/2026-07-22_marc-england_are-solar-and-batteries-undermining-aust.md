# Are Solar And Batteries Undermining Australia's Grid? Deep Dive with Marc England

## 1. Episode Overview

**Guest:** Marc England, CEO of Ausgrid (distribution network service provider for Sydney and surrounding areas in New South Wales, Australia)

**Background:** England brings a varied background across UK, New Zealand, and Australian energy markets, primarily in the competitive generator-retailer sector. This is his first role in a regulated monopoly, where he's been CEO for just over three years.

**Main Topics:**
- The challenges facing distribution networks during Australia's energy transition
- Grid resilience and infrastructure (including Sydney's underground tunnel system)
- The equity implications of distributed energy resources
- Regulatory barriers to network operators providing storage and other services
- The future of electricity demand in Australia

**Key Thesis:** Distribution networks are the "missing middle" in the energy transition debate. While attention focuses on large-scale generation upstream and behind-the-meter solutions downstream, **networks can provide critical buffering services at lower cost** than either alternative—but regulatory frameworks prevent them from doing so.

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### The Equity Challenge of Distributed Energy

**The Question:** Who benefits from the energy transition when rooftop solar and batteries require significant capital investment?

**England's Perspective:** This is one of his biggest disappointments in the energy transition narrative. The prevailing image is of a detached house with solar on the roof, a garage, and a driveway—but **30% of Ausgrid's customers don't have a garage or driveway, and 40% are renters**. These customers cannot participate in behind-the-meter solutions.

**Key Insight:** Networks work "horizontally across the system" and can **take surplus solar, store it in the system, and distribute electrons not only over distance but also over time**, ensuring everyone benefits—not just those who can afford behind-the-meter assets.

**Supporting Evidence:** England points to warehouse roofs as a missed opportunity. A warehouse roof might accommodate 2-3 megawatts of solar, but landlord-tenant misalignment means only 50kW gets installed. If networks could incentivize overbuilding and store that power locally, it could benefit entire communities including social housing residents.

### The Transformation from One-Way to Two-Way Networks

**The Framing:** Historically, distribution networks were one-way systems—taking electricity from transmission and distributing it to end users. Now they're becoming bidirectional.

**England's Analysis:** "The last mile has become the first mile." On sunny days in Australia, generation flows from homes and businesses back upstream. The challenge isn't that electricity can't flow both ways—**networks are resilient to that**—but rather that capacity in particular parts of the network for receiving reverse power flow may be insufficient.

**The Mechanism:** The system operates through "a cascade of transformers a bit like a waterfall"—except unlike a waterfall, electricity can go the other way. Problems arise when the capacity for receiving that reverse power is less than what's flowing from homes and businesses.

**Storage as Solution:** This is where storage becomes critical. **If you have storage, you can keep power local, reducing the need for network infrastructure.** A fascinating statistic: England notes that a study found **70% of Sydney's current demand could be met with warehouse roof solar alone**.

### The Regulatory Sandbox and Community Batteries

**The Challenge:** Current regulations prevent distribution networks from owning and operating batteries in ways that would benefit customers most.

**What Ausgrid Is Doing:** They're currently installing community batteries providing three value streams:
1. Tolling batteries to generator-retailers for wholesale market trading (because Ausgrid isn't allowed to do this directly)
2. Network support services
3. Storage-as-a-service for physically connected customers (saving ~$200 annually on bills)

**The Irony:** Ausgrid is doing this as an **unregulated investment earning ~15% returns when it should be regulated earning ~6.5%**. England argues: "We're saying we want to do more in a regulated sense, in a lower cost of capital sense, because customers will be better off, but it's amazing how hard it is to get heard on that."

**The Sandbox Experiment:** The Australian Energy Regulator approved Ausgrid's proposal to test a new model in two suburbs:
- Auction for lowest-price solar from warehouse roofs
- Deploy batteries throughout the network (both large community batteries and small batteries down feeders)
- Batteries charge/discharge in wholesale market with arbitrage value going to ALL customers in the area
- Testing three hypotheses: lower delivered electricity cost, faster decarbonization, and greater equity

### The Coming Demand Growth

**Historical Context:** For 20 years, the industry has seen "hockey stick forecasts for demand growth that never materialized because efficiency outweighed population growth."

**Why This Time Is Different:** England is confident demand will grow due to two macro trends:

1. **Electric Vehicle Adoption:** A slow trickle over time from a demand perspective

2. **Data Centers:** This is the game-changer. Ausgrid is a **5-12 gigawatt maximum demand network**, and they have "a confident level of at least **2 gigawatts of data center demand coming in over the next few years**"—that's material.

**The Positive Side:** England emphasizes that **every gigawatt of data center load connected results in a $50 reduction for every home** because data centers absorb network costs without requiring proportional new infrastructure (when directed to areas with spare capacity like the Hunter region).

**Key Insight:** "Volume is your friend" when half your costs are network costs. More demand spreads fixed costs across more kilowatt-hours, reducing per-unit costs.

### Resilience: Climate and Beyond

**Climate Adaptation:** Ausgrid operates in environments susceptible to east coast lows, floods, and bushfires. They're investing in resilience through:
- Undergrounding portions of network (expensive)
- Community support mechanisms during outages
- Coordination with telecoms, fire services, and emergency services

**The Equity Dimension:** In urban Sydney, average unplanned outage time is "single-digit minutes" per year due to the triplex underground network. In outer areas with overhead lines, it can be "an hour or two hours a year"—creating an equity issue.

**Non-Climate Risks:** Liebreich raised concerns about:
- **Hailstorms:** England noted these are geographically isolated, and the network provides resilience through rerouting
- **Solar Storms:** England acknowledged this as a genuine concern, referencing the 1859 Carrington Event and a January 2025 incident in Victoria that took out 8,000 homes. He admitted Australia lacks a comprehensive national disaster plan for such events.

## 3. Frameworks & Mental Models

### The "Missing Middle" Framework

England positions distribution networks as the **"missing middle"** in energy transition debates:
- **Upstream:** Large-scale generation (wind, solar replacing coal)
- **Downstream:** Behind-the-meter solutions (home solar, batteries, EVs)
- **Middle (overlooked):** Distribution networks that can buffer volatility

**Application:** When evaluating energy transition solutions, consider whether the distribution network could provide services more cost-effectively than either centralized generation or distributed behind-the-meter assets.

### The Three Transitions Framework

England identifies **three simultaneous transitions**, not just one:

1. **Decarbonization upstream:** Replacing coal with wind and solar in wholesale markets
2. **Electrification downstream:** EVs, heat pumps, hot water behind customer meters
3. **Volatility in the middle:** Demand becoming less predictable (can't determine when someone plugs in their EV) and supply becoming less controllable (solar generation varies)

**Application:** Solutions must address all three transitions simultaneously. For example, storage in the distribution network addresses the volatility challenge while enabling both decarbonization and electrification.

### The Totex vs. Capex Incentive Framework

**The Problem:** Distribution networks are currently incentivized to invest in capital (capex) rather than total cost of ownership (totex), which could include operating costs paid to third parties.

**Example:** A digital twin technology might reduce a $100 million grid upgrade to $80 million, but the network has no incentive to use it because it reduces the regulated asset base.

**The Counterbalance:** England argues several factors prevent pure asset-base maximization:
- Efficiency incentives (penalties for spending over allowance, rewards for coming under)
- 5-year regulatory reviews examining cost-benefit analysis
- Social license and employee conscience (workers live in communities they serve)
- Investor expectations for sustainable growth

**Application:** When evaluating regulated utility behavior, look beyond simple incentive structures to the full regulatory framework and social context.

## 4. Contrarian Takes

### Networks Should Own and Operate Batteries (At Regulated Returns)

**The Consensus View:** Competitive markets should provide storage; regulated monopolies should stick to wires.

**England's Challenge:** Networks can provide storage at lower cost of capital (6.5% regulated vs. 15% unregulated), and the horizontal nature of networks ensures benefits are distributed equitably across all customers, not just those who can afford behind-the-meter solutions.

**Reasoning:** The current framework forces Ausgrid to provide community batteries as an unregulated service earning higher returns, making electricity more expensive for customers than if the same service were provided at regulated returns.

### Subtransmission Renewable Energy Zones Are the Answer

**The Consensus View:** Build large wind farms far from population centers (where coal used to be) and connect via high-voltage transmission.

**England's Challenge:** Connecting renewables at the subtransmission level (132kV) is **about one-third the cost per gigawatt** compared to transmission-level connections.

**Reasoning:**
- Can augment existing lines (brownfield) rather than building new transmission corridors
- Brings generation closer to demand centers
- Reduces community opposition by avoiding new land corridors
- Distribution networks already have the infrastructure

### The Death Spiral Narrative Is Overblown

**The Consensus View:** Rooftop solar and batteries will lead to grid defection and a utility death spiral.

**England's Challenge:** While energy (kWh) has been "hollowed out," **capacity (kW) has barely changed**—maximum demand has fallen only 6% over 20 years, minimum demand only 2%.

**Reasoning:**
- Solar only works when it's sunny; winter heating demand occurs when there's no sun
- Batteries help but don't eliminate the need for network capacity
- Coming demand growth from EVs and data centers will more than offset any defection
- Networks remain essential for resilience and equity

## 5. Practical Implications

### For Energy Policy

1. **Regulatory Reform Needed:** Allow distribution networks to own and operate storage at regulated returns. The current framework perversely makes electricity more expensive by forcing networks into unregulated models with higher costs of capital.

2. **Prioritize Equity:** Behind-the-meter solutions inherently favor homeowners with capital. Policy should enable network-level solutions that benefit renters, apartment dwellers, and low-income households.

3. **Subtransmission Strategy:** Rather than focusing exclusively on large transmission projects, develop subtransmission renewable energy zones that can connect generation at one-third the cost.

4. **Capacity Mechanisms:** Australia's energy-only market will need capacity mechanisms for gas backup to ensure reliability during extended periods of low renewable generation.

### For Investors

1. **Data Center Opportunity:** Distribution networks serving areas with spare capacity (like former coal regions) are well-positioned to benefit from data center growth, which improves economics for all customers.

2. **Storage Value Streams:** Community batteries can provide multiple value streams (wholesale arbitrage, network support, customer services), but regulatory clarity is needed to unlock full value.

3. **Volume Growth:** Contrary to death spiral narratives, distribution networks face demand growth from electrification and data centers. Networks that can accommodate this growth efficiently will see strong returns.

4. **Resilience Premium:** Networks investing in resilience (undergrounding, redundancy, community support) may command premium valuations as extreme weather events increase.

### For Understanding the Australian Energy Transition

1. **Solar Penetration Context:** 41% of Australian homes have solar (lower in Sydney due to urban density), with 400,000 batteries representing 10GWh of storage—and growing rapidly with government subsidies.

2. **The Warehouse Roof Opportunity:** Misaligned incentives between landlords and tenants mean warehouse roofs are dramatically underutilized. A 3MW-capable roof might have only 50kW installed.

3. **Network Costs Are Falling:** Ausgrid's network costs represent less than 30% of a typical electricity bill (~$450 of a $2,000 annual bill), and England is committed to keeping these costs from rising despite the transition.

4. **Geographic Diversity Matters:** Ausgrid operates in urban Sydney (underground networks, triplex redundancy), suburban areas, and rural Hunter Valley (overhead lines, agricultural and mining loads)—requiring different approaches to resilience and service.

### Actionable Takeaways

**For Policymakers:**
- Create regulatory sandboxes to test new models before full-scale deployment
- Reform totex vs. capex incentives to encourage innovation
- Develop national resilience plans for low-probability, high-impact events (solar storms, extreme weather)

**For Network Operators:**
- Engage with data center developers early to direct them to areas with spare capacity
- Develop dynamic connection agreements that allow large loads to connect early with curtailment on peak days
- Invest in community engagement to build social license for necessary investments

**For Developers:**
- Look for opportunities in subtransmission renewable energy zones
- Consider warehouse roof solar with network offtake agreements
- Explore partnerships with networks for community battery projects

**For Consumers:**
- Understand that network costs are less than 30% of bills; generation and retail are larger components
- Support policies that enable network-level storage benefiting all customers
- Consider time-of-use tariffs and flexibility to reduce system costs
