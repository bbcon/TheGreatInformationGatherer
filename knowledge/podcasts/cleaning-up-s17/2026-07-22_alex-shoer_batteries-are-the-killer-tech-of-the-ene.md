# Batteries Are The Killer Tech Of The Energy Transition | Ep266: Alex Shoer

## 1. Episode Overview

**Guest Background:** Alex Shoer is managing director of GridVest, a company focused on bringing batteries into US energy markets. He's a serial entrepreneur who spent nearly a decade in China (2011-2020) developing solar businesses, first with a cleantech market entry company, then founding Cedar Clean Energy to finance rooftop solar for Chinese factories through power purchase agreements (PPAs).

**Main Topics:** The conversation explores the evolution from solar to battery storage as the critical technology for energy transition, drawing parallels between China's renewable energy development and the emerging US battery storage market.

**Key Thesis:** **Batteries are becoming more valuable than solar itself** in many markets, representing the "killer app" that solves intermittency, enables energy independence, and unlocks the full potential of renewable energy. Alex argues that the US should focus on areas of competitive advantage (like solid-state batteries and system integration) while partnering with China on mature technologies like lithium iron phosphate (LFP) batteries, rather than trying to compete directly in manufacturing where China has insurmountable scale advantages.

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### The China Solar Journey: Lessons for US Battery Deployment

**The Question:** What did Alex learn from China's solar transformation that applies to batteries today?

**Guest's Perspective:** Alex moved to China in 2011, intending a 6-week trip but staying nearly a decade after recognizing the government's serious commitment to environmental improvement in their 12th Five-Year Plan. He identified that **financing was the missing piece** in China's solar deployment—the manufacturing capability existed, but projects had 5-10 year payback periods that Chinese businesses couldn't stomach.

**Key Insights:**
- China initially deployed solar through a "Golden Sun" tariff that paid per kilowatt installed, leading to inefficient deployment "wherever they could, not where they needed it"
- In 2014, China shifted to **generation-based subsidies** (paying per kilowatt-hour produced), which incentivized quality and proper placement
- The third-party ownership/PPA model was completely foreign to China initially—Alex's company did the first rooftop solar PPA in Beijing in 2014
- **The pivotal moment came in May 2018** when China abruptly ended solar subsidies overnight, causing module prices to drop 30% within months and creating a brief moratorium followed by explosive growth in subsidy-free projects

**Supporting Evidence:** Alex notes that after the 2018 subsidy elimination, projects that previously needed 13% IRRs with subsidies could achieve similar leveraged returns at 8% unleveraged IRRs through creative financing, with less paperwork and risk. This **"normal market" moment** triggered China's distributed generation boom.

**Mechanism Explained:** The subsidy removal paradoxically accelerated deployment because it forced cost reductions, simplified processes, and created a sustainable market structure rather than one dependent on government support.

### The Duck Curve and Solar Cannibalization

**The Question:** Why are batteries becoming more valuable than solar in some markets?

**Guest's Perspective:** Alex describes a **stunning reversal** where a project in Santa Rosa, Northern California showed that "adding batteries is like three times more valuable than adding solar" alone—something unimaginable just two years ago.

**Key Insights:**
- Solar's success created its own problem: **market cannibalization** where all solar generators compete for revenue during the same peak daylight hours
- The "duck curve" (California's load profile showing midday solar surplus and evening demand peak) makes solar-only projects increasingly uneconomic
- **Demand charges** (utility fees based on peak 15-minute power usage) have become one of the biggest components of commercial electricity bills
- Batteries solve multiple problems simultaneously: time-shifting solar generation, reducing demand charges, providing backup power, and enabling smaller grid connections to function like larger ones

**Supporting Evidence:** Alex notes that in most US markets, "if you're going to build solar, you pretty much need to have batteries with it if you want to make the economics make sense."

### The Manufacturing Reality: Where to Compete vs. Collaborate

**The Question:** Should the US try to compete directly with Chinese battery manufacturing?

**Guest's Perspective:** Alex argues strongly for **strategic partnership rather than direct competition** in mature battery technologies, while focusing US efforts on areas of genuine competitive advantage.

**Key Insights:**
- Chinese LFP battery factories are "almost all automated" with AI-driven quality control, operating at a scale that "looks like a newspaper printing press"
- The **Ford-CATL partnership** represents a smart model: Ford provides domestic presence and automotive expertise, CATL provides world-class battery manufacturing know-how
- Alex advocates for regulations focused on **where batteries are made rather than who owns the company**, allowing joint ventures with majority US ownership
- The US has genuine advantages in: solid-state battery technology, system integration, risk-tolerant early-stage funding, and innovation

**Contrarian Take:** "Let's not try and directly compete on that specific thing because you're never going to win, but either partner... we can figure out where our competitive advantages are that we can win. And I think there are plenty that we can win if we focus on them."

**Supporting Evidence:** Alex points to GM's partnership with Redwood Materials and notes that traditional automakers like Ford and GM becoming "energy companies" represents a fascinating sector convergence that plays to American strengths.

### Foreign Entity of Concern (FEOC) Rules: Help or Hindrance?

**The Question:** Are domestic content requirements and FEOC restrictions helping or hurting US energy transition?

**Guest's Perspective:** Alex suggests these rules may be **counterproductive**, similar to how China's solar subsidies initially seemed helpful but their removal actually accelerated deployment.

**Key Insights:**
- Current rules focus on **ownership rather than manufacturing location**, which Alex sees as backwards
- The Investment Tax Credit (ITC) has "almost become a curse" by creating dependency and complexity around FEOC compliance
- Projects are being delayed or cancelled due to equipment sourcing challenges under new restrictions
- There's a parallel to China's 2018 subsidy removal: short-term pain might lead to long-term market normalization

**Contrarian Take:** "I think we can focus on domestic manufacturing and we can focus on domestic jobs and we can focus on the environment as well... we shouldn't just be arbitrarily shooting ourselves in the foot by saying, well, we can't use this because it's owned by this company."

### Data Centers: The New Demand Driver

**The Question:** How are AI data centers changing energy infrastructure needs?

**Guest's Perspective:** Data centers represent a **massive new demand source** that's driving innovation in off-grid and hybrid power solutions, with batteries playing a critical enabling role.

**Key Insights:**
- Data centers face three power options: fully off-grid (gas turbines + batteries), hybrid (small grid connection enhanced with batteries + generation), or waiting years for large utility interconnection
- **GPU power spikes** create extreme variability that utilities can't handle—batteries act as a buffer to "clean the power up"
- A single 200-250 kW fast EV charger uses as much power as an entire hotel, making batteries essential for adding charging without doubling grid capacity
- Off-grid solutions using natural gas are "definitely not cost effective" and face reliability challenges despite being faster to deploy

**Mechanism Explained:** Batteries enable a **smaller grid connection to function like a larger one** by managing peak loads, storing power during low-demand periods, and providing instantaneous response to demand spikes that would otherwise require massive infrastructure upgrades.

**Supporting Evidence:** Alex notes that California's aging grid infrastructure "cannot just come and double your electrical capacity overnight," making batteries the practical solution for rapid capacity expansion.

### Residential vs. Commercial/Industrial: Different Maturity Levels

**The Question:** Why hasn't battery deployment scaled as easily as solar?

**Guest's Perspective:** While **residential battery systems are "figured out"** (Tesla Powerwall being the prime example), commercial, industrial, and utility-scale systems remain complex with no true turnkey solutions.

**Key Insights:**
- Solar is "relatively plug-and-play" compared to batteries, requiring less engineering expertise
- **Microgrids are "inherently complex"** requiring sophisticated coordination between generation, storage, loads, and grid connections
- The challenge isn't the batteries themselves—"the batteries work just as they should"—but the **system architecture** for seamless power provision and grid integration
- Commercial systems must optimize across multiple variables: sell to grid vs. self-consumption, solar charging vs. grid charging, EV charging priorities, demand charge management

**Contrarian Take:** "The ironic thing is... the big really big projects, the utility-scale projects, are technically much easier... That's actually much more easy than if you're doing it at a school or hotel or office building."

**Practical Implication:** The **mid-market commercial/industrial segment** represents both the biggest technical challenge and the biggest opportunity—solving it will unlock "every building, every factory, every school, every neighborhood."

## 3. Frameworks & Mental Models

### The "Refrigerator of Electricity" Framework

Alex references Arne Dam Alters' analogy: **Batteries are to electricity what refrigerators are to food.** Just as refrigeration transformed food economics by enabling storage and smoothing supply chains (eliminating daily shopping trips), batteries transform electricity economics by enabling time-shifting and smoothing generation/demand mismatches.

**Application:** This framework helps communicate battery value to non-technical audiences and explains why batteries represent infrastructure as fundamental as refrigeration was to 20th-century development.

### The "Electron as Universal Currency" Model

Alex proposes viewing electricity as **increasingly fungible with other fundamental resources**:
- Money (cryptocurrency mining)
- Data and intelligence (AI computation)
- Food (vertical farming, lab-grown meat)
- Water (desalination, atmospheric water capture)

**Application:** This framework suggests that **energy independence equals resource independence** across multiple domains, raising the strategic importance of electricity infrastructure beyond traditional energy policy.

### The China Five-Year Plan Reading Strategy

Alex's approach: **"Most people don't believe it. You're like, 'Ah, that's never really going to happen.' But I just like, 'Well, let's if it does happen, what would that look like?'"**

**Application:** Take government policy documents seriously, especially from countries with strong implementation track records. Model scenarios based on stated intentions rather than dismissing them as aspirational, creating first-mover advantages.

### The Subsidy Paradox

**Framework:** Government subsidies can become a curse by:
1. Creating dependency that delays cost reductions
2. Adding bureaucratic complexity
3. Preventing "normal market" development
4. Making projects vulnerable to policy changes

**The paradox:** Subsidy removal, while initially painful, can **accelerate deployment** by forcing cost reductions, simplifying processes, and creating sustainable market structures.

**Application:** Question whether current subsidies (like the ITC) are still helping or have become obstacles. Consider whether market maturity has reached the point where subsidy removal would be beneficial.
