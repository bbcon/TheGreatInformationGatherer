# Europe Needs Clean Tech More Than Ever: A Conversation with Thomas Pellerin-Carlin

## 1. Episode Overview

**Guest Background:**
Thomas Pellerin-Carlin is a 36-year-old French MEP (Member of European Parliament) representing the Social Democrats, focusing on energy, industry, and climate policy. Born in Normandy, he spent a decade as an energy researcher with institutions including the Jacques Delors Institute and the Institute for Climate Economics, while simultaneously serving as a reserve officer in the French Army. This unique combination of energy expertise and military experience shapes his geopolitical perspective on European energy security.

**Main Topics:**
- European energy policy architecture and decision-making
- The intersection of energy security and geopolitics (Russia-Ukraine war, Iran tensions)
- Europe's clean tech industrial strategy
- Nuclear energy's role in Europe's future
- The challenge of maintaining European competitiveness while pursuing decarbonization

**Key Thesis:**
Pellerin-Carlin argues that **Europe's climate interests, economic interests, and geopolitical security interests are fundamentally aligned**. The path to European freedom and prosperity runs through clean energy transformation—not as an ideological choice, but as a geological and geopolitical necessity. Europe imports 97% of its oil and lacks the capacity (or desire) to secure fossil fuels militarily, making electrification through renewables and nuclear the only viable path to sovereignty.

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### The Geopolitical Imperative for Clean Energy

**The Framing:**
Liebreich opened by noting the episode was recorded during escalating tensions between the US/Israel and Iran, highlighting energy's role in geopolitics.

**Pellerin-Carlin's Perspective:**
The guest makes a striking argument: **"That war is not about Ukraine. It is about Europe."** He cites Russian commentator Sergey Karaganov making this explicit on Russian TV. The center of gravity—the thing that if removed would cause Russia's war effort to collapse—is **"the capacity to turn Russian oil and gas into money."**

**Key Insight:**
Europe needs what he calls a **"theory of victory"** against Russia that doesn't involve military invasion but rather economic strangulation of fossil fuel revenues. In 2024, Russia spent €140 billion on military while earning €250 billion from oil and gas. The strategy doesn't require reducing Russian revenues to zero—just low enough to make the war economically unsustainable.

**Global Dimension:**
Pellerin-Carlin extends this logic globally: **"We in Europe will be safer when India will rely more on electric cars and wind power than on Russian oil and gas."** This frames clean energy transformation as a global security imperative, not just a European one.

### How EU Energy Policy Actually Works

**The Four Tools Framework:**
Pellerin-Carlin provides the clearest explanation yet on Cleaning Up of how EU energy policy operates, identifying four main mechanisms:

1. **EU Regulations**: Hard law that applies directly to all member states, citizens, and companies once passed by Parliament and Council. No national implementation required.

2. **EU Directives**: Orders to member states to achieve specific objectives, but allows flexibility in how they're achieved (through regulation, investment, carbon pricing, etc.). Requires translation into national law.

3. **Investment Tools**: Public investment mechanisms including equity, subsidies, loans, guarantees. Examples include the Battery Booster program (EU's response to the US Inflation Reduction Act).

4. **Enabling Tools**: Softer mechanisms to build consensus and coordinate stakeholders. Example: Maroš Šefčovič's European Battery Alliance (2016-2017), which brought together mining companies, battery manufacturers, automakers, and recyclers to create a coordinated vision.

**The Fifth Element:**
Beyond these four, there's what Pellerin-Carlin calls **"the slogan"**—overarching narratives like the Green Deal, Energy Union, or REPowerEU that bundle multiple policies and provide direction, even if they lack direct legal force.

**Current Challenge:**
Since 2023, Europe has been **"dismantling part of the Green Deal"** under pressure from diesel and fossil fuel lobbies, creating what Pellerin-Carlin identifies as a critical lack of "sense of purpose and direction."

### The Electric Vehicle Case Study

**The Policy Evolution:**
Pellerin-Carlin traces EU car policy from voluntary emissions reductions in the 1990s (which failed) through mandatory CO2 standards (2006-2009) to the 2023 regulation requiring **zero-emission new cars by 2035** in the EU (2030 in UK, similar to California).

**The German Disruption:**
The German Constitutional Court (Bundesverfassungsgericht) cancelled a €60 billion investment plan, forcing Germany to eliminate its €6,000 EV subsidy overnight. The German EV market subsequently dropped ~10% in 2024, which **diesel companies weaponized** to argue EVs "can't work" and lobby for policy rollback.

**Pellerin-Carlin's Response:**
He argues for **"staying the course"** while being flexible on tactics. The fundamental logic remains: Europe doesn't have oil (imports 97%), lacks capacity/will to invade oil-producing countries, and therefore must electrify transport for prosperity and freedom.

### The "Midden" Problem: Policy Accumulation and Inflexibility

**Liebreich's Challenge:**
Liebreich introduced the archaeological term **"midden"** (a rubbish heap accumulating over centuries) to describe UK energy policy—layers of feed-in tariffs, renewable obligation certificates, contracts for difference, etc. He argues Europe has similar issues, with **outdated policies that should be discarded but remain embedded** in the system.

**The Hydrogen Example:**
The grids package includes €1.2 trillion for electrical grids (€720 billion for distribution), but also **€240 billion for hydrogen pipelines**—despite virtually no one in Brussels actually believing in 20 million tons of hydrogen, hydrogen fueling stations, or extensive pipeline networks. Yet this remains in regulations, requiring every country to maintain teams working on it.

**The Structural Speed Problem:**
Liebreich pressed on whether the EU is **"structurally going to be very slow"** to respond to invasions, scientific developments, technological learning, and political changes. The German subsidy example illustrates how the system struggles with nimbleness.

**Pellerin-Carlin's Defense:**
He offers a "yes and no" response:
- **Yes**, the EU takes time (weeks to years) to agree on things
- **But** it can move fast when needed (Russian gas regulation: proposal in June, agreement in December, Parliament position in 3 weeks)
- The EU's **"unique selling point" is predictability**: "We're a little bit slow, but we are very predictable... we will not do a U-turn like what we've seen in the US"
- He wants the EU to **"act more like an elephant"**: slow but smart, strong, not aggressive but ready to defend itself

### The Democracy vs. Efficiency Tension

**The Unanimity Problem:**
Pellerin-Carlin explains that domestic energy policy requires only majority votes in Parliament and Council, but **foreign policy requires unanimity** among all 27 member states. This allows single countries (Hungary, Slovakia) to obstruct EU will, even when acting on behalf of countries hostile to EU security.

**Liebreich's Provocation:**
This wouldn't be tolerated in functioning democracies—imagine a UK home nation declaring its ally is a country hostile to the UK.

**Pellerin-Carlin's Vision:**
He advocates for **"a federation of nation states"** (Jacques Delors' concept), arguing the EU must become more federal to survive against Putin, Trump, and China. However, he frames this not as transferring sovereignty but as **"abandoning the illusion of national sovereignty to have genuine capacity to pool sovereignty at the EU level."**

**The Reality Check:**
France, Germany, and Italy combined equal the population of Uttar Pradesh in India. European nation-states worked when Europeans dominated the world; now **"the only way for us to protect our people, to protect also our national diversity, is to be able to act together at the EU level."**

### The Electro-Democracy Vision

**Three Concentric Circles:**
Pellerin-Carlin proposes a geopolitical framework:

1. **Core EU**: The federation of European nation-states
2. **Alliance of Electro-Democracies**: Countries that don't want to be vassals of China, US, or Russia—including Canada, Australia, Senegal, Chile, India, South Africa, Ethiopia
3. **Global clean tech cooperation**: Post-colonial partnerships based on mutual benefit

**The China Question:**
Liebreich challenged: China does "electro" better than anyone, so should Europe align with China against "petro-authoritarians"?

Pellerin-Carlin's response: Europe has a **"historic duty to be one provider of clean tech for the rest of the world."** If Europe doesn't, 100% of clean tech will be Chinese. Other democracies need options to **de-risk from China** (suggesting 20-30% of battery/EV/wind imports from Europe rather than total Chinese dependence).

**The Post-Colonial Framework:**
He acknowledges Europe's colonial legacy (Senegal oppressed by French Empire, Ireland by British, Poland by Russian/German/Austrian empires) and argues for building a **"post-colonial power"** through win-win partnerships. Example: Working with Chile on lithium—some exported unrefined, some refined in Chile, some made into batteries there, creating mutual benefit rather than extraction.

### The Energy Cost Competitiveness Challenge

**The Problem:**
Liebreich pressed hard on this: European energy costs are crippling chemicals, metals, steel, and other industries. Europe has neither cheap clean energy nor cheap fossil energy—**just expensive energy of all types**. This undermines all industrial ambitions, including AI where Europe is "clearly behind the curve."

**Pellerin-Carlin's Three-Part Solution:**

1. **Nuclear**: Legacy nuclear provides cheap, decarbonized electricity (France, Belgium, Sweden). He supports continuing existing plants as long as safety engineers approve, plus building new plants for the second half of the century. His Social Democratic party is one of few left-wing parties supporting new French nuclear.

2. **Flexible Demand**: Heat pumps, EVs, industrial heat storage (easier than electricity storage). Example: Breweries or cheese factories storing heat to stop consuming electricity for hours/days, providing grid flexibility.

3. **Collocating Supply and Demand**: Historical example: After the Iranian Revolution, France had two nuclear reactor vessels intended for the Shah. They were installed at Gravelines near Dunkirk, then an aluminum factory was built there consuming electricity equivalent to the city of Marseille (1 million people). Modern example: Dunkirk as an "energy super island" for battery factories and potentially data centers.

**The Granular Reality:**
Pellerin-Carlin challenges the narrative that EU electricity is uniformly expensive. While the average is higher than the US, **comparing specific regions shows some EU areas (northern Sweden, France) are already cheaper than some US areas (Texas, New England)**. This proves the concept works.

**The Fundamental Advantage:**
Since the 2021 Russian decision to cut gas supply, Europe lives with **Japanese-level gas prices**. Europe can never outcompete the US on oil and gas (geological reality). But on electricity from renewables and nuclear, Europe can be cost-competitive, and **more resilient to oil and gas shocks** that will become more frequent as the world destabilizes.

### The Nuclear Debate

**Flamanville's Lessons:**
Liebreich challenged Pellerin-Carlin on Flamanville—supposed to take 4.5 years, took 18 (4x longer); cost 7x budget according to French national auditors. Can Europe beat China, which builds nuclear at ~30% of European costs?

**Pellerin-Carlin's Explanation:**
Flamanville was expensive because **"the company had to start from scratch."** France built well through the 1970s-90s, then stopped. The entire value chain, SMEs, suppliers disappeared. Flamanville was closer to a **demonstration project cost** than commercial deployment.

**The China Question:**
He argues Europe **"cannot surrender our entire industry into Chinese hands"** or lose freedom. Nuclear is strategic—Chinese investment in elderly care or highways is fine, **but not in nuclear**. He supports a **"Made in Europe"/"Buy European" approach** for strategic sectors.

**The Export Challenge:**
Can Europe export nuclear? The problem isn't just technology but **financing packages to de-risk investment**. China and Russia succeed partly because they offer comprehensive financing. Europe needs to **decrease cost of capital** both domestically and for exports—critical for nuclear, offshore wind, and building renovation.

**Small Modular Reactors:**
Liebreich noted the EU is spending ~€9.5 billion on nuclear in the next budget cycle, but **more than half goes to ITER fusion** (which he called a "black hole"). After removing fusion and support for existing generations, only ~€200 million here and there remains for SMRs—**less than individual US private equity investors** put into single SMR technologies.

**Cultural Divisions:**
Pellerin-Carlin explains nuclear is **"extremely divisive"** in Europe in ways it isn't in the US:
- **France**: Centralized state tradition; nuclear associated with military and defending freedom; the bomb was the answer to WWII defeat
- **Germany**: Decentralization ideal; preference for distributed generation (onshore wind, solar) reflects political vision

This cultural dimension explains why Europe "drags its feet" on nuclear despite some technical advantages.

### The Priorities Question

**The Numbers:**
Nuclear accounts for **~10% of final energy** in Europe. Two-thirds of European energy is oil and gas. 

**Pellerin-Carlin's Hierarchy:**
The **"big priority is to phase out oil and gas"** for:
- Geopolitical survival (Europe doesn't have it)
- Climate and air pollution
- Economic protection from future shocks

**The Answer:**
**"Primarily about efficiency and renewables. You can do more or less nuclear, but you cannot do without efficiency, you cannot do without renewables."**

Some nations will choose nuclear (Slovakia, France, Sweden); others won't (Denmark, Germany, possibly Italy). Europe must be **"united in diversity"**—united on efficiency and renewables, accepting national diversity on nuclear.

**Democracy Above All:**
When Liebreich pressed about Spanish colleagues shutting nuclear plants with long lifetimes despite evidence this raises costs and slows climate progress, Pellerin-Carlin responded: **"I put democracy and freedom above all... people are free to make their own choices."** He doesn't want France to shut plants, but respects national sovereign decisions.

## 5. Practical Implications

### For Markets/Investing

**Battery Value Chains**: Europe is attempting to build complete battery value chains (mining in Finland/France, manufacturing, recycling). The Battery Booster program signals policy support, though Pellerin-Carlin admits it's **"too little, too late"** compared to the US Inflation Reduction Act. Investors should watch for:

- De-risking mechanisms to lower cost of capital
- Collocation opportunities (Dunkirk as example)

**Nuclear Renaissance—With Caveats**: France and some other nations will build new nuclear, but costs remain problematic (Flamanville 7x budget, 4x timeline). The real opportunity may be in:
- Financing packages to de-risk investment (where Europe currently lags China/Russia)
- SMR development (though current EU funding is inadequate)
- Life extensions of existing plants (where safety permits)

**Regional Energy Price Arbitrage**: Not all of Europe has expensive electricity. Northern Sweden, parts of France, and other areas are already competitive with parts of the US. **Opportunities exist in collocating energy-intensive industry** (aluminum, data centers, battery manufacturing) near cheap renewable resources.

**EV Market Volatility**: The German subsidy elimination shows how quickly markets can shift based on fiscal policy. However, the underlying regulation (zero-emission vehicles by 2035) remains in place. **Long-term trajectory is clear; short-term volatility is high**.

**Clean Tech Export Finance**: Europe lacks competitive export financing for clean tech (nuclear, offshore wind, etc.). Companies that can navigate or provide financing solutions have opportunities, as this is a recognized gap versus Chinese/Russian competitors.

### For Understanding Macro
