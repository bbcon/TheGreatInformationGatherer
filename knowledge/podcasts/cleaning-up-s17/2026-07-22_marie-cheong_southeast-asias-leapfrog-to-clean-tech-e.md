# Southeast Asia's Leapfrog to Clean Tech: A Conversation with Marie Cheong

## 1. Episode Overview

Marie Cheong is a founding partner of 100 by 100 (spinning out from Wavemaker Impact), a climate tech venture builder and VC firm operating across South and Southeast Asia. A self-described "reluctant venture capitalist," Cheong's passion lies in building companies that can meaningfully decarbonize the world.

The episode, recorded in Singapore during Ecosperity Week, explores the unique opportunities and challenges of climate tech in Southeast Asia—a region representing 7% of global emissions but receiving less than 3% of climate VC funding. The conversation includes a fascinating factory visit to People's Bihun Factory, where portfolio company Moon AI is using artificial intelligence to optimize noodle production.

**Key thesis**: Southeast Asia can "skip the landline moment" by greening energy demand at the point of consumption, rather than waiting for large-scale grid transformation. With energy demand set to double in 20 years and a burgeoning middle class, the region faces a critical choice between locking in carbon-intensive infrastructure or leapfrogging to clean solutions.

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### The 100 by 100 Model: Venture Building vs. Traditional VC

**The Challenge**: Southeast Asia lacks sufficient climate tech startups to support a traditional VC strategy, despite having tremendous entrepreneurial talent.

**Cheong's Solution**: Work with experienced entrepreneurs over 6 months to build "100 by 100 companies"—businesses with potential to abate **100 million tons of CO2 equivalent** and generate **$100 million in revenue** at scale. The goal: create 50 such companies to abate 5 gigatons (10% of global emissions).

The fund has deployed **$60 million** across **23 portfolio companies** since 2021, initially targeting $25 million but hitting their hard cap due to strong investor interest. Portfolio companies tackle everything from rice cultivation (the second-largest driver of agricultural emissions globally) to coal replacement from agricultural waste, bamboo alternatives to wood products, and AI solutions for fashion overproduction.

**Key insight**: "We can abate 50% of global carbon emissions using the technology that we have today. There's green discounts everywhere. These are more engineering problems than they are tech problems."

### The "Skip the Landline Moment" 

**Framing**: Just as many developing countries bypassed landline infrastructure and went straight to mobile phones, Southeast Asia can bypass carbon-intensive development.

**The Opportunity**: Energy demand doubling in 20 years is driven by first-time purchases—air conditioners, cars replacing two-wheelers, modern appliances. Cheong argues for "greening it at the point of demand rather than having to wait for a big energy transition to happen."

**Example - Arisan Solar**: One portfolio company sells small-scale solar systems with batteries to tier 2 and tier 3 Indonesian cities. The killer insight wasn't about solar or savings—it was about **certainty**. Indonesia's state utility (PLN) uses a prepaid token system, forcing households to constantly top up credits. This causes people to ration electricity use ("Do I run my washing machine today?") and results in 80% of households having at least one broken appliance due to power fluctuations.

Arisan's solar-plus-battery systems, priced at the cost of a TV or fridge, eliminate "range anxiety for washing machines." The company achieved first revenue within weeks of launching with tremendous growth rates.

**The Challenge**: These hyper-local insights don't necessarily translate across the region's 11+ countries, making scaling complex despite similar income levels.

### Manufacturing: The Hidden Gigaton

**The Scale**: Manufacturing in Southeast Asia emits **1 gigaton annually**—equivalent to all global aviation emissions and representing about half of the region's 2-gigaton total footprint.

**The Problem**: Small and medium enterprises (SMEs) form the "heartbeat" of Southeast Asian manufacturing. These are often family-owned businesses producing everything from noodles to textiles—the supply chain hub for global consumer goods.

**Why It Matters**: Billions have been invested in modern infrastructure. These are "stranded assets" at risk from climate change, with cities like Bangkok potentially underwater by 2050.

**Moon AI Case Study**: The noodle factory visit illustrated the opportunity. People's Bihun Factory, operating since 1947, had invested in Industry 4.0 sensors generating **terabytes of data** but lacked the capability to extract insights. They were "data-rich but insight-poor."

Moon AI's intervention revealed:
- Two of five steaming chambers were over-processing by 20-25 minutes
- One chamber had a potential seal leakage (detected through pressure drift patterns)
- Significant opportunities for energy optimization

**Critical Framework**: Cheong looks for both a **"carbon pocket"** (how to reduce emissions) and a **"value pocket"** (how to create business value). Energy costs alone don't motivate change—businesses view electricity as "cost of doing business" and focus on growth. The real incentives are reducing batch rejection rates, lowering maintenance costs, and improving customer experience.

As Liebreich observed during the visit, the natural gas-powered dryer could be replaced with industrial heat pumps (like those from his portfolio company Heaton), achieving efficiency gains even if still powered by gas, with a clear pathway to renewable electricity.

### Regional Context and Climate Attitudes

**Geographic Scope**: 
- **Southeast Asia**: 11 ASEAN countries including Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia (200M people), Philippines, Vietnam, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, Brunei
- **South Asia**: Primarily India (also ~7% of global emissions but as a single country), Bangladesh, Pakistan
- Combined population: approximately half a billion+

**Economic Transformation**: The region has experienced "incredible growth in generational wealth" with people moving from subsistence farming to urban middle class in a single generation. This creates a population with new disposable income thinking about "experiences and consumerism, not degrowth."

**Climate Perception**: Cheong argues the region hasn't been "hysterically involved in climate action" but also isn't falling out of love with it like some Western countries. The focus remains on development and poverty alleviation. 

**Key challenge**: "Emissions and growth in Southeast Asia is very very closely linked. We haven't seen that delinking that happens in China, that happens in the EU."

**Climate Vulnerability**: Over 100 million people rely on coastal regions for livelihoods. The region faces unstable weather, typhoons, and crop impacts. Bangkok could be underwater by 2050, with massive stranded infrastructure assets at risk.

**Energy Crisis Reality**: The Philippines has approximately 20 days of energy supply remaining due to Strait of Hormuz tensions. This drives demand for solar appliances and battery systems. One team member plugs her Wi-Fi into a battery to work from home during frequent brownouts. The Philippines has the region's highest energy prices and most unstable grid despite a liberal energy market.

## 3. Frameworks & Mental Models

### The Carbon Pocket + Value Pocket Framework

When evaluating opportunities, Cheong simultaneously identifies:

1. **Carbon Pocket**: Where and how emissions can be reduced
2. **Value Pocket**: What business value drives adoption beyond emissions reduction

**Application**: Don't assume businesses will optimize energy "just for the sake of optimizing energy." Find incentives aligned with growth priorities—reduced waste, improved quality control, lower maintenance, better customer service.

### The "Emissions Live in the Real World" Principle

**Core idea**: "If we don't change how noodles are made, then we're not really going to solve for emissions. It doesn't matter how many conferences we do in air-conditioned Singapore hotels."

**Application**: Move from ideological climate imperatives to practical business cases. Decarbonize in ways that make business sense, not just environmental sense.

### Skip the Landline vs. Grid Transformation

**The Choice**: Southeast Asia can either:
- Wait for large-scale utility grid transformation (slow, capital-intensive)
- Green demand at the point of consumption (fast, distributed, market-driven)

**Application**: Focus on behind-the-meter solutions, consumer appliances, and distributed generation that solve immediate problems (reliability, cost certainty) while delivering climate benefits.

### The Anti-"Silver Bullet" Thesis

Cheong explicitly rejects what Liebreich calls "bro VC" thinking—the belief that only breakthrough technologies (fusion, direct air capture, superconducting transmission) matter.

**Counter-argument**: "We can abate 50% of global carbon emissions using the technology that we have today." The focus should be on deployment, business model innovation, and solving "engineering problems" rather than waiting for technological breakthroughs.

**Regional application**: Technologies like hydrogen have been discussed for a decade in Southeast Asia but remain far from meaningful impact, while solar appliances generate revenue within weeks.

## 4. Contrarian Takes

### Against Degrowth in Developing Economies

Cheong firmly pushes back on applying degrowth frameworks to Southeast Asia: "You've got households that suddenly have disposable income...they're thinking about experiences, consumerism. They're not thinking about degrowth."

**Reasoning**: Two to three generations have benefited from tremendous growth. Life is "getting better" with widespread optimism. Asking this population to embrace degrowth is both unrealistic and arguably unfair given historical emissions from developed nations.

### Manufacturing as Climate Priority Over Aviation

By highlighting that Southeast Asian manufacturing alone equals global aviation emissions, Cheong implicitly challenges the disproportionate attention paid to sectors like aviation and sustainable aviation fuel (SAF).

**Implication**: The climate community may be overlooking massive emission sources in developing world manufacturing while obsessing over relatively smaller sectors in developed economies.

### The Data Paradox in Industry 4.0

**Contrarian observation**: Factories investing in Industry 4.0 and IoT sensors are generating massive data but lack the capability to use it. They're "data-rich but insight-poor."

**Challenge to conventional wisdom**: Digital transformation alone doesn't drive efficiency or decarbonization. The value lies in the intelligence layer that makes data actionable—which is where AI companies like Moon AI enter.

### Against Waiting for Policy/Grid Solutions

Rather than focusing on government policy, carbon pricing, or utility-scale renewable deployment, Cheong's approach works around these constraints by focusing on behind-the-meter solutions and direct business value.

**Reasoning**: In a region where "emissions and growth are very closely linked" and grids are unstable, waiting for top-down transformation means locking in decades of carbon-intensive infrastructure.

## 5. Practical Implications

### For Investors

**Geographic opportunity**: Southeast Asia is dramatically underfunded (3% of climate VC despite 7% of emissions) with strong entrepreneurial talent and massive growth trajectory.

**Venture building model**: In nascent markets lacking sufficient startups, the venture builder model—spending 6 months co-creating companies with experienced entrepreneurs—can generate better deal flow than traditional VC sourcing.

**Due diligence insight**: Talk to ~1,000 founders annually to find 8 worth building with. The ratio matters.

**Portfolio construction**: 23 companies from $60M suggests initial checks around $2-3M, appropriate for venture building with significant hands-on support.

### For Climate Tech Entrepreneurs

**Market entry strategy**: Solve immediate business problems (reliability, waste reduction, quality control) that happen to reduce emissions, rather than leading with climate benefits.

**Localization imperative**: Insights that work in Indonesia (prepaid electricity tokens) don't translate to Vietnam or Malaysia. Deep local knowledge is essential—"you can't just sit in London and pontificate about Southeast Asia."

**Technology pragmatism**: Proven technologies with business model innovation often beat breakthrough tech. Solar appliances generated revenue in weeks; hydrogen has been discussed for a decade with minimal impact.

**Customer insight**: The factory visit revealed that 80% of households had broken appliances due to power fluctuations—a problem solar-plus-battery solves beyond just energy savings.

### For Understanding Macro/Energy Transition

**The doubling demand challenge**: Southeast Asian energy demand doubling in 20 years is driven by first-time purchases and economic growth. This is fundamentally different from developed economy transitions focused on replacing existing infrastructure.

**Coal's resilience role**: Countries like Vietnam and Indonesia have abundant domestic coal, making it the "easiest to grow" and most resilient fallback. The question becomes whether coal can play a flexible, backup role (like gas in Europe) rather than baseload.

**Stranded asset risk**: Modern infrastructure built over 50 years of rapid development faces climate risks (sea level rise, extreme weather) creating massive potential write-downs.

**Trade and supply chains**: Southeast Asia as a global supply chain hub means emissions here are embedded in products consumed globally (H&M clothes, shoes, plastics). Decarbonizing the region has worldwide implications.

### Actionable Takeaways

**For manufacturers**:
1. Audit existing sensor/data infrastructure—you may already have the data needed for optimization
2. Look beyond energy bills to batch rejection rates, maintenance costs, and quality control as optimization targets
3. Consider behind-the-meter solar-plus-battery for reliability and cost certainty, not just sustainability

**For policymakers**:
1. Enable distributed generation and behind-the-meter solutions rather than only focusing on utility-scale projects
2. Recognize that prepaid electricity systems (while improving payment collection) create barriers to efficient energy use
3. Support SME decarbonization with technical assistance, not just financing

**For climate funders**:
1. Manufacturing in developing economies deserves attention proportional to its emissions (1 gigaton in Southeast Asia alone)
2. Venture building models can create deal flow in nascent markets
3. The "value pocket" matters as much as the "carbon pocket"—fund solutions that solve business problems

**For technology providers**:
1. Industrial heat pumps, AI optimization, and solar-plus-storage have immediate applications
2. Focus on quick wins and rapid revenue rather than long development cycles
3. Partner with local entrepreneurs who understand regional nuances
