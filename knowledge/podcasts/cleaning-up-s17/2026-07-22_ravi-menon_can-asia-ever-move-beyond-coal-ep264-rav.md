# Can Asia Ever Move Beyond Coal? | Episode Summary

## 1. Episode Overview

**Guest:** Ravi Menon, Singapore's Ambassador for Climate Action and Senior Advisor to the Prime Minister's Office on climate matters. Previously served as Managing Director of Singapore's Monetary Authority (central bank) and Permanent Secretary for Trade.

**Main Topic:** Asia's unique climate transition challenges, particularly the coal phase-out problem, and innovative financing mechanisms to accelerate decarbonization in the region.

**Key Thesis:** Asia cannot simply "grow the green stuff and leave the rest behind" like Europe or America. With 60-70% of Asia's electricity from coal and energy demand expected to double by 2050, the region must actively phase down existing fossil infrastructure while scaling renewables—requiring fundamentally different approaches to climate finance and policy.

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### Singapore's Unique Decarbonization Challenge

**The Problem:** Singapore faces what Menon calls being "**alternate energy disadvantaged**"—a city-state with limited land area but a substantial manufacturing base (20% of GDP), including carbon-intensive oil refining and petrochemicals.

**Key Constraints:**
- Solar potential limited to 6-8% of electricity needs (currently 2%), possibly reaching mid-teens with technology improvements
- Dense cloud cover reduces solar insolation compared to India or Australia
- No space for wind turbines
- Nuclear politically challenging given small land area
- Yet must achieve net zero by 2050 like other nations

**Singapore's Three-Transition Framework:**

1. **Energy Transition:** Greening the electricity grid through maximized solar deployment and renewable energy imports
2. **Carbon Transition:** Addressing non-electricity emissions from industrial heating, cooling, and chemical processes
3. **Economic Transition:** Restructuring the economy by growing green sectors and "greening what is brown" (the 80% of GDP currently fossil-dependent)

Menon emphasized the importance of Singapore's **National Climate Change Secretariat** under the PM's office, which coordinates climate policy across all government agencies—solving the coordination challenge that plagues many countries.

### The Asia Coal Problem: Why "Just Build Renewables" Won't Work

**The Core Disagreement:** Host Michael Liebreich advocates focusing on rapidly scaling clean energy, letting fossil fuels decline naturally through market forces. Menon pushes back forcefully: **"I don't quite agree that all we need to do is to grow the green stuff and leave the rest behind. It doesn't work in Asia."**

**Menon's Reasoning:**
- 60-70% of Asia's electricity comes from coal (down to 50% in China from 80%)
- Energy demand expected to **double by 2050** in Asia
- If existing coal fleet remains operational, it will consume the bulk of the world's remaining carbon budget
- China built 90 GW of coal last year (more than 2x rest of world combined), even while burning less coal overall
- Southeast Asia faces particular challenges: poor transmission infrastructure, lack of battery storage financing, inability to charge high electricity tariffs to poor rural populations

**The Counterargument (Liebreich):** 
- China and India are already burning less coal despite building capacity
- Coal is being forced into backup role by renewable economics
- Pakistan achieved massive solar deployment through unregulated market forces, saving $6 billion in foreign exchange
- Private capital is available for renewable projects

**Menon's Response:** Continental economies like China and India have invested heavily in grids and storage. Southeast Asia's **poor transmission infrastructure** makes the challenge fundamentally different—the issue isn't financing solar panels, but financing the grid systems and battery storage needed to integrate them.

### The FAST-P Blended Finance Model

**The Financing Gap Problem:** 
- Cost of capital in Asia is very high due to actual and perceived risks
- Carbon pricing is too low or non-existent
- Public capital is insufficient (governments face deficits, competing priorities like defense)
- Private capital exists but won't take the risk on marginally bankable projects

**FAST-P Structure (Financing Asia's Transition Partnership):**

Menon introduced an innovative **program-level blended finance approach** rather than project-by-project negotiations:

- **$500 million** from Singapore government as grant (first-loss capital)
- **$500 million** matched from other sovereigns, DFIs, philanthropies
- **$1 billion total concessional base** to crowd in **$4 billion commercial capital**
- First-loss capital absorbs initial risks (project delays, missed payments)
- Commercial investors bear zero percent of first losses, then their proportional share

**Key Innovation:** Unlike traditional blended finance that goes directly into projects (causing endless negotiations between concessional and commercial providers), FAST-P operates at the **platform level**. Terms are settled once at the program level, then fund managers deploy capital more agilely.

**The Additionality Challenge:** Liebreich raised concerns about the CDM (Clean Development Mechanism) experience, where proving "additionality" spawned an industry of consultants gaming the system. Menon acknowledged this is "**more art than science**" but argued the ~30% of projects in the "marginally bankable" zone is substantial enough to warrant the approach. He conceded: "At the margin you'll make some mistakes on either end...I think we don't we shouldn't let the perfect be the enemy of the good."

Notably, Menon agreed that if FAST-P never loses money, that would actually be **worrying**—indicating they funded projects that didn't need concessional capital.

### Carbon Markets: Reform, Don't Exit

**Current State:** The voluntary carbon market has "basically tanked"—transaction volumes collapsed, prices collapsed, demand and supply frozen. Reputational damage from conservation projects that didn't actually remove carbon and flawed crediting methodologies.

**Menon's Position:** "If a market is not functioning, the solution is not to exit it, but to **reform it**, to make it work."

**Market Distinction:**
- **Compliance markets** (Article 6 of Paris Agreement): Held up relatively well due to strict criteria and sovereign involvement, but small (mainly Singapore, Japan, Korea, Switzerland, Sweden, Luxembourg)
- **Voluntary markets:** Developed organically without regime, facing integrity problems

**Why Carbon Markets Matter:** Menon sees carbon markets and blended finance as the only viable financing pathways to net zero, given insufficient public capital and private capital's risk aversion.

### Energy Security and Climate Convergence

**The Positive Shift:** Menon observed that **energy security and climate transition agendas have started to converge** in Asia, driven by events in the Strait of Hormuz and oil/gas price volatility.

**Evidence:**
- EV demand surging not from climate concern but from fuel station queues in Manila, Vientiane, Bangkok
- BYD outsold Toyota at Bangkok Auto Show for first time in history
- Double and triple-digit EV growth rates across region
- 40-50% of new car sales in Singapore are electric
- Policy to eliminate internal combustion engines by 2040

**The Caveat:** "It's a **fragile convergence**. It can break down." Asia still represents >50% of global emissions and that proportion will increase due to growth, urbanization, and need to electrify millions in poor villages.

### Renewable Energy Imports and Long-Distance Transmission

**The Vision:** Singapore is negotiating renewable electricity imports, having already started small-scale imports through Laos-Cambodia-Malaysia line.

**Long-Distance Projects Discussed:**
- **Sun Cable:** Australia to Singapore subsea cable (thousands of kilometers, "exorbitant" cost but still under consideration)
- India's east coast to Singapore across Bay of Bengal
- **Xlinks:** Morocco to UK (Liebreich is an investor)
- Proposed UK to northeast US (4,000 km, 15% losses)

**Menon's Analogy:** Just as earlier eras traded goods and services for mutual prosperity, the world needs to transition to **"trading green electrons"** between renewable-surplus and renewable-deficit regions.

**The Challenge:** Requires multilateral frameworks before it can scale. Individual links are vulnerable (can't have any one link providing too much power). Liebreich argued that comparing "exorbitant" cable costs to oil/gas price shocks changes the calculation—if resilience risks were properly priced in.

### Biofuels: The Right and Wrong Applications

**Singapore's Approach:** Focusing biofuels on **maritime bunkering** and extremely heavy vehicles, not land transportation.

**Liebreich's Strong Position:** Almost every liter of biofuels globally goes into land transportation—"**completely absurd**" given EV economics already beat diesel, even at European electricity prices. Heavy goods vehicles have even better economics due to high utilization rates.

**The Bioeconomy Opportunity:** Southeast Asia has substantial potential from agricultural waste and waste-to-energy, but must ensure bio sources don't result from deforestation or wasteful agricultural practices. Malaysia could produce significant biogas (Denmark gets 45% of gas from agricultural biogas).

**Ammonia Reality Check:** Pilot projects using green ammonia for bunkering and combustion, but Liebreich noted current costs around **$400/MWh** for ammonia-based power generation—truly exorbitant.

## 3. Frameworks & Mental Models

### The Three Transitions Framework

Singapore's approach to net zero comprises three parallel transitions:
1. **Energy Transition** (greening electricity)
2. **Carbon Transition** (non-electricity emissions)
3. **Economic Transition** (restructuring economy, building skills)

**Application:** This framework recognizes that electricity decarbonization alone is insufficient—must address industrial processes and economic restructuring simultaneously.

### Program-Level vs. Project-Level Blended Finance

**Traditional Approach:** Concessional and commercial capital providers negotiate terms for each individual project, leading to endless arguments about risk allocation and returns.

**FAST-P Innovation:** Settle terms once at platform level, then deploy capital through fund managers without project-by-project negotiations.

**Application:** Can be replicated by other regions/countries. Menon estimates need for "**about another dozen blended finance platforms**" of similar scale.

### The 30-70-30 Project Bankability Distribution

Menon's estimate of project landscape:
- ~30% commercially viable (don't need concessional capital)
- ~30% marginally bankable (FAST-P target)
- ~40% completely unviable (can't be helped with current approach)

**Application:** Focus concessional capital on the middle 30%—substantial enough to move the needle, tractable enough to achieve results.

### Policy-Technology-Finance Trinity

**Menon's "Three Bullets":**
1. **Policy** (especially carbon pricing, competitive market structures)
2. **Technology** (some areas still not good enough)
3. **Finance** (the oil that greases the machine, but not the machine itself)

**Key Insight:** "Finance can't do this job alone...there is no silver bullet in any of this."

**Application:** Integrated approach required—can't rely on any single lever.

## 4. Contrarian Takes

### "Growing the Green Stuff" Isn't Enough in Asia

**Consensus View:** Focus on rapidly scaling renewables; fossil fuels will decline naturally through market forces and economics.

**Menon's Challenge:** This works in Europe/America but not Asia. Must actively phase down existing coal fleet or it will consume remaining carbon budget, especially with energy demand doubling by 2050.

**Reasoning:** Unlike mature economies with stable/declining energy demand, Asia's growth trajectory means leaving existing infrastructure in place is catastrophic for climate, regardless of renewable additions.

### MDBs Are Part of the Problem

**Consensus View:** Multilateral Development Banks (World Bank, ADB) are key climate finance institutions.

**Menon's Reality Check:** "**MDBs don't do this. The MDBs don't fund marginally bankable projects.**" They're constrained by shareholders, need to maintain AAA ratings, and end up funding commercially viable projects—crowding out rather than crowding in private capital.

**Reasoning:** Institutional structure prevents MDBs from taking first-loss positions. Even progressive institutions like ADB can only use trust funds (philanthropic money), not shareholder capital, for higher-risk climate projects.

### Private Capital Abundance Doesn't Solve the Problem

**Liebreich's Position:** Enormous private capital available; main barriers are policy (price signals, market structures) and project development capacity.

**Menon's Counterargument:** "Private markets total financial assets are several dozens of times or hundreds of times what's needed...It's all a matter of **risk and return**. And those mechanics don't work."

**Reasoning:** Capital availability is necessary but not sufficient. Without mechanisms to bridge the risk-return gap (blended finance, carbon markets), private capital won't flow to where it's needed most.

### The Multilateral Order Requires New Approaches

**On the changing world order:** "Worry is not a strategy." Rather than lamenting the decline of US-led global governance, small and middle countries must create **"variable geometries"**—new coalitions of the willing on specific issues that don't map to existing multilateral forums.

**Reasoning:** As a small country and "price taker," Singapore must be flexible and nimble, finding new corridors and relationships (Middle East, India, East Africa) rather than waiting for global leadership to return.

## 5. Practical Implications

### For Markets/Investing

**EV Opportunity in Asia:** Consumer-driven adoption accelerating due to energy security concerns, not climate policy. BYD's Bangkok success signals major market shift. Singapore targeting 40-50% of new sales already electric.

**Biofuels Misallocation:** Current biofuel use in land transportation represents massive misallocation. Investors should focus on maritime/aviation applications and avoid land transport biofuel projects.

**Grid and Storage Infrastructure:** In Southeast Asia, the bottleneck isn't renewable generation capacity but **transmission and battery storage**. These require blended finance approaches—pure commercial plays face challenges.

**Long-Distance Transmission:** Multiple HVDC subsea cable projects (Australia-Singapore, India-Singapore, Morocco-UK) will eventually happen, but need to be part of diversified networks. Time zone arbitrage particularly valuable.

**Carbon Market Caution:** Voluntary carbon markets remain troubled despite reform efforts. Compliance markets (Article 6) more credible but small. Wait for integrity frameworks to solidify before major commitments.

### For Understanding Macro

**Asia's Energy Demand Trajectory:** Expected doubling by 2050 fundamentally changes climate math. Solutions that work for mature economies with flat/declining demand don't translate.

**Energy Security-Climate Convergence:** Strait of Hormuz disruptions and oil/gas price volatility are driving Asian climate action more effectively than climate policy. This convergence is "fragile" but currently powerful.

**Fossil Fuel Subsidy Crisis:** Malaysia facing budget crisis from fossil subsidies—expect similar pressures across region to accelerate policy changes.

**China's Coal Paradox:** Building massive coal capacity while burning less coal overall. Capacity being forced into backup role by renewable economics, but Southeast Asia can't replicate this without grid/storage investment.

### Actionable Takeaways

**For Singapore/Small States:**
- Maximize solar deployment even with constraints (rooftops barely utilized)
- Electrify land transport aggressively (economics already favorable)
- Negotiate renewable import agreements with multiple sources (diversification critical)
- Position as regional climate finance hub through platforms like FAST-P

**For Development Finance:**
- Shift from project-level to program-level blended finance structures
- Accept that first-loss capital should actually experience losses (if it doesn't, you're funding wrong projects)
- Focus on the ~30% of marginally bankable projects, not commercially viable ones
- Create more FAST-P-style platforms (need ~dozen more at similar scale)

**For Private Investors:**
- Heavy goods vehicle charging infrastructure in Asia (better economics than light vehicles)
- Grid and battery storage in Southeast Asia (with blended finance partnerships)
- EV-related infrastructure in ASEAN countries (consumer demand accelerating)
- Avoid land transport biofuel projects; focus maritime/aviation

**For Policy Makers:**
- Carbon pricing remains "single most important thing"
- Coordinate climate policy across government agencies (Singapore's NCCS model)
- Create competitive, market-based energy systems (enables financing and technology solutions)
- Build "variable geometry" coalitions rather than waiting for global frameworks
