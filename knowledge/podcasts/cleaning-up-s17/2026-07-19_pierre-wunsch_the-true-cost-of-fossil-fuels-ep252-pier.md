# The True Cost of Fossil Fuels: A Central Banker's Perspective on Energy, Inflation, and Climate

## 1. Episode Overview

**Guest:** Pierre Wunsch, Governor of the National Bank of Belgium, member of the European Central Bank's Governing Council, and Board member of the International Monetary Fund

**Relevance:** Wunsch sits at the intersection of monetary policy, financial stability, and climate transition debates in Europe. As a central banker with a PhD in economics and prior experience in energy sector regulation, he brings both technical expertise and institutional authority to discussions about the macroeconomic impacts of energy volatility and climate policy.

**Main Topics:**
- Energy price shocks and inflation management
- The true costs of climate transition versus fossil fuel volatility
- Central banks' appropriate role in climate policy
- Europe's industrial competitiveness challenges
- Realistic pathways to decarbonization

**Key Thesis:** The climate debate has become polarized between "great opportunity" narratives and "project fear," obscuring the need for honest, granular analysis of transition costs and benefits. Europe needs a "climate reset" - a pragmatic, sector-by-sector approach that acknowledges real tradeoffs while maintaining credible decarbonization pathways.

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### Energy Shocks and Monetary Policy

**The Question:** How should central banks respond to energy price spikes, particularly in light of recent experience with post-COVID inflation?

**Wunsch's Perspective:** Central banks got it wrong with "team transitory." The conventional wisdom held that supply shocks (like energy price spikes) should be "looked through" rather than countered with interest rate hikes. This consensus emerged after the 1970s-80s oil shocks but proved disastrous during 2021-2022.

**Key Insights:**
- **The "team transitory" mistake**: Inflation went from 2% to 3% to 4% to 10% while central banks waited, believing energy shocks would be temporary
- **Why it failed**: The shock lasted long enough and combined with fiscal support (like the IRA) to trigger broader inflation in goods and services, eventually driving wage demands
- **The new approach**: Central banks are now "much more cautious" and willing to react to energy shocks even if they're supply-driven
- **Current Gulf crisis response**: If oil and gas prices remain elevated for "another one or two months," rate hikes become "a possibility"

**The Irony:** Wunsch notes it's "a bit weird" that previous high inflation episodes came from energy shocks requiring intervention, yet a "new consensus" had emerged that central banks shouldn't react to supply shocks.

### The Hidden Costs of Fossil Fuel Volatility

**The Framing:** The debate has focused on clean energy's costs and externalities while ignoring fossil fuels' own massive externalities.

**Wunsch's Analysis:** When oil prices spike 20%, "people get nervous"; at 50%, they become "very nervous." Yet the climate narrative suggested forcing people to stop using gas and oil would be "a great opportunity."

**Key Mechanism:** The **volatility cost of fossil fuels** has never been properly priced in. Assumptions always treated oil and gas prices as stable baselines, with clean energy bearing all adjustment costs.

**Supporting Evidence:** 
- Current Gulf crisis demonstrates ongoing exposure to geopolitical energy price shocks
- Europe's post-Ukraine invasion experience showed the economic damage of energy dependence
- The UK's Climate Change Committee found that "one oil and gas price spike costs the country more than the net zero transition"

**Liebreich's Synthesis:** We're experiencing a Hegelian moment - thesis (clean energy is expensive), antithesis (fossil fuels are stable and cheap), synthesis (both have externalities; proper comparison reveals clean energy's advantages).

### The Cost of Climate Transition: Bottom-Up Analysis

**The Question:** What does net zero actually cost for a developed European economy?

**Wunsch's Methodology:** Rather than relying on macro models with "one number," the National Bank of Belgium conducted **granular, bottom-up analysis** - technology by technology, sector by sector.

**The Numbers for Belgium:**
- **Total cost: 3.5% of GDP** by 2050 (relative to today's GDP, or ~2.5-3% of 2050 GDP)
- **Annual growth impact: -0.10 percentage points** per year
- **Context:** "From a macro perspective, it's like a permanent oil shock" - significant but absorbable

**Why Bottom-Up Matters:**
- Enables conversations with industry experts to validate assumptions
- Reveals which technologies are cheap vs. expensive
- Identifies where political challenges will emerge
- Avoids the "great opportunity" trap of investment-focused models

**The Investment Fallacy:** Wunsch challenges the notion that transition is just about upfront investment: "I hear too many people saying, 'When you've done the investment, it's free.' I can buy you a castle. When I bought it, it's free. No, you still have to maintain it."

### The 3x2 Matrix: A Framework for Climate Policy

**Wunsch's Mental Model:** Climate transition challenges can be mapped across two dimensions:

**Dimension 1: Cost/Feasibility**
1. **~50% of emissions: No-brainer technologies** - Cost-competitive or cheaper than fossil alternatives
   - EVs (with falling battery costs)
   - Heat pumps for buildings
   - Renewable electricity generation
   - **Cost: Close to zero**

2. **~25% of emissions: Moderate cost** - More expensive than current fossil use but within range of existing carbon prices/fuel taxes
   - Some industrial applications
   - **Cost: ~€100/ton CO2 equivalent**

3. **~15-20% of emissions: Very expensive** - Fundamentally difficult to decarbonize
   - High-temperature industrial heat (>400°C)
   - Chemical industry requiring fossil feedstocks
   - Carbon capture and storage
   - **Cost: >€200-600/ton CO2**

**Dimension 2: Trade Exposure**
- **Tradeable sectors**: Face international competition; unilateral climate action creates competitiveness problems
- **Non-tradeable sectors**: Domestic-focused (heating, transport, local services); can pursue climate policy without competitive disadvantage

**The Trilemma:** For tradeable, hard-to-abate sectors, Europe faces an impossible choice between:
1. **Openness** (free trade)
2. **Single market** (no national subsidies)
3. **Climate ambition**

"You cannot have the three," Wunsch argues. Current approach relies on national/regional subsidies, "damaging the single market."

**Strategic Implication:** "If we do all the rest in Europe, we'll be at 1% of worldwide emissions. The last mile will be part of political discussion. I don't even care so much because if we are to 1% of the emission, the problem is with the rest of the world."

### Central Banks and Climate: The Independence Question

**The Controversy:** When Wunsch was reappointed as Governor, NGOs and Positive Money Europe claimed he was "not equipped to meet the challenges of climate change."

**The Real Issue:** Not his climate commitment (he fully supports net zero 2050) but his position that **central banks should not do climate policy**.

**Wunsch's Argument:**
- Central bank independence is "a privilege that you have" that must be "deserved on a day-by-day basis"
- Independent central banks should "stick to a very strict mandate and not do policy stuff"
- Risk analysis is appropriate; tilting monetary policy instruments toward climate is not
- **The consistency test**: "Should we tilt our portfolio to support the defense industry? Nobody's asking for that."

**Geographic Divide:** 
- **US Federal Reserve**: Clear consensus that climate policy isn't the central bank's job
- **European Central Bank**: More ambiguous, with Christine Lagarde suggesting central banks should "tilt the playing field for climate"
- **Dissenting voices in Europe**: Only Wunsch and Jens Weidmann (former Bundesbank president)

**The Politicization Risk:** When issues become polarized (as climate has), central bank involvement puts the institution "in one camp," threatening its independence and effectiveness.

**Wunsch's Position:** "If you want the central banks to do this stuff, give them a clear mandate" through democratic processes, not through independent central bank discretion.

### Climate Risk Scenarios: The NGFS Problem

**The Framework:** The Network for Greening the Financial System (NGFS) developed climate risk scenarios for financial stability analysis, including:
- **Orderly transition**: €600/ton carbon price
- **Disorderly transition**: €1,000/ton carbon price (equivalent to $300-450/barrel oil)

**Wunsch's Critique:** These scenarios assume politicians will "suddenly impose an oil price of 300 to 450 dollar per barrel" when they realize climate action is insufficient. "This is not going to happen."

**The Real Problem:** Even the "orderly" €600 carbon price is unrealistic. Current EU ETS price is ~€100, and Wunsch considers anything over €200 "politically probably impossible."

**Institutional Bias:** Wunsch observes that climate institutions have "aligned ourselves to the highest bidder on the climate impact front very quickly" - choosing the most dramatic scenarios as references, partly in reaction to Trump's "hoax" claims.

**The Polarization Trap:** "The more Trump says one thing, the more we'll be digging and looking at the one paper that is the most dramatic, and this becomes the reference." This moves institutions away from the "sensible center."

**Example:** An unpublished Nature paper with dramatic climate impact projections became the reference for NGFS, Belgium's planning bureau, and other institutions.

### Europe's Narrative Crisis

**The Failed Narratives:**

1. **"Great Opportunity" (2015-2023)**: Climate transition presented as economic boom
   - Based on macro models focused on investment volumes
   - Ignored that investments would "not increase productivity, but actually decrease it"
   - Sold as "Option A: invest in green tech and save the planet; Option B: miss opportunity and don't save planet"
   - **Verdict**: "We were too optimistic... not honest with ourselves"

2. **"Project Fear" (emerging)**: Collapse scenarios and extreme climate impacts
   - Based on worst-case scenarios and tipping points
   - Wunsch: "Tipping points... unfold in decades if not 100 years"
   - Doesn't maintain public support
   - **Verdict**: Also ineffective

**The Current Mess:** "Death by a thousand cuts" - politicians maintain net zero commitments but reject every policy instrument:
- "I'm fine with the 2050, but not with that. And also not with that. And also not with that."
- Result: "Target without instruments"

**What's Needed:** A "climate reset" - honest discussion of costs, credible instruments, and maintained public support through the "sensible center."

## 3. Frameworks & Mental Models

### The 3x2 Climate Policy Matrix

**Application:**
1. **Identify the sector** along cost dimension (no-brainer/moderate/expensive)
2. **Assess trade exposure** (tradeable/non-tradeable)
3. **Select appropriate instruments:**
   - No-brainer + non-tradeable = minimal intervention needed
   - Moderate cost + non-tradeable = carbon pricing + standards
   - Expensive + tradeable = requires subsidies, R&D support, infrastructure investment, possibly border adjustments
   - Expensive + non-tradeable = public support for first-mover technologies

**Key Insight:** "We can probably green the economy 50% for a cost that's close to zero... maybe 25% for which it costs something but it's around the price we're already paying for fossil fuel... Then if the cost is over a hundred euro per ton, you cannot use heat pumps for industry... That's probably the 15, 20% that is very, very difficult to do."

### Carbon Price Credibility vs. Effectiveness

**The Theory:** Carbon pricing is the most "elegant" and "efficient" instrument because it lets markets find least-cost abatement.

**The Reality Check (Liebreich's challenge):** 
- Carbon prices work for **dispatch decisions** (which plant to run today)
- They fail for **investment decisions** (which plant to build for 20+ years)
- **Why**: Investment committees and bankers "discount carbon price" heavily when evaluating long-term projects
- Banks "don't lend against the forward curve of carbon prices"

**Wunsch's Refinement:** "We can agree if we say a **credible carbon price**" - but political credibility is the binding constraint.

**The Volume vs. Price Dilemma:**
- EU chose emissions trading (volume target) because carbon tax requires unanimity
- Volume targeting creates price volatility
- Price stabilization makes volume the "adjustment variable"
- But legally, EU is committed to net zero by 2050 (volume target)
- **Result**: Fundamental tension in the architecture

**Practical Approach:** 
- Carbon price as "the wave that is going to move all the boats"
- Layer additional instruments for expensive technologies: subsidies, R&D, infrastructure support, risk analysis
- Accept that "you cannot do it all with a carbon price"

### The Tradeable/Non-Tradeable Distinction

**Critical Insight:** "We need to distinguish between tradeable and non-tradeable... one of the lesson of the last two or three years."

**Tradeable Sectors:**
- Energy-intensive industries in global competition
- **Challenge**: Unilateral climate action creates competitive disadvantage
- **Current situation**: Structurally uncompetitive if moving to expensive technologies while competitors don't
- **Example**: If EU targets net zero for industry by 2040 while US is "out of Paris" and others target 2060-2070

**Non-Tradeable Sectors:**
- Heating homes, personal transport, domestic services
- **Advantage**: "A political choice that you have to make within your own electoral system"
- **Technologies available**: Relatively cheap (heat pumps, EVs, renewable electricity)

**Policy Implication:** The political battle is concentrated in tradeable sectors (though only ~15% of emissions), creating "a clear choice between the economy and climate" that risks "throwing the baby with the bathwater."

### Adaptive Pathways / Real Options Thinking

**Liebreich's Proposal:** Rather than committing to extreme scenarios or dismissing them, build **flexibility and option value** into the system.

**The Approach:**
- Assume median climate outcome as base case
- Invest in monitoring and information systems
- Build in flexibility to accelerate if needed
- Review every 5-10 years against new information
- Avoid locking into irreversible paths

**The Political Challenge:** "Politicians find that they kind of say, 'Well, that's great in theory, but how the hell do you communicate that to people?'" They want simple narratives, not nuanced option strategies.

**Wunsch's Alignment:** Focus on "what can be done relatively easily and is going to have an impact over the coming years" while maintaining flexibility for harder challenges.

## 4. Contrarian Takes

### "Team Transitory" Was Wrong - And We Need to Learn From It

**The Consensus View (2021):** Supply shocks like energy price spikes should be "looked through" - don't raise interest rates because inflation will be temporary.

**Wunsch's Contrarian Position:** This was a mistake, and central banks are now "revisiting" this consensus. The fact that inflation went from 2% to 10% while central banks waited shows the danger of assuming energy shocks are always transitory.

**Reasoning:** 
- Energy shocks can persist long enough to trigger second-round effects
- Combined with fiscal stimulus, they can embed in broader inflation
- The "new consensus" that emerged after the 1970s-80s was wrong

**Current Implication:** If Gulf crisis energy prices persist, rate hikes are back on the table despite being a "supply shock."

### Climate Transition Reduces Productivity (In Some Sectors)

**The Consensus Narrative:** Climate transition is an investment boom that will boost GDP.

**Wunsch's Contrarian Take:** Many transition investments "would not increase productivity, but actually decrease it" because they involve "switching to more expensive technologies."

**Examples:**
- Switching from €20/MWh gas to electricity for industrial heat
- Carbon capture reduces efficiency of energy use
- High-temperature industrial processes have no cheap alternatives

**Reasoning:** Macro models focused on investment volumes missed that these investments replace cheaper technologies with more expensive ones - a productivity loss, not gain.

### The Last 15% Doesn't Matter (For Europe)

**The Consensus:** Every ton of emissions matters; Europe must reach absolute net zero.

**Wunsch's Contrarian Position:** "If we do all the rest in Europe, we'll be at 1% of worldwide emissions... I don't even care so much because if we are to 1% of the emission, the problem is with the rest of the world."

**Reasoning:**
- The hardest 15-20% of emissions are extremely expensive to abate
- They're concentrated in tradeable sectors where unilateral action destroys competitiveness
- Europe's total emissions are small globally
- **Better strategy**: Focus on the 80-85% that's feasible, demonstrate technologies, avoid political backlash

**Implication:** The 2040 net zero target for European industry is "optimistic" and may need to become 2050 or 2060.

### Central Banks Shouldn't Do Climate Policy

**The European Consensus (Lagarde, etc.):** Central banks should "tilt the playing field for climate" and support green transition.

**Wunsch's Contrarian Position:** This threatens central bank independence and isn't the institution's role.

**Reasoning:**
- Independence must be "deserved on a day-by-day basis"
- When issues are polarized, central bank involvement puts institution "in one camp"
- **The consistency test**: "Should we tilt our portfolio to support the defense industry? Nobody's asking for that."
- If society wants central banks to do climate policy, "give them a clear mandate" through democratic processes

**Supporting Evidence:** US Federal Reserve maintains clear boundaries; only works in Europe because of different institutional culture.

### Carbon Pricing Alone Won't Work

**The Economic Consensus:** Carbon pricing is the most efficient climate policy instrument.

**Wunsch's Contrarian Take:** "You cannot do it all with a carbon price" - need layered instruments.

**Reasoning:**
- Marginal abatement technology (direct air capture) is so expensive it would require politically impossible carbon prices
- For expensive technologies, need subsidies, R&D support, infrastructure investment
- Carbon price is "the wave that moves all boats" but insufficient alone

**Liebreich's Addition:** Carbon prices aren't credible for long-term investment decisions because they're heavily discounted by investors and lenders.

### The NGFS Climate Scenarios Are Unrealistic

**The Institutional Consensus:** NGFS scenarios (€600-1,000/ton carbon prices) represent plausible climate policy risks.

**Wunsch's Contrarian Position:** "This is not going to happen" - scenarios assume politicians will suddenly impose catastrophic energy costs.

**Reasoning:**
- Current EU carbon price is ~€100/ton
- Anything over €200 is "politically probably impossible"
- €600-1,000 scenarios equivalent to $300-450/barrel oil
- No democratic political system would impose such costs

**Implication:** Financial stability analysis based on these scenarios overstates climate policy risk while potentially understating physical and transition risks.

## 5. Practical Implications

### For Markets/Investing

**Energy Price Volatility Remains Underpriced:**
- Markets still don't fully account for geopolitical energy shock risks
- "One oil and gas price spike costs the country more than the net zero transition"
- **Implication**: Clean energy investments have hidden value as volatility hedge

**Carbon Price Trajectory Is Uncertain:**
- Forward curves showing €200-250/ton are not credible
- Political pressure will likely cap or stabilize prices below theoretical levels
- **Implication**: Don't base long-term investment decisions solely on carbon price forecasts

**Sector-Specific Opportunities:**
- **No-brainer technologies** (50% of emissions): EVs, heat pumps, renewables - minimal policy risk, increasingly cost-competitive
- **Moderate cost** (25% of emissions): Some policy support needed but feasible
- **Expensive technologies** (15-20%): High policy risk, requires sustained subsidies, may not happen in Europe

**The Subsidy Dependency Problem:**
- European industrial decarbonization increasingly relies on national/regional subsidies
- This "damages the single market" and creates uneven playing field
- **Implication**: Investment decisions must account for subsidy availability and political sustainability

**Trade-Exposed Sectors Face Existential Questions:**
- Energy-intensive industries in Europe face "structural" competitiveness problems
- Without global coordination, these sectors may relocate
- **Implication**: Long-term investments in European heavy industry carry significant policy and relocation risk

### For Understanding Macro

**Energy Shocks Are Back on the Monetary Policy Menu:**
- Central banks are abandoning "look through" approach to energy price spikes
- If current Gulf crisis persists 1-2 months, rate hikes become possible
- **Implication**: Energy price movements now have more direct transmission to interest rates

**The 3.5% GDP Cost Is Manageable:**
