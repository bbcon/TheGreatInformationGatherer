# How China Became an Energy Superpower | Ep262: Professor Ning Li

## 1. Episode Overview

**Guest Background:** Professor Ning Li is Dean of the School of Energy Research at Xiamen University in China. He studied complexity science at UC Santa Barbara, worked at US national laboratories on nuclear technologies, and served on the technical committee for China's first AP1000 nuclear reactors. Notably, **he's credited with coining the term "small modular reactors" (SMRs)** nearly two decades ago.

**Main Topics:** China's energy transition strategy, the impact of Middle East conflicts on energy security, the rise of electrification as economic development strategy, nuclear power prospects, and US-China technology cooperation.

**Key Thesis:** China has become the world's first "electro-state" not through central planning alone, but through embracing **modularity, efficiency, and rapid learning** across multiple technologies simultaneously. This approach has created structural efficiency gains that far exceed incremental improvements.

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### China's Energy Security Response to Middle East Crisis

**The Question:** How has China weathered the Strait of Hormuz closure when it's a major oil importer?

**Guest's Perspective:** Li explains that China has been preparing for this vulnerability "for quite a while back." The impact was initially "quite a scare" but turned out to be minimal because:

- **Diversified supply chains** reduced dependence on Middle Eastern oil
- **Strategic petroleum reserves** provided buffer capacity
- **Domestic reduction in petroleum reliance** as fuel source over several years
- Rapid **electrification of transport and industry** decreased oil dependency

**Key Insight:** The contrast with other Asian nations was stark. Japan, South Korea, and Vietnam faced factory shutdowns and work-from-home mandates to save fuel, while China's economy continued largely unaffected. Li notes: "China has sort of diversified its supply and domestically beginning to reduce reliance on petroleum as a fuel source or energy source from several years ago."

### The "New Three Exports" and Electrification Boom

**The Framing:** China's export growth is now driven by what's called the "new three exports": solar panels, lithium batteries, and electric vehicles.

**Key Mechanisms Explained:**

Li describes how **modularity enables super-linear scaling**: "Technologies especially since the mid 1980s to 90s they become more and more modular. And the modular technologies just can scale faster."

The growth has been extraordinary:
- China added **over 260 GW of solar and wind in one year** (more than most countries' total installed capacity)
- EV exports **more than doubled** in March 2025 following the Middle East crisis
- Solar and battery exports nearly doubled simultaneously
- Over **50% of new car sales** in China are now electric or hybrid

**Supporting Evidence:** Li explains the demand spike: "All of a sudden people sort of the shock or the crisis shocked people out of the comfort zone...suddenly start realizing using these systems you have more reliability and security and energy security fundamentally is the basis of national security."

### Structural vs. Incremental Efficiency Gains

**The Framework:** Li distinguishes between incremental efficiency improvements (adding insulation, slightly better appliances) and **structural efficiency gains from adopting new technology systems**.

**Key Insight:** "When you think about efficiency improvement people often think in the incremental ways...but there are structural efficiency gains...going from heating from whatever you use to heat pump the efficiency jumped not by like 10% or 20% by several order several folds."

**Examples Given:**
- **Heat pumps:** Coefficient of Performance (COP) of 3-5 means one unit of electricity moves 3-5 units of heat (vs. 100% efficiency of direct electric heating)
- **Electric vehicles:** Well-to-wheel efficiency is **double that of ICE vehicles** (~40% vs ~20%); renewable-to-wheel is **90% vs 20%**
- These represent **step-change improvements**, not marginal gains

**Application:** Li argues: "Why would people fight against these kind of dramatic improvement when you fight for like a one or two percentage point of improvement in incremental? When you have such a step improvement options in place sticking with incrementalism is a losing proposition."

### Managing the Coal Transition

**The Question:** How is China managing its continued coal capacity while adding massive renewables?

**Guest's Perspective:** China's approach is **"build the new before dismantling the old"** to avoid disruption.

**Key Mechanisms:**
- Coal's share dropped from **over 80% to about 50%** of power generation
- Coal plants now operate **under 4,000 hours/year** (vs. designed 5,500 hours), some approaching 3,500 hours
- Coal consumption is **dropping despite plants remaining operational**
- Plants are being **retrofitted for flexibility** and converted to combined heat and power (CHP)

**Supporting Evidence:** Li explains: "Before you dismantle the old you have to build the new first so that you don't have disruptions in your system." He contrasts this with Germany, which "shut down and phased out coal and nuclear before solar, wind and energy storage is really sort of can sustain yourself."

**Practical Application:** Coal plants are increasingly becoming **"grid firmer stabilizer rather than the main source of power supply"** - providing reliability during weeks without sun or wind while renewables handle baseload.

### The Nuclear-Westinghouse Collaboration Story

**The Context:** This example demonstrates successful US-China technology cooperation despite current tensions.

**What Happened:** 
- Westinghouse brought AP1000 reactor design to China around 2005-2006
- Design wasn't finalized when deal was approved
- **Thousands of people went back and forth** between countries
- CEOs and chairmen spent **months on-site** solving problems together
- Main components had to be scaled up and tested through iteration

**Key Outcome:** 
- Before: Only Japan Steel Works could make large reactor vessels globally
- After: **China gained two suppliers** capable of manufacturing these components
- One innovation required **23-24 iterations and millions of dollars** to perfect large-diameter bent pipes without welding
- Result: "AP 1000 is much simplified design is safer...one of the most advanced sort of large water reactor design in the world"

**Li's Reflection:** "That's only capable because the US first invented and then working with China to actually realize it now it's able to scale."

## 3. Frameworks & Mental Models

### The Modularity Framework

**Core Concept:** Li studied why nuclear stagnated while other technologies scaled rapidly, discovering that **modular technologies scale super-linearly** while bespoke, site-built technologies don't.

**Application to Nuclear:** "If we do nuclear the next around we have to do it modular and we have to make many of them and so we make them smaller...smaller nuclear systems are also intrinsically safer because...by design eliminate the possibility of a meltdown."

**Broader Application:** This framework explains China's success across solar, wind, batteries, and EVs - all benefit from factory manufacturing, learning curves, and network effects.

### The "Electro-State" Concept

**Definition:** A nation where **electrification becomes the primary development pathway**, replacing fossil fuel combustion across transport, heating, and industry.

**Characteristics:**
- Abundant, sometimes oversupplied electricity (negative prices for hundreds of hours/year)
- Stable grid despite high renewable penetration
- Electrified transport reducing oil dependency
- Industrial heat transitioning to electric sources

**How It Emerged:** Li notes this wasn't planned: "These kind of ideas and stuff often emerge in the western media...China is sort of more focused on doing things...often times it become a hindsight to study them rather than sort of a foresight to think it through."

### The "Build New Before Dismantling Old" Transition Strategy

**Principle:** Avoid system disruption by ensuring new capacity is operational before retiring legacy infrastructure.

**Application:** 
- Keep coal plants operational but reduce utilization
- Add nuclear for firm power
- Build massive renewable capacity
- Convert coal plants to CHP and flexibility providers
- Only then begin actual coal retirement

**Contrast:** Germany's approach of shutting down coal and nuclear before alternatives were ready created reliability problems.

## 4. Contrarian Takes

### On Energy Dependency Concerns

**Consensus View:** Switching from fossil fuel dependency to Chinese clean tech dependency is dangerous.

**Li's Counter:** "There are many technologies and productions are centralized...Middle East petrol states, automobiles were used to mostly built in America in Europe...airplane is even more highly concentrated...we can work these things out."

**Reasoning:** He points to the **Octopus Energy-BYD partnership** as a model: UK company leases batteries from Chinese EVs for grid storage (V2G), customers drive for free because battery capacity far exceeds driving needs. "Both sides wins...if you break down these silo of barriers...it just opens up this dramatic" opportunity.

**On Security Concerns:** "Communication systems are less sophisticated than our mobile phones. So whatever you do with the mobile phones, you can make the electric vehicle safe as well." He argues treating EVs differently from phones and laptops is inconsistent.

### On Protectionism vs. Efficiency

**Consensus View:** Protect domestic auto/manufacturing jobs through tariffs.

**Li's Nuanced Take:** "Countries with significant...legacy systems do have to protect their industry employment and their assets. So that's not a universal solution to everybody."

**But:** "You while you want to protect the old but don't sort of sunk subsidies and all these other things into them because what you need to invest in is into these newer technologies and newer systems."

**Key Warning:** "If you skew too heavily on the protection side, you'd be left out and then you know a generation or two later you'd be so far behind...you become the underdeveloped."

### On Climate Framing

**Consensus Approach:** Frame climate action as burden-sharing, cost, preservation, existential threat.

**Li's Alternative:** "If you frame a transition or these climate mitigation etc as a cost issue as a preservation issue, it's very difficult to get more people around it."

**Better Framing:** "The solution is how we grow...when China first started doing like environmental protection, it was considered a cost. But over the years, once you started doing realize, you frame it as a development...rather than a mitigation and prevention issue, it suddenly opens up."

**Evidence:** China initially fought climate responsibilities, then realized "growing solar wind the renewable electric vehicle, it's a great economic engine."

### On US Deindustrialization

**Li's Perspective:** "US is probably one of the first country that gone so far in industrialization that it didn't...had to innovate what to do next and unfortunately the financial engineer took over."

**Observation:** "It sort of breaks my heart to think that...you're selling to the rest of world like soy beans and corn...why not other things?" He notes China now exports electronics including semiconductors alongside the "new three."

**Hope:** "There's signs of this being corrected...the AI build out, the new SMRs and all these things, the reshoring."

## 5. Practical Implications

### For Markets/Investing

**Modularity Premium:** Technologies that can be factory-manufactured and benefit from learning curves will scale super-linearly. This explains why:
- Solar, wind, batteries, EVs have sustained **double-digit growth for over two decades** in China
- Nuclear, despite being "best in class" in China, adds only 5-10 GW/year vs. 200-300 GW/year for renewables
- **SMRs could change this** if they achieve true modularity

**The "New Three" Export Boom:** Following the Middle East crisis, demand for Chinese clean tech spiked dramatically. This trend likely continues as countries prioritize **energy security through electrification**.

**Grid Storage Opportunities:** The **Octopus-BYD V2G model** demonstrates how EVs become mobile grid storage. Li notes a typical EV battery stores "almost a week worth of power for a typical family" but the car isn't used constantly - creating arbitrage opportunities.

**Heat Pump Wave:** Li suggests heat pumps could become the **"fifth" major export** (after wind turbines as the fourth). The European heat pump mini-wave after Russia-Ukraine war demonstrated demand potential.

### For Understanding Macro

**China's Resilience:** The Middle East crisis revealed China's **strategic preparation paid off** through:
- Supply diversification
- Strategic reserves
- Structural shift away from oil in domestic economy
- Electrification reducing vulnerability

**Electricity Oversupply:** China now has **negative electricity prices for hundreds of hours/year** in some regions. This creates opportunities for:
- Energy-intensive industries to relocate
- Heat pumps and thermal storage to absorb excess
- Flexible demand response systems

**The Transition Timeline:** Li's framework suggests **protecting legacy industries while investing in new** is politically necessary but economically risky if protection dominates. The window for catching up narrows quickly with super-linear scaling technologies.
