# Is Africa Ready To Become A Clean Energy Powerhouse? | Clemens Calice, Signum Capital

## 1. Episode Overview

**Guest Background:** Clemens Calice is CEO and founder of Signum Capital, an investment bank and financial services firm focused on frontier markets, predominantly Africa. Austrian by background, Calice worked at Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs before founding what became Signum Capital. The firm has approximately 100 professionals (half based in Africa) and manages around **$1.3 billion across seven funds**, primarily focused on renewable energy and energy transition. They've invested in 35 of Africa's 54 countries.

**Main Topics:** The conversation explores why Africa—despite representing 18% of global population—uses less than 4% of global energy and attracts only 1% of global energy investment. With Africa's population projected to grow from 1.5 billion today to 2.5 billion by 2050 (potentially 4 billion by 2100), the continent faces a critical juncture: it can either leapfrog to clean energy infrastructure or replicate the fossil fuel mistakes of developed nations.

**Key Thesis:** Africa presents a unique opportunity to build energy infrastructure correctly from the start, bypassing the legacy fossil fuel systems that developed nations are now struggling to replace. However, **"the most expensive commodity in Africa is time"**—infrastructure development takes too long due to inefficiencies, and this timeline challenge is the primary barrier to capital deployment.

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### The Commercial & Industrial Renewable Energy Boom

**The Question:** What specific opportunities is Signum pursuing in Africa's energy transition?

**Calice's Perspective:** The **commercial and industrial (C&I) renewable energy market** has "really taken off." This involves solar installations on factory rooftops or dedicated solar plants serving specific industrial customers like mines or manufacturing facilities. With solar panel prices dropping rapidly and battery costs following suit, these solutions are now highly competitive and can be deployed in small or large quantities in remote locations.

**Key Mechanism:** Most industrial companies aren't in the business of generating electricity—they prefer third-party providers. Companies like **Crossboundary Energy** (which Signum finances) sign power purchase agreements with industrial clients, then Signum provides financing for the solar infrastructure. Projects have evolved from hundreds of thousands of dollars for smaller sites to **hundreds of millions for mine electrification**, replacing diesel and heavy fuel oil generation.

**Supporting Example:** Calice highlights electric two-wheelers (motorbikes) as another rapid transition area. These vehicles are adapted for African conditions (heavier loads, more passengers, worse roads) and eliminate the entire logistics chain of moving fuel from ports to inland countries. In Ethiopia, **60% of new cars purchased are electric**—an outlier but demonstrating the leapfrog potential.

### The Leapfrog Opportunity vs. Colonial Baggage

**The Framing:** Host Michael Liebreich notes the term "leapfrog" should be central to climate negotiations for Africa—helping countries jump directly to superior systems (like East Germany going straight to mobile phones, skipping landlines). Yet debates often focus on what Africa *shouldn't* do rather than enabling better alternatives.

**Calice's View:** Africans care about **development at home**, not Europe's problems. Africa contributes minimally to global CO2 emissions. The continent needs power and mobility efficiently delivered. The European transmission model (large centralized power stations connected to distant consumers) doesn't suit Africa—**decentralized generation wherever there's sun** makes more sense from the start.

**Key Insight:** Unlike Europe, which is "trying to fix a problem we created 100 years ago," Africa can build infrastructure that's "fit for the future" from day one. The continent needs massive infrastructure buildout anyway—it should be constructed with a forward-looking, carbon-neutral perspective.

**Contrarian Element:** Calice pushes back against both extremes—the extractive resource view of Africa AND the patronizing development/climate view that prohibits all hydrocarbons. He advocates for **pragmatism**: some natural gas may be necessary for economic resilience and grid stability, even as renewables form the generation basis.

### The Geography and Fragmentation Challenge

**The Context:** Africa's diversity is staggering—54 countries with a combined GDP of ~$3 trillion (roughly Italy's size) spread across massive distances. The distance from Nairobi to Dakar (east to west) equals Moscow to Vladivostok. Tanzania alone is the size of France, Germany, and Benelux combined. Yet many West African nations (Togo, Benin, Guinea-Bissau, Gambia) are tiny.

**Calice's Framework:** The continent used to be viewed as "two ends"—North Africa (reasonably understood by Europeans) and South Africa (a developed BRICS nation)—with empty space between. **This has shifted over the last 10-15 years.** The middle—Nigeria (220 million people), Ethiopia (170 million)—is where growth is concentrated. Africa is no longer just bookends but a continent of diverse opportunities.

**Practical Implication:** Signum exists precisely to "construct bridges between the outside world and Africa and within Africa" because the fragmentation creates complexity that deters large asset managers. Individual markets are too small for pan-African scale, yet each requires understanding different legal regimes, compliance requirements, and local conditions.

### The Capital Allocation Problem

**The Question:** Why isn't Africa attracting more capital given fantastic wind/solar resources, abundant land, and low current penetration?

**Calice's Analysis:** The primary issue is **infrastructure development takes too long**. "The most expensive power you can have is the power you don't have." Various inefficiencies slow project timelines, making the opportunity less attractive to investors despite strong fundamentals.

**The Asset Manager Paradox:** Even if you convince a European asset manager (managing €1 trillion) that African returns are "fabulous," they face structural barriers:
- Investment opportunities are small by global standards and distributed across 54 countries
- Deploying even $100 million takes extensive time
- They lack local presence and understanding of legal systems
- Compliance requirements are complex
- **They can't deploy enough capital to justify the effort**

**Signum's Solution:** Bundle dispersed opportunities (individually quite small), perform de-risking, and create vehicles allowing larger investment flows. They've raised over $1 billion in the last 12 months alone—half from institutional investors, representing a "dramatic rise in activity."

### Risk Perception vs. Reality

**The Misperception:** The finance world's "first instinct" is that Africa is "very risky." While risk exists, **it's generally overstated**—recent data on default rates (especially infrastructure) substantiates this. Many places are risky; Africa isn't uniquely so.

**The Real Barriers:** Non-financial obstacles create perceived risk:
- Time required to understand 54 different legal regimes
- Macro shocks, government changes, military coups
- Currency/foreign exchange risk
- Getting money out again (repatriation)

**Key Insight from Liebreich:** Lucy Heights (Actis) and Daniel Calderon (Alcazar Energy) made the point in previous episodes that **power infrastructure doesn't disappear**. If a country needs electricity and has high domestic prices (often set by imported fuel), they need the asset. It will continue producing and requires payment. The asset maintains value even through political instability.

**Calice's Addition:** Traditional European financing models don't fit. Africa needs new financial instruments to properly assess and price infrastructure risk, recognizing that a solar plant won't vanish even if other risks materialize.

### The Multilateral Evolution

**Historical Context:** Five years ago, **80% of infrastructure capital came from multilaterals** (World Bank, African Development Bank, European development banks) with minimal institutional/private capital.

**The Shift:** This is changing as multilaterals and institutional investors **work together in new ways**. Multilaterals provide stability that creates pathways for private sector co-investment. In the last 12 months, Signum raised over $1 billion—**half from institutional money** (insurance companies, pension funds).

**Local Capital Formation:** Importantly, **Pan-African banks and local pension funds/insurance companies** are taking keener interest in infrastructure that "really should be owned by them and not by European investors." South African banks, previously focused only domestically, now participate in most deals across the continent with "reasonably sizable tickets."

**The Challenge:** Individual markets remain too small. A bank serving one country is like serving "one Italian province"—it can't be globally competitive. What's needed is **pan-African capital** that can diversify across countries, but regulations still restrict local banks from investing outside borders, and pension fund rules remain tight.

### The Hydrogen Distraction

**Liebreich's Contrarian Take:** Green hydrogen projects in Africa (particularly Namibia's strategy) represent a "smoking crater." For local African fertilizer demand, green ammonia will cost twice as much as fossil-based alternatives. Export to Europe only works if carbon prices justify it—which they don't. **"Almost none of those projects will happen."**

**Calice's Nuanced Response:** While agreeing green hydrogen isn't a "panacea for stopping climate change," certain pockets (like Namibia) merit discussion. More importantly, the focus should be on **value addition to natural resources**—processing copper or other minerals locally rather than shipping raw materials for offshore treatment.

**The Employment Imperative:** With an average age of 19 and staggering numbers entering working age annually, Africa needs **large-scale manufacturing** to employ people. "Not everybody can be an influencer or engaged in the gig economy." This requires cheap electricity—where renewables and development goals converge.

## 3. Frameworks & Mental Models

### The "Time is the Most Expensive Commodity" Framework

**The Model:** In infrastructure development, particularly in Africa, **time costs more than capital**. "The most expensive power you can have is the power you don't have." Long development timelines compound costs, create uncertainty, and deter investment.

**Application:** Prioritize solutions that can be deployed quickly:
- Decentralized solar (can be installed anywhere with sun, no long transmission buildout)
- Modular approaches (C&I solar scales from small to large)
- Standardized structures (reducing time spent on legal/compliance for each deal)
- Bundling projects (amortize due diligence costs across multiple investments)

### The Leapfrog vs. Lock-in Framework

**The Model:** Developing economies face a choice: replicate developed world mistakes (fossil fuel infrastructure that must later be replaced) or leapfrog to superior systems (clean energy, electric mobility).

**Application:** 
- Build decentralized renewable generation from the start rather than centralized fossil plants
- Deploy electric two-wheelers rather than building out fuel distribution logistics
- Use HVDC transmission for long distances rather than traditional grid architecture
- Combine renewable base generation with flexible gas peaking (not inflexible CCGT baseload)

**Key Principle:** Don't build infrastructure that will be obsolete in 5 years or serve only part of the country. Private capital provides a "checks and balance" against poor government decisions because investors ask hard questions about asset longevity.

### The Bridge-Building Framework

**The Model:** Signum's role is creating **three types of bridges**:
1. Between outside world and Africa (bringing international capital in)
2. Within Africa (connecting fragmented markets)
3. Between multilaterals and private capital (co-investment structures)

**Application:** Success requires:
- Local presence (understanding legal systems, having boots on ground)
- International standards (regulated in UK, Netherlands, etc.)
- Information networks (150 investments across continent = phone call access everywhere)
- Bundling capabilities (taking small dispersed opportunities, packaging for larger flows)
- De-risking mechanisms (structures that address specific investor concerns)

### The Pragmatic Energy Mix Framework

**The Model:** Reject both extremes—pure extractive fossil development AND absolute prohibition of hydrocarbons. Instead, pursue **economic resilience through pragmatic energy choices**.

**Application:**
- Renewable energy as generation basis (solar/wind where resources exist)
- Natural gas for grid stability and flexibility (not large inflexible CCGT baseload)
- Repowering existing heavy fuel oil generation with gas (West Africa)
- Private sector participation as check on government overreach
- Recognition that different regions need different solutions (East Africa ≠ West Africa ≠ South Africa)

**Calice's Principle:** "Climate resilience only makes sense if you also create economic resilience. These countries need to continue to grow."

## 4. Contrarian Takes

### Risk is Overstated, But That's Not the Real Problem

**The Consensus:** Africa is too risky for institutional capital; high default rates and political instability make it unsuitable for large-scale investment.

**Calice's Challenge:** Risk perception doesn't match reality—recent infrastructure default data proves this. But even if you convince investors returns are "fabulous," they still won't deploy capital because:
- **Deal sizes are too small** for trillion-euro asset managers
- **Ecosystem doesn't exist** (lawyers, consultants, insurance)
- **Compliance burden is high** relative to capital deployed
- **They can't move enough money** to justify the effort

**Implication:** The problem isn't risk—it's **infrastructure for capital deployment**. You need intermediaries like Signum to bundle opportunities and create vehicles for larger flows.

### Europe's Defensive Africa Narrative is Self-Defeating

**The Consensus:** European engagement with Africa should focus on development assistance, preventing migration, and climate obligations—essentially defensive postures driven by guilt over colonial past or fear of immigration.

**Calice's Challenge:** This "schizophrenia" prevents Europe from articulating a **win-win narrative**. Europe already has a private sector base in Africa. If built upon, it "could be a growth engine for many years to come." 

**The Missed Opportunity:** Europe faces low growth, demographic problems, and high environmental regulations making manufacturing difficult. **"Just head south"**—enormous opportunities, huge numbers of young people, dynamism and entrepreneurship. Developing Africa "aligned with a European model" safeguards Europe's own future.

**Calice's Frustration:** "There's just not enough effort. People are too distracted and politicians are in general too much on the back foot." The question should be **"what do you want us to do?"** rather than imposing solutions, then evaluating through the lens of European interests.

### The Baku COP Agreement Was Misguided

**Liebreich's Critique:** European environment and climate ministers (few heads of state or finance ministers attended) signed documents committing €300 billion annually to countries that will be **Europe's economic competitors**—without a vision of what Europe is trying to achieve beyond climate metrics.

**The Contrarian View:** This represents seeing development "just through a climate lens" rather than understanding **multiple European goals**: solving core European problems while helping Africa develop in mutually beneficial ways. It's neither defensive ("stop them coming to Europe") nor purely altruistic—it's strategic economic partnership.

### Green Hydrogen is a Distraction from Real Opportunities

**The Hype:** Green hydrogen will enable Africa to become a clean energy powerhouse, with countries like Namibia building entire strategies around it.

**Liebreich's Blunt Assessment:** "Almost none of those projects will happen." Green fertilizer for local African demand costs twice as much as fossil-based alternatives. Export to Europe only works with higher carbon prices than currently exist. Namibia's roadside signs about hydrogen futures represent a **"smoking crater of a strategy."**

**The Better Focus:** Value addition to natural resources (processing minerals locally), large-scale manufacturing for employment, and cheap electricity from renewables—not speculative hydrogen export schemes.

## 5. Practical Implications

### For Investors

**Market Sizing:** Africa's economy (~$3 trillion, size of Italy) is growing 3-4% annually but should grow at 7%. This means **50-100% growth over a decade**. Financial institutions should grow faster than underlying economic growth—Signum targets 20-25% annual growth.

**Entry Strategy:** 
- Don't try to deploy capital directly across 54 countries
- Partner with intermediaries who have local presence and deal flow
- Co-invest with multilaterals for stability
- Focus on infrastructure (particularly renewable energy) where default rates are lower than perceived
- Consider Pan-African funds that bundle opportunities

**Specific Opportunities:**
- **C&I solar:** Hundreds of thousands to hundreds of millions per project
- **Mine electrification:** Replacing diesel/HFO with solar + batteries
- **Electric two-wheelers:** Transport electrification happening rapidly
- **Power purchase agreements:** Third-party providers to industrial customers
- **Local banks/pension funds:** Increasingly active, creating co-investment opportunities

### For Understanding Macro Trends

**Demographic Imperative:** Average age in Africa is 19. Massive numbers entering working age annually require **large-scale manufacturing employment**—not gig economy or influencer jobs. This demands cheap, reliable electricity.

**The Decentralization Shift:** Unlike developed world grid architecture (centralized generation, long transmission), Africa is building **decentralized generation** from the start. Wherever there's sun, you can put solar. This fundamentally different model has implications for:
- Transmission infrastructure needs (less critical initially)
- Grid stability solutions (batteries, flexible gas)
- Speed of deployment (no waiting for transmission buildout)

**Capital Flow Evolution:** The 80/20 split (multilateral/private) is shifting toward 50/50. **Local capital formation** is accelerating—South African banks going Pan-African, pension funds investing in infrastructure. This creates a more sustainable, locally-owned development model.

**Technology Leapfrogging:** Mobile phones bypassed landlines. Electric vehicles (especially two-wheelers) are bypassing fuel distribution infrastructure. **HVDC transmission** enables long-distance power movement, potentially connecting North African solar to both Europe and rest of Africa.

### For Policy and Development

**Time Reduction is Critical:** Every initiative should ask: "Does this shorten infrastructure development timelines?" Regulatory streamlining, standardized contracts, and efficient permitting matter more than marginal capital cost reductions.

**Avoid Ideological Extremes:**
- Don't prohibit all hydrocarbons (some gas needed for stability/economic resilience)
- Don't default to fossil baseload (inflexible CCGT plants will become stranded assets)
- Don't impose European preferences (let African demand drive solutions)
- Don't view through purely climate lens (economic development is equally critical)

**Enable Pan-African Capital:** 
- Relax regulations restricting local banks from cross-border investment
- Liberalize pension fund rules to allow infrastructure investment
- Support African free trade zone development
- Build legal/regulatory harmonization across countries

**The European Opportunity:** Rather than defensive postures (preventing migration, colonial guilt, climate obligations), articulate **mutual benefit**: African development aligned with European partnership creates growth engine for both. Ask "what do you want us to do?" then evaluate if it serves European interests.

### Actionable Takeaways

**For Institutional Investors:**
1. Allocate to Africa-focused funds with local presence (don't try direct investment)
2. Co-invest with multilaterals for de-risking
3. Focus on infrastructure/renewable energy (better risk profile than perceived)
4. Partner with Pan-African banks increasingly active in deals
5. Accept smaller initial allocations to build track record and understanding

**For Companies:**
1. Industrial facilities: Consider third-party solar PPAs rather than grid dependence or self-generation
2. Mining operations: Solar + battery can replace diesel at competitive cost
3. Manufacturing: Locate in Africa for young labor force, but ensure reliable power supply
4. Technology providers: Adapt solutions for African conditions (heavier loads, worse roads, remote locations)

**For Development Organizations:**
1. Prioritize timeline reduction over marginal cost improvements
2. Create standardized structures to reduce transaction costs
3. Enable co-investment between multilaterals and private capital
4. Support local capital market development (banks, pension funds, insurance)
5. Focus on economic resilience, not just climate resilience

**For Policymakers:**
1. Streamline permitting and regulatory processes
2. Allow private sector participation in power generation
3. Avoid locking in inflexible fossil infrastructure
4. Enable cross-border capital flows within Africa
5. Articulate win-win narratives rather than defensive postures
