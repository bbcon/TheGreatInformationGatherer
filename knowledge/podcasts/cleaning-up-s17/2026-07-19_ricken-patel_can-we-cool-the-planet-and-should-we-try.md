# Can We Cool The Planet, And Should We Try? | Ricken Patel

## 1. Episode Overview

**Guest Background:** Ricken Patel is a lifelong activist and founder/former CEO of Avaaz, the online activist platform that grew to 70+ million members. A Canadian with international roots (family from Kenya), Patel spent 15 years building Avaaz before shifting focus to defending democracy and promoting public research into climate stabilization technologies.

**Main Topics:** The conversation explores Patel's journey from campus activism to building global movements, the current state of climate risk, and the controversial topic of geoengineering/climate intervention. The central thesis: **we're terraforming the planet whether we acknowledge it or not—the question is whether we do so thoughtfully or recklessly.**

**Key Framing:** Patel argues we face a double standard where corporations can "geoengineer the crap out of the planet" for profit without scrutiny, but any intentional intervention to protect the climate faces intense opposition. This paradox must be resolved as climate risks accelerate beyond what mitigation alone can address.

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### The Making of an Activist

**Patel's Origin Story:** From childhood, Patel felt "a sense of responsibility for the way the world is." Early campaigns included opposing tuition fees at Oxford (nearly getting expelled for occupying university offices) and leading Harvard's living wage campaign (occupying the president's office for 3 weeks).

**Key Insight:** These experiences gave Patel **"a sense of agency"**—the understanding that "you don't have to accept the world the way it appears, that you can have agency over changing it." This realization that the world is "the product of a vector sum of all the influences of all of us" became foundational to his activism.

**The 90% Rule:** From working in war zones (Sierra Leone, Liberia, Sudan, Afghanistan), Patel developed the principle that **"90% of politics is who shows up."** Decision-makers are "just people" influenced by media coverage, constituent pressure, and personal relationships—not untouchable elites.

### Building Avaaz: Democracy at Scale

**The Core Idea:** Avaaz was built on the premise that "there's a world that most people everywhere want" when you examine opinion polls—we're less divided than media suggests. The platform used email technology to unite people across borders and political divides.

**The Model:** Petitions brought in members, but the "magic mix" was hiring world-class advocates (White House staff, presidential deputies) who were "subservient to a democratic discipline." Members made final campaign decisions through testing with randomized samples of ~20,000 people.

**Addressing "Clicktivism" Criticism:** Patel dismisses early skepticism about online activism: "Who now has any degree of seriousness will sit back and say everything on the internet is not real, never has any impact?" Avaaz raised **half a billion dollars**, organized the largest climate marches in history, and ran 2,500 campaigns over 15 years.

**The Disinformation Battle:** Avaaz pioneered a **corrections model** for Facebook to combat fake news, which Facebook implemented across platforms. The approach worked—2020 saw far less disinformation than 2016. However, Patel notes Facebook has since repealed these measures, partly because "fact-checkers kind of sold us out" by becoming ideologically biased.

### Climate Activism and the Paris Agreement

**Top Achievement:** Patel identifies the Paris Climate Agreement as Avaaz's signature win. They were active in all pre-Paris COPs, G7 meetings, and helped establish the concept of a **"North Star" temperature target** to make climate goals comprehensible to the public.

**The 1.5°C Story:** Patel clarifies Avaaz initially supported 2°C, not 1.5°C. The 1.5°C target emerged from small island nations arguing "are you going to save the least of us?" whose agriculture would be destroyed at 2°C. Paris "fudged it" with "1.5 to 2."

**Context Matters:** In 2013-2014, climate ambassadors from G7 countries told Patel climate "isn't an issue. The public does not care." After Copenhagen's failure, the movement was demoralized. **Simple, inspiring goals were needed to mobilize political will**, even if imperfect.

**The Real Paris Victory:** Patel argues Paris succeeded not through binding governance but as **"an enthusiasm rally"** that sent market signals driving technological change. The shift from top-down allocation (Kyoto) to bottom-up nationally determined contributions (NDCs) was "wise architecture for governance for the times."

### The Parasol Problem: New Climate Risks

**Three Compounding Factors:**

1. **Despite progress, we're not on track** to avoid catastrophic climate change
2. **We're losing Earth's reflectivity** through multiple mechanisms
3. **Tipping points threaten civilization-ending scenarios**

**The Reflectivity Crisis:** Patel highlights that as we clean up traditional pollutants for air quality, we're inadvertently removing a "global sun shield":

- **Cloud loss:** Earth may have lost up to 5% of cloud cover over 30 years—the radiative forcing equivalent to "all of human emissions since industrialization"
- **Shipping emissions:** Removing sulfur from heavy fuel oil eliminated particulates that seeded cloud formation over oceans, creating a "termination shock"
- **The gas problem:** Switching to "clean" natural gas added methane emissions (high short-term warming) while removing cooling particulates—"the worst possible thing we could have done"

**Tipping Point Risks:**

- **AMOC (Atlantic Meridional Overturning Current):** If this ocean current critically slows, Europe could drop 8-10°C while a billion people in Africa/India/Asia lose half their rainfall, likely causing Amazon rainforest dieback
- **Thwaites Glacier:** Antarctic ice shelf displacement could raise sea levels 3 feet
- **Feedback loops:** Temperature-cloud interactions could make Earth far more sensitive to warming than IPCC projects

**The Heat Dome Wake-Up:** Experiencing Canada's deadly heat dome personally shocked Patel: "I did not have that on my climate impact bingo list that we were going to have deaths in Canada due to extreme heat."

### The Geoengineering Double Standard

**The Central Paradox:** Patel's most provocative argument: **"Right now you can just geoengineer the crap out of the planet as long as you do it for your own personal profit."** Corporations can "stick anything you like in the atmosphere or the water or the sea" without oversight—"that's called capitalism and growth."

**The Insane Asymmetry:** "If you replace [sulfur emissions from container ships] with salt water foggers that had the exact same cooling effect, people would be like, 'Geoengineering! Disgusting, dangerous!'" We accept harmful terraforming for profit but condemn potentially beneficial interventions done with good intentions.

**Examples of Unregulated Geoengineering:**
- Ever-larger vehicles (no assessment of accident risk or climate impact)
- Industrial emissions changing atmospheric composition
- Agricultural practices transforming landscapes

**The Moral Imperative:** "We are terraforming this planet in real time... We've got to take responsibility for all of those impacts." Refusing to research climate interventions while allowing destructive ones is "insanity."

### Democracy and Information Ecosystems

**Why Democracy Matters:** Patel shifted Avaaz's focus to democracy because "all the roads to all the changes we wanted to see including climate just ran through democracy." Democracy is "precious and adaptive and fragile"—anomalous in human history.

**The Current Crisis:** Only ~30% of the world lives under democratic government—back to 1989 levels. A "new playbook of authoritarian populists" threatens democracies globally.

**The Disinformation Challenge:** Defining truth is "so subjective," but Patel advocates for **community notes models** over centralized fact-checking. Society needs both curated sources (Apple's approach) and distributed verification, but "I wouldn't want all of our information to be like that cuz so many important views that need to surface start out fringe or radical."

**Historical Perspective:** Indigenous governance and village-level systems often featured "quite deliberative democratic governance" within their constraints. The Indaba technique from South Africa—standing in circles rather than behind podiums—helped break through Paris negotiation deadlocks.

## 3. Frameworks & Mental Models

### The Medicine Analogy

Patel uses his family to illustrate the tension between natural and interventionist approaches: his sister writes about alternative healthcare (naturalistic), his brother is a medical professor (interventionist). **Both poles have wisdom.**

"Listen to your body's rhythms" has value, but "if we didn't have like a C-section kind of thing, my wife and my kids would be dead." The framework: **pragmatic use of intervention when necessary, respect for natural systems when possible.**

**Application to climate:** Don't reflexively oppose all intervention (geoengineering) or embrace unlimited intervention. Evaluate each approach on evidence of safety, justice, and sustainability.

### The Civilization Collapse Framework

Drawing on Jared Diamond's work, Patel thinks about **"non-linear feedback loops between natural and human systems"** that have historically collapsed civilizations (Mayans losing water, Romans deforesting Italy).

**Current risk:** Our "very interconnected, very interdependent, quite fragile civilization" may face shocks it "can't adapt to. It's not that it's theoretically possible we could adapt, it's that we won't."

**Application:** This drives Patel's focus on both making human systems more resilient AND preventing catastrophic shocks through climate intervention research.

### The Agency Principle

**Core belief:** "The world actually isn't just happening to all of us. It actually is the product of a sort of vector sum of all the influences of all of us upon it."

Patel quotes: "Nothing splendid was ever achieved by anyone who didn't believe that something inside themselves was superior to circumstance." Also: "The best way to predict the future is to invent it."

**Application:** This mindset shift from passive to active is "what makes an activist"—experiencing that campaigning leads to change confers "a sense of responsibility."

### The Risk-Based Approach

Rather than certainty-based decision-making, Patel advocates **acknowledging uncertainty and preparing for tail risks:**

"The bands of uncertainty in the IPCC projections are conservative... all the evidence suggests the band is much wider. The earth may heat much more than is projected currently."

**Application:** Even if AMOC collapse or rapid ice sheet loss are uncertain, their civilization-threatening potential demands research into responses. "We better figure out what we're going to do about this."

## 4. Contrarian Takes

### Paris Was About Market Signals, Not Governance

**Contrarian view:** "By Paris we had sort of quietly internally acknowledged that [binding governance] to some degree and said this is not a governance like this is a rally."

Patel admits: "George W. Bush kind of won that one"—it was "largely a market signal that drove technological change that is having the outsized impact on some of our climate outcomes," not the NDCs themselves.

**Reasoning:** The US was never going to accept binding treaties. Rather than fight that reality, Paris succeeded by rallying enthusiasm and signaling to markets that energy transition was coming.

### The Geoengineering Taboo Is Hypocritical

**Contrarian view:** Opposing all geoengineering research while allowing destructive terraforming for profit is morally indefensible.

**Reasoning:** "You can do the worst to each other as long as it's for profit. If you do anything with good intentions, you're a nasty dirty person." This double standard must end—we need "science-led and evidence-based and open-minded" exploration of climate interventions.

### Adaptation Didn't Create Moral Hazard

**Contrarian view:** Early fears that discussing climate adaptation would reduce mitigation efforts were unfounded.

**Reasoning:** "A lot of people were like, 'Don't do adaptation. That will have a moral hazard and people won't do enough emissions mitigation.' And none of it happened. No one emitted less because we're doing adaptation."

**Implication:** Similar fears about researching climate intervention are likely equally unfounded.

### Carbon Markets Fundamentally Misunderstand the Problem

**Contrarian view:** Making emissions and removals "fungible" through carbon markets was "a classic misunderstanding of the complexity of the problem."

**Reasoning:** "You've got is a one very certain impact of a fossil emission which will be in the atmosphere for 1,000 years against a somewhat uncertain hope that the carbon stored in a tree [persists]. Those things are not fungible."

This reflects skepticism of "old-fashioned macroeconomics with Newtonian physics underpinning it, which turns out not to be at all how the world works."

### Most People Agree on Climate—Media Creates Division

**Contrarian view:** "We're not as divided as our media and our politics would have us believe" on climate and other issues.

**Reasoning:** Opinion polling shows broad agreement on desired outcomes. The perception of division is partly manufactured by media incentives toward controversy and algorithmic amplification of extreme views.

## 5. Practical Implications

### For Climate Action

**Mitigation remains essential but insufficient:** Solar and batteries have their own momentum now, but "the game's not over yet." Hard-to-abate sectors (high heat, aviation, shipping) still need intensive work.

**Expand the toolkit beyond mitigation:** We need research into:
- **Carbon dioxide removal (CDR):** "The IPCC says there's no way we fix climate change without CDR"
- **Reflectivity restoration:** Potentially replacing harmful sulfur emissions with benign alternatives like salt water foggers
- **Rewilding and nature-based solutions:** "Who's against" turning deserts into carbon-sequestering grasslands?

**Monitor tipping points:** Establish better systems to "look really clearly at what those risks are and understand them better and monitor them better and risk assess better" for AMOC, ice sheets, cloud feedback loops, etc.

### For Activism and Organizing

**Test everything:** Avaaz's practice of testing ~20 versions of campaigns with randomized samples of 20,000 people provided "a very special kind of education" in what resonates.

**Simple North Stars work:** Even if imperfect, clear targets like "1.5 to 2°C" or "fair, ambitious, and binding" help mobilize people who don't understand "arcane climate regulations."

**Rally for hope, not just against failure:** Pre-Paris, radicals wanted to "burn the process down" and "declare it a failure." Patel chose to "mobilize and rally for that hope"—and it worked.

**Democracy requires showing up:** The "90% of politics is who shows up" principle means consistent pressure on decision-makers matters more than perfect arguments.

### For Understanding Risk

**Conservative projections may underestimate danger:** IPCC bands are "conservative" and don't fully account for cloud feedback, ice dynamics, or other factors that could accelerate warming.

**Civilization is fragile:** "Even small shocks" like "some inflation for a bit can really hack at the roots of democratic society." Climate shocks could trigger "non-linear feedback loops" between natural and human systems leading to collapse.

**Prepare for the unexpected:** "I did not have [Canadian heat deaths] on my climate impact bingo list" in 2025. The "global experiment" with Earth's atmosphere will produce surprises.

### For Policy and Governance

**Research bans are immoral:** "I haven't heard any argument for why we don't have to figure out how to do CDR safely, justly, and sustainably."

**Evaluate interventions holistically:** "We have to think holistically about all of the stuff that we're doing for good or for ill and think does this make us safer?"

**Learn from Paris's adaptive architecture:** Bottom-up NDCs worked better than top-down allocation because they were "responsive to how much that push was succeeding" in each country.

**Community-based verification scales better:** For information ecosystems, "community notes" models are "more scalable" than centralized fact-checking and allow fringe ideas to "make their way into the mainstream and that's called progress."

Worthington shares her personal framework:

- **1 day/week:** Adaptation and resilience
- **1 day/week:** "Moonshot" science ideas and climate intervention

This balanced approach acknowledges mitigation's primacy while not neglecting other necessary responses.
