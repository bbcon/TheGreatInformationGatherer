# Is Australia Finally Turning Its Back on Coal? Lily D'Ambrosio | Deep Dive Australia 04

## 1. Episode Overview

**Guest:** Lily D'Ambrosio, Victoria's Minister for Climate Action, Energy, and the State Electricity Commission - Australia's longest-serving energy and climate minister.

**Main Topics:** Victoria's coal phase-out, renewable energy transition, gas policy in homes, transmission infrastructure challenges, and offshore wind development.

**Key Thesis:** Victoria is executing an aggressive energy transition - moving from 80%+ coal dependence to 44.5% renewables in just 10 years, with targets of 65% by 2030 and 95% by 2035 - while managing political, economic, and infrastructure challenges that define the "sausage making" of decarbonization.

**Context:** Australia is a federation where **states control critical levers** including land use planning and transmission decisions. Victoria, home to over a quarter of Australia's population, has historically been the country's manufacturing base powered by lignite ("brown coal") - the most polluting coal globally with energy content famously described as "a wet telephone directory."

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### The Coal Phase-Out and Replacement Strategy

**The Challenge:** Victoria has three major coal plants (Yallourn, Loy Yang A, and Loy Yang B) that must close to meet climate targets.

**D'Ambrosio's Position:** 
- **Yallourn will definitively close in 2028** under a "structured transition agreement" struck with Energy Australia in 2021
- **Loy Yang A will close in 2035** under a similar agreement with AGL
- These dates are "absolutely" set in stone with no negotiations to extend

**Liebreich's Challenge:** Why not release the contracts (with commercial details redacted) to build public confidence? Why hasn't modeling been shared with independent arbiters despite requests from the upper house?

**D'Ambrosio's Response:** Points to the **Australian Energy Market Operator (AEMO)** as the independent authority that has certified Victoria "does not have an energy security or reliability problem for at least the next 10 years." She argues the companies involved are "Gentailers" (generation + retail) who must replace their own supply to serve customers.

**Key Mechanism:** Victoria set **legislated renewable energy targets** to give industry confidence, then introduced Australia's first **reverse auctions for renewable electricity** - a critical shift from feed-in tariffs that kept prices high.

### The Battery Revolution

**The Insight:** Victoria pioneered **big batteries** starting in 2017 (before South Australia's famous Elon Musk battery) through a $20 million battery storage initiative.

**Why It Matters:** Big batteries have fundamentally changed market dynamics by **outbidding gas peaking generation to set the price**. This is a "jujitsu move" that gets gas off the margin, addressing a critical problem where marginal pricing systems blame renewable transitions for what are actually gas price problems.

**The Evidence:** AEMO's latest report shows Victoria's electricity costs in the last quarter were "significantly lower than what it otherwise would have been" specifically because batteries set prices instead of gas.

**Global Significance:** This demonstrates that before reaching 70-80% renewable penetration, batteries can solve the gas price problem that has plagued European markets since Russia's invasion of Ukraine.

### Transmission: The Unpopular Necessity

**The Problem:** Victoria already has **curtailment issues** - wind and solar being turned off because transmission can't accept the electricity.

**D'Ambrosio's Solution:** Victoria **took back control of transmission planning** from national market bodies (which "were not elected, not accountable to voting populations") and established **VicGrid** to plan infrastructure.

**Key Projects:**
- The **Western Renewable Link** will unlock existing generators and enable new projects
- Six new **renewable energy zones** were gazetted just last week, including for offshore wind

**The Political Challenge:** Liebreich pressed on transmission's unpopularity, especially in tourist areas like the Great Ocean Road and agricultural regions. D'Ambrosio acknowledged it's "not an easy thing" but argued it's necessary for "broad public good" and economic competitiveness.

**Risk Management Concern:** The state's auditor general identified risks that replacement generation (especially offshore wind) and transmission may not arrive in time, but the risk register isn't published and modeling hasn't been released - a transparency issue Liebreich repeatedly challenged.

### Gas in Homes: The Cost of Living Battleground

**The Context:** Over 90% of Victorian homes have some gas (dual fuel), with over half using gas heating - far higher than other Australian states due to historically cheap Bass Strait gas that's now depleting.

**The Policy:**
- **New homes and developments must be all-electric** (no new gas connections)
- From March next year, **gas hot water systems must be replaced with electric heat pumps** when they reach end of life
- Victorians can keep gas room heating and cooking stoves
- The state offers support through "the biggest energy efficiency program in the country" to swap appliances at no extra cost

**D'Ambrosio's Argument:** 
- Gas prices have "gone through the roof" since Australia entered the global export market in 2017, tying domestic prices to international markets
- She called for **domestic gas reservation** as early as 2016-17 (which the Commonwealth is now implementing)
- Electric appliances with heat pumps will save Victorians "hundreds of dollars off their energy bills each and every year"
- By 2035, conserved residential gas will equal **85% of Victoria's industrial gas needs** - "like finding another gas project off the coast that we don't have to find"

**Liebreich's Challenge:** If electrification is so much cheaper, why ban gas replacements? People should flock to electric voluntarily. The ban suggests it's not actually cheaper, undermining the cost-of-living argument.

**D'Ambrosio's Response:** Rejects the "benevolent dictatorship" framing. "People are not mugs" - they see their gas bills every two months and understand prices have skyrocketed. The policy prevents **locking Victorians into sunk costs** of expanding gas networks that will be expensive for decades. It's about avoiding stranded assets and future regret.

**The Climate Reality:** Liebreich noted that even if gas prices were fixed, continuing gas use means missing net zero 2050 targets - the fundamental driver regardless of economics.

### Offshore Wind: The Great Unknown

**The Target:** 2 gigawatts in the first auction (August launch), scaling to **9 gigawatts by 2040**.

**The Value Proposition:** "When we do offshore wind, we'd have to do less onshore. And onshore means less transmission."

**The Price Question:** Liebreich pressed hard on pricing, noting UK offshore wind went from £37/MWh (2012) to £90/MWh (~A$180) in recent auctions. Would Victoria accept such prices? Is there a price cap?

**D'Ambrosio's Non-Answer:** Refused to disclose acceptable prices before the competitive tender, but insisted "the combination of offshore wind and the continuation of building onshore... when you take a whole system approach... is cheaper than the counterfactual" of more gas generation and keeping unreliable coal longer.

**The Transparency Issue:** Again, this claim is "based on a model that you won't release" - Liebreich's recurring criticism.

**The Players:** 12+ developers including Star of the South, with "fantastic global players that are really keen to build in Victoria."

## 3. Frameworks & Mental Models

### The Whole System Approach

D'Ambrosio repeatedly emphasized evaluating **total system costs** rather than individual technology costs:
- Offshore + onshore renewables + transmission + batteries vs.
- Extended coal (unreliable) + expanded gas generation + associated infrastructure

**Application:** Don't evaluate offshore wind in isolation at A$180/MWh - evaluate whether the total system with offshore wind is cheaper than alternatives including stranded assets, reliability issues, and gas price volatility.

### The Social License Framework

**Key Insight:** "If you lose social license in this, you lose the authority to be able to take action on climate."

The transition must be framed around **cost of living improvements**, not just climate goals. This explains the gas policy's emphasis on bill savings rather than emissions.

**Application:** Climate policies must deliver tangible economic benefits to maintain political sustainability for the long transition ahead.

### The Sunk Cost Avoidance Principle

Victoria's gas policy is explicitly designed to avoid **locking in infrastructure costs** that consumers will pay for decades:
- Don't expand gas networks to new developments
- Don't replace appliances that create 15-20 year commitments to expensive fuel
- Conserve existing gas for industrial uses where alternatives are harder

**Application:** In transition planning, avoiding future regret and stranded assets is as important as immediate cost comparisons.

### State Control vs. Market Bodies

**The Argument:** National market bodies (like AEMO for transmission) are "not elected, not accountable to voting populations, and were not building and planning in anticipation of this big transition."

Victoria's solution: **Take back direct control** of transmission planning (VicGrid), set legislated targets for certainty, and use state power for land use planning.

**Application:** In federal systems, subnational governments may need to reclaim authority from technocratic bodies to execute transitions at necessary speed.

## 4. Contrarian Takes

### Big Batteries Beat Gas on Price-Setting (Not Just Capacity)

**The Claim:** The conventional focus is on batteries providing capacity or firming. D'Ambrosio's insight is that batteries' **most valuable current function is setting marginal prices** below gas peakers, reducing wholesale costs across the entire system.

**Why It Matters:** This challenges the narrative that renewable transitions inherently raise prices. The problem is gas prices, and batteries solve it before reaching very high renewable penetration.

### Transparency Isn't Always Optimal

**The Contrarian Position:** D'Ambrosio consistently refused to release:
- Structured transition agreements with coal plant owners
- Modeling for replacement generation
- Acceptable price ranges for offshore wind tenders
- Detailed risk registers

**Her Reasoning:** 
- Commercial confidentiality protects competitive processes
- Independent arbiters (AEMO) provide sufficient validation
- CSIRO and other experts confirm the approach
- Detailed disclosure could undermine investor confidence

**Liebreich's Counter:** Transparency is itself a risk management tool. Without it, stakeholders can't validate assumptions, protect themselves, or build confidence in the plan.

**The Tension:** This represents a fundamental disagreement about governance - technocratic confidence vs. democratic accountability.

### Gas Reservation Was Right (But Insufficient)

**The Claim:** D'Ambrosio was calling for **domestic gas reservation** in 2016-17 when Australia entered global LNG markets - a position that was politically lonely at the time.

**Why Contrarian:** Gas reservation is often dismissed as market interference, but D'Ambrosio argues "it was absolutely ludicrous that a country that had so much gas produced... too much of it was being sent overseas" while domestic prices skyrocketed.

**The Limit:** Even if reservation fixed prices, gas still can't be part of a net-zero future - the climate constraint is binding regardless of economics.

### The "Crazy Lefties" Are Actually Cost-Cutters

**The Reframing:** D'Ambrosio explicitly rejected Murdoch media framing of gas bans as ideological: "People are not mugs. People understand that gas prices have gone through the roof."

**The Argument:** The gas phase-out is **conservative economics** - avoiding sunk costs, reducing bills, and preventing stranded assets. The radical position is locking people into expensive, depleting fuel sources.

## 5. Practical Implications

### For Energy Markets

**Battery Arbitrage Opportunity:** Victoria demonstrates that batteries can profitably outbid gas peakers for price-setting, creating a business model beyond simple capacity payments or ancillary services.

**Gentailer Dynamics:** When generators also retail electricity, coal closure agreements create natural incentives to build replacement capacity - they must fill their own supply gap.

**Reverse Auctions Work:** Victoria's experience validates competitive procurement over feed-in tariffs for cost reduction while maintaining investment certainty through legislated targets.

### For Policy and Regulation

**State vs. Federal Authority:** In federal systems, states may need to reclaim transmission planning and land use authority from national market bodies to execute transitions at necessary speed.

**Appliance Standards as Transition Tools:** Mandating efficient electric replacements at end-of-life (rather than banning existing appliances) provides a politically viable path to electrification.

**The Transparency Dilemma:** There's a genuine tension between commercial confidentiality in competitive processes and democratic accountability for major infrastructure decisions - no clear resolution emerged.

### For Investors and Developers

**Victorian Opportunity:** With legislated targets, structured coal closures, state-controlled transmission planning, and upcoming offshore wind auctions, Victoria offers unusual policy certainty.

**Offshore Wind Caution:** No price guidance provided, but UK experience suggests A$180/MWh may be realistic - investors should model whether Victoria will accept such prices or risk auction failure.

**Transmission Timing Risk:** Despite state control and new planning powers, transmission remains the critical path item with political opposition - factor delays into project timelines.

### For Households and Businesses

**Gas Appliance Decisions:** If in Victoria, don't replace gas hot water with gas after March next year. Consider proactive replacement using state efficiency programs before mandates to access support.

**Diesel Exposure:** Australia's diesel dependence (agriculture, mining, transport) creates vulnerability to global price shocks - businesses should accelerate electrification where possible.

**Electricity Price Outlook:** If Victoria's battery strategy works and transmission arrives on time, wholesale prices should remain "the cheapest in the country" - but significant execution risk remains.

### For Other Jurisdictions

**The Victorian Model:**
1. Set legislated renewable targets for certainty
2. Use reverse auctions for competitive pricing
3. Deploy big batteries to get gas off price-setting margin
4. Take state control of transmission planning
5. Phase out residential gas through appliance standards
6. Frame everything around cost of living, not just climate

**The Risks:** This model requires:
- Political durability across election cycles
- Successful offshore wind procurement
- On-time transmission delivery
- Maintaining social license through tangible bill reductions

**Applicability:** Best suited to subnational governments in federal systems with land use authority, aging coal fleets, and high gas dependence.
