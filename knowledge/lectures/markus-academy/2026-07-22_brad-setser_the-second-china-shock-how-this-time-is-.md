# The Second China Shock: A Comprehensive Analysis of Evolving Global Imbalances

## 1. Core Thesis & Intellectual Framework

Brad Setser's analysis presents a fundamental argument: **we are experiencing a "second China shock" that is qualitatively different from the first**, requiring new analytical frameworks and policy responses. This is not merely a continuation of pre-2008 imbalances but represents a structural transformation in how China engages with the global economy and how those engagements create dependencies, vulnerabilities, and financial flows.

The intellectual framework draws on several theoretical traditions. First, there's the **dynamic comparative advantage literature** pioneered by Paul Krugman and Elhanan Helpman, which recognizes that comparative advantage isn't static but can be built through industrial policy, learning-by-doing, and increasing returns to scale. This contrasts sharply with traditional Ricardian trade theory that treats comparative advantage as given. Second, Setser engages with the **infant industry protection** and **natural resource curse** literatures, applying these concepts to understand how China's strategic industrial policies create path dependencies and lock-in effects. Third, the analysis draws on **balance of payments accounting** and international finance theory, but pushes beyond conventional current account analysis to propose what Marcus calls a "resilience account" - a second ledger that measures not just the value of goods traded but the **geopolitical dependencies and choke points** they create.

The core intellectual problem being wrestled with is this: **How do we measure and respond to trade imbalances when the goods being traded carry embedded strategic value beyond their market price?** Traditional economic analysis treats a solar panel or semiconductor as simply a bundle of value to be exchanged. But in the current geopolitical environment, these goods represent potential leverage, technological lock-in, and strategic vulnerability. A country that becomes dependent on Chinese rare earth processing or pharmaceutical precursors faces risks that don't appear in conventional trade statistics.

This matters profoundly because **the policy prescriptions that flow from traditional current account analysis may be inadequate or misdirected** if they don't account for these strategic dimensions. The IMF's currency undervaluation models, for instance, might suggest a 19% undervaluation of the renminbi, but when corrected for measurement issues and strategic considerations, the true figure might be closer to 30%. The difference isn't academic - it determines whether currency adjustment should be a central policy priority or a secondary concern.

## 2. Key Concepts Defined

### The Second China Shock

**Definition**: The current expansion of China's trade surplus and global imbalances, beginning around 2020-2021, characterized by stagnant Chinese imports, surging exports, and a bifurcated Chinese economy where the export sector thrives while domestic demand stagnates.

**Why it matters**: Unlike the first China shock (2001-2008), this one occurs without broad-based Chinese economic growth, meaning China is not generating offsetting demand for the world's goods. The shock is more concentrated in specific sectors (EVs, batteries, solar panels, advanced manufacturing) and creates different dependency patterns.

**Relationship to other concepts**: The second shock is intimately connected to China's **import substitution strategy**, its **dual circulation policy**, and the shift from a **rules-based to transactional international order**. It manifests differently in financial flows than the first shock, with less reserve accumulation and more complex financing patterns.

**Example given**: Chinese auto imports have fallen from 1 million units to 400,000, while Chinese auto exports have surged. This isn't just trade rebalancing - it represents the elimination of a dependency (on foreign cars) while creating new ones (as other countries become dependent on Chinese EVs).

### Dual Imbalances / Resilience Account

**Definition**: Marcus's proposed framework for measuring trade in two dimensions: (1) the traditional goods and services value, and (2) the geopolitical dependencies and choke points created by that trade. The "resilience account" would weight goods differently based on how easily they can be substituted, whether alternative suppliers exist, and what strategic leverage they create.

**Why it matters**: A country might have a balanced traditional current account but a severely imbalanced resilience account if it depends heavily on imports that have few substitutes or alternative suppliers, while its exports are easily replaceable. This hidden imbalance represents strategic vulnerability.

**Relationship to other concepts**: This connects to **dynamic comparative advantage** (dependencies build over time through learning effects), **choke points** (specific goods or technologies that can be weaponized), and the distinction between **gross and net dependencies** (mutual dependencies may be balanced even if gross flows are large).

**Example given**: Rare earth elements represent a classic choke point - China controls processing capacity, and while the trade value may be modest, the strategic dependency is enormous because these materials are essential for defense systems, electronics, and green technology.

### Dynamic Comparative Advantage

**Definition**: The concept that comparative advantage is not fixed by factor endowments but can be actively built through industrial policy, subsidies, learning-by-doing effects, and strategic investment in sectors with increasing returns to scale. Countries can deliberately create comparative advantages in targeted industries.

**Why it matters**: If comparative advantage is dynamic rather than static, then **exchange rate policy and industrial policy become tools for shaping long-term economic structure**, not just responding to existing patterns. A temporarily undervalued currency can help build permanent advantages in strategic sectors.

**Relationship to other concepts**: This justifies China's approach of combining **currency management** with **massive manufacturing investment** and **import substitution**. It also explains why the effects compound over time - early advantages in battery production lead to supply chain clustering, which attracts more investment, which generates more learning, creating a self-reinforcing cycle.

**Example given**: China's dominance in solar panel manufacturing didn't emerge from natural comparative advantage but from sustained industrial policy, subsidies, and an undervalued currency that allowed Chinese firms to gain market share, move down the learning curve, and eventually achieve genuine cost advantages that would persist even without subsidies.

### Import Substitution Strategy

**Definition**: A development strategy where a country deliberately reduces imports by building domestic capacity to produce goods it previously imported, often through tariffs, subsidies, local content requirements, and other industrial policies.

**Why it matters**: China's import substitution is **unusually broad and technologically sophisticated**, extending across advanced manufacturing, semiconductors, aircraft, and other high-tech sectors. This isn't the traditional developing country import substitution of the 1960s-70s, which often produced inefficient industries. China's version aims to achieve technological leadership while reducing dependencies.

**Relationship to other concepts**: Import substitution directly drives the **divergence between Chinese domestic demand and import growth**. It's also central to China's **dual circulation strategy** and President Xi's emphasis on reducing China's vulnerability to foreign choke points while maximizing foreign dependence on Chinese supply.

**Example given**: In semiconductors, China has invested massively in domestic chip production not because it's currently cost-effective but to reduce dependence on foreign suppliers who might be subject to U.S. export controls. The strategy accepts short-term inefficiency for long-term strategic autonomy.

### Investment Income Balance Anomaly

**Definition**: The puzzling phenomenon where China, despite having approximately **$4 trillion more in foreign assets than liabilities**, reports an **investment income deficit of $125 billion** rather than the expected surplus of around $100 billion. This suggests either extremely poor returns on Chinese foreign investments or significant measurement problems.

**Why it matters**: This anomaly distorts China's reported current account surplus by roughly **1.5-2% of GDP**, making China's imbalances appear smaller than they actually are. This feeds into IMF models that assess currency undervaluation, potentially understating the degree of renminbi undervaluation by 10 percentage points or more.

**Relationship to other concepts**: The anomaly connects to China's shift from holding reserves at the central bank to deploying capital through **state banks, sovereign wealth funds, and Belt and Road lending**. It may reflect poor accounting of returns earned by entities other than the central bank, or it may indicate that some Chinese foreign assets are being hidden or misreported.

**Example given**: If China's foreign assets earned even a modest 3% return (the historical average on its reserves), and accounting for 8% returns on FDI, China should show a substantial investment income surplus. The fact that it doesn't suggests roughly $200 billion in annual investment income is missing from the accounts.

## 3. Detailed Argument Analysis

### The Three Key Differences Between the First and Second China Shocks

Setser's argument builds systematically by establishing that **treating current imbalances as a continuation of pre-2008 patterns fundamentally misunderstands the situation**. He identifies three critical differences:

**Geographic Concentration**: The first China shock (2001-2008) saw surpluses expand across nearly all emerging economies - China, oil exporters, Brazil, and other commodity producers all ran large surpluses simultaneously. This reflected a global savings glut driven by multiple factors: high oil prices, commodity booms, and China's export surge. Today's imbalances are **almost entirely concentrated in East Asia** - specifically China, Taiwan, and Korea. Oil exporters run only modest surpluses, and other emerging markets are roughly balanced or in deficit.

This geographic concentration matters because it changes the nature of dependencies and the political economy of adjustment. When surpluses were distributed across many countries, no single nation had overwhelming leverage, and adjustment required coordination among multiple parties. Now, **China's manufacturing surplus alone is approximately 2% of world GDP** - roughly twice the size of Japan's largest-ever surplus and bigger than the combined peak surpluses of Japan and Germany. This concentration creates a single point of leverage and makes the imbalances more about the bilateral China-rest of world relationship than about global macroeconomic coordination.

**China's Internal Bifurcation**: During the first shock, China's entire economy was booming. Exports grew rapidly, but so did investment, consumption, and imports. China was generating demand for the world's commodities, capital goods, and even some consumer products. The current account surplus emerged because exports grew faster than imports, but both were growing robustly. This created a sense that China's integration into the global economy was mutually beneficial - China got export markets and technology, the world got cheap goods and a growing market for its products.

The second shock presents a starkly different picture: **China's export sector is thriving while its domestic economy has stalled**. Domestic demand growth has slowed to perhaps 1-3% annually (with significant uncertainty about the true figure), investment outside manufacturing has collapsed, and consumption growth is anemic. The property sector, which represented 12% of GDP in investment at its peak, has contracted to 6% - a massive drag on growth. Manufacturing investment has surged, but this is highly capital-intensive and creates relatively few jobs, contributing to youth unemployment problems.

This bifurcation means that **China is no longer generating offsetting demand for the world's goods**. Chinese imports in volume terms have barely grown since 2021 - up less than 5% cumulatively over four years, or about 1% per year. Meanwhile, Chinese exports have surged. The result is that China's surplus expansion represents a pure demand drain on the rest of the world, with no compensating increase in Chinese demand for foreign goods. This is why Setser emphasizes that regions selling into the Chinese market, particularly Europe, have been hit especially hard.

**Financial Flow Patterns**: The first China shock was characterized by **explosive reserve accumulation**. From 2002-2012, global reserves grew from roughly $2 trillion to over $11 trillion. China's reserves alone went from under $300 billion to nearly $4 trillion. This reserve growth exceeded the supply of U.S. Treasury securities, creating what Ben Bernanke called a "global savings glut" and driving a search for safe assets that contributed to the housing bubble and financial crisis.

The second shock shows **virtually no reserve growth** despite the return of large current account surpluses. China's reserves have been essentially flat at around $3.2 trillion for a decade (with some volatility around 2015-16 when China allowed depreciation). Other Asian surplus countries have similarly stopped accumulating reserves. Instead, these countries have shifted to deploying surpluses through **state banks, sovereign wealth funds, and other quasi-official entities** that have mandates to seek returns rather than just hold safe assets.

This creates a fundamentally different financial architecture. Before 2008, the problem was finding enough safe assets to absorb reserve demand when Treasury issuance was modest. Now, with U.S. fiscal deficits at 6% of GDP, Treasury issuance is abundant, but the automatic recycling of surpluses into Treasuries has broken down. Instead, financing flows through more complex, opaque, and potentially fragile channels - including leveraged basis trades, private credit, and equity investments. This creates different risks: not a shortage of safe assets, but potentially more volatility in Treasury markets and less stable financing of U.S. deficits.

### The Mechanics of China's Import Stagnation

A crucial part of Setser's argument involves explaining **why Chinese imports have essentially stopped growing** despite continued (albeit slower) economic growth. This isn't just about weak demand - it reflects a deliberate strategic reorientation.

The data shows a **complete breakdown of the normal relationship between domestic demand and imports** after 2021. Typically, imports grow roughly in line with domestic demand - as an economy expands, it consumes more, including more imported goods. In China, reported domestic demand has grown at 3% or so annually, but import volumes have been essentially flat. This 3 percentage point gap compounds over time, creating an enormous divergence.

Several mechanisms drive this:

**Broad-based import substitution**: China has systematically built domestic capacity across sectors where it previously imported. The auto sector provides the clearest example - imports have fallen from 1 million units to 400,000 as domestic production (including EVs) has displaced foreign vehicles. But this extends across machinery, chemicals, and increasingly semiconductors and other high-tech components.

**Strategic technological upgrading**: China's "Made in China 2025" and related policies explicitly target sectors where China depends on imports, particularly in advanced technology. The goal is "indigenous innovation" - not just producing in China, but having Chinese firms control the technology. This has reached the point where China can execute import substitution across a much broader range of industries than in the past.

**Learning effects and increasing returns**: As Chinese firms gain experience in producing sophisticated goods, they move down learning curves and achieve genuine efficiency gains. What starts as subsidized, inefficient import substitution can evolve into genuine comparative advantage. This is the dynamic comparative advantage mechanism in action.

**Weak domestic demand amplifying the effect**: The property collapse and weak consumption mean that even as domestic production capacity expands, domestic demand isn't absorbing all the output. This forces Chinese firms to export more aggressively, turning what might have been import substitution into export surges.

The result is visible in sector-specific data: **in sectors where Chinese domestic demand has been weakest, export growth has been strongest**. This isn't coincidental - it reflects firms with excess capacity relative to domestic demand turning to foreign markets. The ECB's research shows this pattern clearly across multiple industries.

### The Export Surge and Its Composition

While imports stagnated, **Chinese exports in volume terms have exploded**, growing far faster than global trade or global GDP. This isn't just about traditional labor-intensive exports like textiles and toys - the composition has shifted dramatically toward capital-intensive, technology-intensive goods.

The surge is particularly pronounced in:

- **Electric vehicles**: China has gone from a net importer to the world's largest exporter, with companies like BYD competing globally on both price and increasingly on technology.

- **Batteries and battery components**: China dominates the entire supply chain from lithium processing to cell manufacturing to battery packs.

- **Solar panels**: China produces over 80% of global solar panels and controls most of the polysilicon supply chain.

- **Advanced manufacturing equipment**: Including robotics, automation systems, and production machinery.

This composition matters because these are **precisely the sectors that create dependencies and choke points**. A country that becomes dependent on Chinese EVs isn't just importing cars - it's becoming dependent on Chinese battery technology, software systems, and potentially surveillance capabilities (if the vehicles are connected). A country dependent on Chinese solar panels for its energy transition has limited alternatives if that supply is disrupted.

The volume growth is particularly striking when contrasted with price trends. **Chinese export prices have fallen significantly**, meaning the volume surge is even larger than the dollar value increase suggests. China is gaining market share through a combination of genuine efficiency, overcapacity driving down prices, and currency management that keeps the renminbi competitive.

### The Measurement Problem and Its Policy Implications

A technically complex but crucial part of Setser's argument concerns **how China's surplus is measured and why conventional measures understate its true size**. This matters because IMF models and policy discussions rely on these measurements to assess currency undervaluation and appropriate policy responses.

Several measurement issues compound:

**The 2022 Balance of Payments Methodology Change**: China changed how it reports its goods balance in the balance of payments, shifting from customs data to a survey-based approach that the IMF says follows international standards. This change **lowered China's reported surplus by roughly 1% of GDP**. The red line (balance of payments goods balance) falls below the blue line (customs data) after 2022, creating a wedge that makes the surplus appear smaller.

Setser argues this is problematic because: (1) the adjustment isn't constant - it varies over time in ways that are hard to explain; (2) it creates inconsistency with other measures like the contribution of net exports to GDP growth; (3) it makes China's data incomparable to other countries that don't make similar adjustments. Notably, the adjustment has become smaller in recent quarters, which is one reason China's reported current account has suddenly jumped.

**The Investment Income Anomaly**: As discussed earlier, China reports an investment income deficit when it should show a substantial surplus. This **understates China's current account by roughly $200 billion annually**, or about 1.5% of Chinese GDP. For a country with China's asset position, even conservative return assumptions (3% on reserves, 8% on FDI) should generate a surplus of $100 billion or more, not a deficit of $125 billion.

Setser's hypothesis is that China is failing to capture investment income earned by entities other than the central bank - particularly state banks that have set up offshore vehicles, or returns on Belt and Road lending and other foreign direct investment. This isn't necessarily deliberate misreporting, but rather inadequate data collection systems that were designed when most foreign assets were central bank reserves.

**The Cumulative Effect**: When Setser constructs an adjusted current account using: (1) the old goods balance methodology, (2) a model-based investment income balance, and (3) other standard components, he finds China's true current account surplus is approximately **5.5% of GDP rather than the reported 3-4%**.

This seemingly technical difference has major policy implications. The IMF's model suggests China's currency is undervalued by about **19%** based on the reported current account. Using Setser's adjusted figures, the undervaluation is closer to **30%**. This 11 percentage point difference determines whether currency adjustment should be a central focus of policy discussions or a secondary concern.

Moreover, because China manages its currency against the dollar, and many other Asian currencies move with China, correcting a 20% real undervaluation would require a nominal appreciation of perhaps 30% against the dollar and euro. This is the difference between a modest adjustment and a fundamental revaluation of Asian currencies.

### The Financial Architecture Transformation

The shift in how surpluses are financed represents a fundamental change in global financial architecture, with implications for stability, volatility, and crisis dynamics.

**Before 2008**: Surpluses flowed almost automatically into reserves, and reserves flowed almost automatically into U.S. Treasuries and other safe sovereign bonds. Central banks had mandates to hold safe, liquid assets. They weren't trying to maximize returns - they were trying to maintain stability and liquidity. This created **enormous demand for safe assets at a time when Treasury supply was limited** (the U.S. ran fiscal surpluses in the late 1990s and modest deficits in the early 2000s).

The result was what Setser calls "treasury scarcity" - reserve demand exceeded Treasury supply by a wide margin. This pushed central banks and other official investors into agency debt (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac), and pushed private investors searching for yield into increasingly complex and risky structured products. The financial system had to create synthetic safe assets (AAA-rated mortgage-backed securities) to meet demand. This financial alchemy ultimately failed catastrophically in 2008.

**After 2020**: The situation has reversed. Treasury supply is abundant - the U.S. is running 6% of GDP fiscal deficits, issuing over $2 trillion in new debt annually. But reserve demand has disappeared - global reserves are essentially flat. Instead, surplus countries are deploying capital through:

- **State banks**: China's state banks have accumulated $500-700 billion in foreign assets annually in recent years, but this doesn't show up as reserve growth.

- **Sovereign wealth funds**: Korea's National Pension Service, China Investment Corporation, and similar entities have mandates to seek returns, not just safety.

- **Quasi-private flows**: Entities that are government-owned but operate with private-sector mandates, blurring the line between official and private flows.

- **Belt and Road lending**: China's policy banks lending to emerging markets, which is a form of capital export but very different from holding Treasuries.

This creates a different set of risks and dynamics:

**More complex financing chains**: Instead of the simple flow from trade surplus → reserves → Treasuries, financing now involves multiple intermediaries, currency hedging, derivatives, and leverage. The "basis trade" (buying Treasuries and hedging currency risk with derivatives to capture small spreads) has grown enormously, creating leverage and potential fragility reminiscent of Long-Term Capital Management.

**Less automatic recycling**: There's no guarantee that Asian surpluses will finance U.S. deficits. If Asian investors decide they want European assets, or gold, or Bitcoin, there's no mechanism forcing them into Treasuries. This could lead to higher U.S. interest rates or dollar depreciation.

**More price-sensitive investors**: Central banks would hold Treasuries even at low yields because they needed safety and liquidity. Sovereign wealth funds and state banks will sell if returns are unattractive. This makes Treasury markets potentially more volatile.

**Different crisis dynamics**: Before 2008, the risk was that the search for safe assets would create financial fragility in private credit markets (which is what happened). Now, the risk might be more in Treasury market functioning, with leveraged basis trades unwinding rapidly in stress, or with foreign official investors suddenly pulling back from Treasury purchases.

However, Setser notes an important counterargument: **Treasury markets might actually be more stable than housing finance**. Government bonds converge to their nominal value at maturity with certainty (barring default), whereas housing values can collapse. The leverage in basis trades is concerning, but it's secured by government bonds, not subprime mortgages. So while the financial architecture is different, it's not obviously more fragile.

## 4. Historical/Theoretical Context

### The Plaza Accord Parallel

Setser explicitly compares current imbalances to the mid-1980s situation that led to the **Plaza Accord of 1985**, when the U.S., Japan, Germany, France, and the UK agreed to coordinate intervention to depreciate the dollar and reduce trade imbalances. The parallel is instructive:

In 1985, Japan's surplus was roughly **1% of world GDP** (excluding Japan). The combined surplus of Japan and Germany was perhaps 1.5% of world GDP. This was considered unsustainable and prompted coordinated action including currency revaluation, with the yen appreciating from 240 to the dollar to 120 over several years.

Today, **East Asia's manufacturing surplus is approximately 2% of world GDP** - twice as large as the imbalances that prompted Plaza. China alone runs a surplus of similar magnitude to the combined Japan-Germany surplus of the 1980s. Yet there has been no comparable policy response, no coordinated adjustment effort.

Why the difference? Several factors:

- **China's managed capital account** makes currency adjustment more difficult technically and politically than it was for Japan, which had open capital markets.

- **Geopolitical tensions** make cooperation more difficult. Plaza occurred during the Cold War but among allies. Today's tensions between the U.S. and China make coordination fraught.

- **China's strategic intent** differs from Japan's. Japan was willing to adjust (albeit reluctantly) to maintain the alliance with the U.S. China views its surplus and currency management as strategic tools.

- **Distributional effects** are different. Plaza led to a Japanese asset bubble and eventual lost decades. China observes this history and is determined to avoid a similar outcome.

The implication is that **market forces or unilateral pressure, rather than coordinated adjustment, will likely drive any resolution** of current imbalances.

### The Krugman-Helpman Dynamic Comparative Advantage Literature

The theoretical foundation for understanding China's strategy comes from **trade theory developed in the 1980s-90s** by Paul Krugman, Elhanan Helpman, and others. This "new trade theory" departed from traditional Ricardian comparative advantage in crucial ways:

**Increasing returns to scale**: In industries with high fixed costs and learning effects, the first mover or largest producer gains cost advantages that are difficult for others to overcome. This creates path dependence - early advantages become permanent.

**Learning by doing**: Production experience itself generates productivity improvements. The more you produce, the better you get at producing, creating a virtuous cycle for whoever gets started first.

**Strategic trade policy**: If comparative advantage is dynamic rather than static, government policy can create comparative advantage through temporary protection, subsidies, or currency management that allows domestic firms to gain market share, move down learning curves, and achieve permanent cost advantages.

This literature was initially developed to explain Japanese industrial policy and European Airbus subsidies. It provided theoretical justification for industrial policy while acknowledging the risks (government failure, retaliation, rent-seeking).

**China has implemented these ideas at unprecedented scale**. The combination of currency management (keeping exports competitive), massive subsidies (allowing firms to gain market share even at losses), import protection (giving domestic firms captive markets to achieve scale), and strategic investment (targeting high-learning sectors) has created dynamic comparative advantages across a wide range of industries.

The result is that **China's current export competitiveness isn't just about low wages or currency undervaluation** - it reflects genuine efficiency gains from learning and scale. This makes adjustment more difficult because even if the currency appreciated or subsidies ended, much of China's advantage would persist.

### The Infant Industry and Import Substitution Debates

China's strategy also connects to older debates about **infant industry protection** going back to Alexander Hamilton and Friedrich List in the 19th century. The argument was that developing countries needed temporary protection to allow domestic industries to develop, after which they could compete globally.

This strategy was widely attempted in Latin America, Africa, and Asia in the 1960s-70s, generally with poor results. Protected industries often remained inefficient indefinitely, becoming sources of rent-seeking rather than competitive exporters. This led to a strong consensus against import substitution by the 1980s-90s, with the Washington Consensus emphasizing open trade and comparative advantage.

**China's import substitution is different in several ways**:

- **Technology focus**: Rather than protecting low-tech industries, China targets advanced manufacturing, semiconductors, aerospace - sectors with high learning potential.

- **Export discipline**: Unlike classic import substitution, Chinese firms face pressure to eventually export and compete globally, not just serve protected domestic markets.

- **Scale**: China's domestic market is large enough that achieving scale doesn't require exports initially, but the market is competitive enough (often with multiple domestic firms competing) that efficiency pressures remain.

- **State capacity**: China's government has the administrative capacity and resources to implement sophisticated industrial policies, unlike many earlier import-substituting countries.

The result is that **China has achieved successful import substitution across a much broader range of industries** than historical precedent suggested was possible. This challenges the post-1980s consensus against industrial policy and raises questions about whether other countries should attempt similar strategies.

### The Global Savings Glut Hypothesis

Ben Bernanke's **"global savings glut" hypothesis** from 2005 provides crucial context for understanding both the first China shock and how the second differs. Bernanke argued that the U.S. current account deficit wasn't primarily caused by U.S. profligacy but by excess savings in emerging markets (particularly Asia and oil exporters) that flowed into U.S. assets, driving down interest rates and inflating asset prices.

This hypothesis explained several puzzles:

- Why U.S. interest rates remained low despite large fiscal and current account deficits (the "Greenspan conundrum")
- Why the dollar remained strong despite large deficits
- Why housing prices inflated so dramatically

The mechanism was that **emerging market reserve accumulation created enormous demand for safe assets**, which flowed into Treasuries, agencies, and ultimately mortgage-backed securities, driving down yields and inflating housing prices.

The second China shock presents a different configuration:

- **No comparable reserve accumulation**, so no automatic flow into safe assets
- **Large Treasury supply** from fiscal deficits, so no safe asset scarcity
- **More diverse capital flows** through state banks, sovereign wealth funds, and private channels

This means the financial stability risks are different. We're less likely to see a housing bubble driven by a search for yield, but more likely to see Treasury market volatility or sudden stops in capital flows.

## 5. Regional Deep Dive: Europe's Dilemma

While the lecture doesn't extensively detail Europe's situation (Setser indicates this will be covered more in the second part), several crucial points emerge about **why Europe is more affected by the second China shock than the first**:

### Europe's Disappearing Surplus

The data shows that **Europe's goods surplus has essentially disappeared** when Ireland is excluded (Ireland's surplus is primarily pharmaceutical exports to the U.S. for tax reasons, not representative of European manufacturing). This is striking because Europe, particularly Germany, has traditionally run large manufacturing surpluses.

The mechanism is what Adam Tooze calls **"mercantilist on mercantilist violence"** - China's surplus expansion has come partly at the expense of other traditional surplus countries. European manufacturers are losing market share both in third markets and increasingly in their own markets to Chinese competitors.

### Exposure to Chinese Demand Weakness

Europe is more exposed than the U.S. to the weakness in Chinese domestic demand because **European exports are more concentrated in capital goods, luxury goods, and autos** - precisely the categories where Chinese demand has weakened most. German machine tool exports, French luxury goods, and European auto exports have all suffered from China's property collapse and weak consumption.

The U.S. exports more services, agricultural products, and semiconductors to China - categories less affected by the domestic slowdown. Moreover, U.S. exports to China were never as large relative to GDP as European exports.

### The Currency Dimension

Because **China manages its currency primarily against the dollar**, and the euro has depreciated against the dollar in recent years, the renminbi has appreciated against the euro even while remaining stable or depreciating against the dollar. This means European manufacturers face a more appreciated currency relative to Chinese competitors than U.S. manufacturers do.

Setser notes this creates a complex policy problem: if the U.S. pressures China to appreciate against the dollar, but China maintains its dollar peg, the automatic result is that China also appreciates against the euro, helping European manufacturers. But if China only appreciates against the dollar while maintaining its euro rate, European manufacturers don't benefit.

### Strategic Choices

Europe faces **more acute choices about how to manage its China trade relationship** than the U.S. does:

- **Dependence on Chinese demand**: European manufacturers are more dependent on Chinese markets than U.S. manufacturers, making confrontation more costly.

- **Competition from Chinese exports**: European markets are more exposed to Chinese import competition in key sectors like autos and machinery.

- **Limited leverage**: Europe has less geopolitical leverage over China than the U.S. does and less ability to use security relationships as bargaining chips.

- **Internal divisions**: European countries have different exposures and interests, making coordinated policy difficult.

The implication is that **Europe may face a choice between accepting deindustrialization in key sectors or implementing more aggressive trade and industrial policies** to counter Chinese competition. This is a more difficult choice than the U.S. faces because European manufacturing is more exposed.

## 6. Contrarian/Provocative Arguments

### China's Investment Income Deficit is Largely a Measurement Error

**The claim**: China's reported investment income deficit of $125 billion is implausible and likely reflects poor data collection rather than reality. China should show a surplus of $100 billion or more.

**The reasoning**: With $4 trillion more in foreign assets than liabilities, even conservative return assumptions (3% on reserves, 8% on FDI) should generate substantial net income. The fact that China's investment income didn't improve as global interest rates rose (unlike every other surplus country) suggests data problems rather than genuine poor returns.

**Why this challenges conventional wisdom**: It implies that China's current account surplus is roughly **1.5-2% of GDP larger than reported**, which would make it one of the largest surpluses in history relative to GDP. This has major implications for assessing currency undervaluation and appropriate policy responses. Most analysts and the IMF take China's reported data at face value; Setser argues this is a mistake.

### The Second China Shock is Bigger Than the First

**The claim**: Current imbalances are approximately **twice as large** relative to world GDP as the pre-2008 imbalances that led to the global financial crisis.

**The reasoning**: East Asia's manufacturing surplus is roughly 2% of world GDP, compared to about 1% in 2007. China's surplus alone is comparable to the combined Japan-Germany surplus of the 1980s that prompted the Plaza Accord. The concentration in a single country makes the imbalance more severe, not less.

**Why this challenges conventional wisdom**: Many analysts view current imbalances as a return to pre-crisis patterns, not as something larger and more concerning. The narrative has been that imbalances "corrected" after 2008 and are now returning to normal levels. Setser argues they've actually exceeded previous peaks and are more concentrated and potentially more destabilizing.

### Currency Undervaluation is 30%, Not 19%

**The claim**: When properly measured, China's currency is undervalued by approximately **30% rather than the IMF's estimate of 19%**.

**The reasoning**: The IMF's models use China's reported current account surplus of 3-4% of GDP. Correcting for the 2022 methodology change and the investment income anomaly suggests the true surplus is 5.5% of GDP. The IMF's own model, when fed this higher surplus, implies 30% undervaluation.

**Why this challenges conventional wisdom**: This suggests that **currency adjustment should be a much higher policy priority** than current discussions assume. A 30% undervaluation is massive - it's the difference between a modest policy concern and a fundamental distortion of global trade. It also suggests that market forces alone are unlikely to correct the imbalance without policy intervention.

### The Financial Architecture is More Stable Than Before 2008

**The claim**: Despite the complexity and opacity of current financial flows, the system may actually be **more stable than the pre-2008 architecture** that channeled surpluses into housing finance.
