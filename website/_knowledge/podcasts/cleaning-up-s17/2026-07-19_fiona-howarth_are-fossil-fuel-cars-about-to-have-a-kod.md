# Summary: Are Fossil Fuel Cars About to Have A Kodak Moment? Ep248: Fiona Howarth

## 1. Episode Overview

**Guest Background:** Fiona Howarth is the founder of Octopus Electric Vehicles, part of Octopus Energy. She holds an engineering degree from Oxford (where women comprised only 4% of engineering students), worked at BMW on hydrogen fuel cells in the early 2000s, and later pioneered smart home technology with Hive thermostats before joining Octopus in 2017 to build their EV business.

**Main Topics:** The future of the UK electric vehicle market, China's EV dominance, vehicle-to-grid charging technology, and whether legacy automakers are facing a "Kodak moment."

**Key Thesis:** The EV transition is inevitable and accelerating, driven by technological advancement and policy support. Legacy manufacturers resisting this change risk becoming the "Blackberries" of the automotive world, while those investing early will become the "iPhones." The transition requires maintaining policy courage despite political headwinds.

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### The Chinese EV Advantage

**The Question:** Why has China leapfrogged Western manufacturers in EV technology?

**Howarth's Perspective:** Chinese manufacturers like BYD started investing significantly earlier and benefited from **legacy battery manufacturing experience** (BYD began as a mobile phone battery company for Nokia and Motorola). This heritage gave them fundamental advantages in battery technology.

**Key Insights:**
- **Battery costs have dropped from ~$1,000/kWh to ~$100/kWh over the past decade** - representing roughly a $20,000 reduction per typical car battery
- Chinese EVs arriving in Europe are "not just low cost and affordable but are also really very good cars and people really like them"
- BYD produces over 4 million EVs annually - more than total UK car sales
- Chinese companies went from 1.5-3 million vehicles in a single year, demonstrating unprecedented scaling capability
- The innovation pace is extraordinary: "You just stumble into a shopping center and there'd be another Chinese EV brand that you haven't heard of"

**Supporting Evidence:** When Howarth visited China, she witnessed the sheer diversity of EV brands and innovation - karaoke systems, detachable drones for filming your drive, advanced entertainment systems - demonstrating how Chinese manufacturers understand EVs as "tech on wheels" rather than just cars.

### The Policy Imperative: ZEV Mandates

**The Question:** Are UK policies forcing EVs too quickly, or are they essential for the transition?

**Howarth's Perspective:** The **Zero Emission Vehicle (ZEV) mandate** has been critical for creating market conditions for success. Without it, the transition would stall.

**Key Mechanisms:**
- The ZEV mandate (inspired by California's earlier version) requires manufacturers to sell a certain percentage of EVs
- This policy **unlocked over £6 billion in private investment for public charging infrastructure**
- It solved the chicken-and-egg problem: manufacturers claimed they couldn't sell EVs without charging infrastructure; the policy gave investors confidence to build that infrastructure
- Currently only requires 80% electric by 2030, with full ban by 2035 (hybrids allowed until then)

**The Resistance:** Some legacy manufacturers are "lobbying against the policy because they're not ready," but Howarth argues this is precisely why the policy is needed. Those ahead of the curve (Tesla, Mercedes, BMW) are "stealing a march" while laggards distract themselves with lobbying rather than investing in catching up.

**Howarth's Warning:** "We don't want to invest in the kind of Blackberries. We want to make sure that we are as a country investing in the future of the sector too and getting behind those that are making the iPhones and the cars of the future."

### Vehicle-to-Grid: The Game-Changing Technology

**The Question:** How can EV batteries help balance the electricity grid?

**Howarth's Insight:** A typical Tesla battery (65 kWh) **can store enough power for the average UK home for 5 days**. This distributed storage capacity could revolutionize the energy system.

**The BYD Partnership:** Octopus announced the **first commercially viable vehicle-to-grid partnership globally** with BYD:
- £299/month gets you the car (BYD Dolphin), installed bidirectional charger, and **free electricity for driving**
- The business model works because Octopus can use the car battery as a grid asset, storing cheap/green energy and selling it back during peak times
- This moved the market: BMW and Mercedes announced vehicle-to-grid products at the 2023 Munich auto show in response

**Broader Implications:** 
- Ofgem planning documents envision up to 40% of UK vehicles providing grid services by 2040-2050
- This addresses the intermittency challenge of renewable energy without massive investment in stationary batteries
- Smart charging means EVs charge when electricity is cheapest and greenest (middle of the night), saving drivers 80-90% versus petrol costs

### The Affordability Narrative

**The Challenge:** Electricity is perceived as expensive in the UK, potentially undermining EV adoption.

**Howarth's Counter-Argument:** 
- **Home charging saves 80-90% of fuel costs versus petrol**
- Public rapid charging costs about the same as petrol, but most charging happens at home
- New innovations like "Airbnb for home charging" and curbside gullies allow on-street parkers to access cheap rates
- The **perception gap is enormous**: the Climate Change Committee estimates net zero will cost 0.2% of GDP, but the average Brit thinks it will cost **14,000% more than that**

**The Political Weaponization:** Howarth notes that "living costs are high and they've got other things to do in the immediate term like pay their bills, feed their kids" - making cost concerns a political vulnerability even when the reality is more favorable than perceived.

### The Decarbonization Debate

**Worthington's Contrarian Take:** The UK's push to fully decarbonize electricity by 2030 is "the wrong priority right now." Once the grid is below ~700g CO2/kWh (down from coal's 900g), **it's already more efficient to use that electricity to displace fossil fuels in transport** than to obsess over the last 100g/kWh in the power sector.

**The Strategic Argument:** 
- Focus should be on "where people can save the most money" - which is transport
- This creates a "win-win-win of cleaner air, cleaner climate and affordability"
- The current approach risks making electricity more expensive (constraining wind, pushing too hard too fast) which undermines electrification of transport and heating

**Howarth's Agreement:** She diplomatically acknowledges the challenge of politicians who've committed to targets not wanting to appear to backtrack, but agrees the pragmatic approach would be to "tweak that and change it" - though this would be "weaponized" politically.

### Supply Chain Realities

**The Question:** Will mineral constraints limit EV growth?

**Howarth's Nuanced View:**
- **Lithium and cobalt concerns are overstated**: "History has shown that when we need more of it we find more of it" (e.g., lithium discovered in Cornwall)
- Technology is evolving away from scarce materials: **sodium-ion batteries** entering production this year don't require lithium
- Cobalt content in batteries has been steadily reduced
- **Copper is the real challenge** - needed across the entire energy transition (grid, EVs, heat pumps, data centers), and mines take 10-15 years to bring online

**The Circularity Advantage:** Unlike fossil fuels which are "poof gone" after one use:
- EV batteries last 10-15 years in cars
- Then can be repurposed for stationary storage for another 20-30 years
- Then can be recycled
- "You mine it once and you're going to keep using that for so many years"

Some battery recyclers are finding incoming batteries in such good condition they're **repurposing rather than recycling** them.

## 3. Frameworks & Mental Models

### The "Kodak Moment" Framework

**The Concept:** Just as Kodak failed to adapt to digital photography despite inventing it, and Blackberry failed to compete with the iPhone, legacy automakers risk obsolescence if they resist rather than embrace the EV transition.

**Application:** 
- Identify whether companies are investing in the future or lobbying to slow change
- Recognize that **"the first player through the wall always gets a bloody nose"** (Moneyball reference) - disruption creates resistance from incumbents whose way of life is threatened
- Understand that lobbying against policy is a sign of weakness, not strength

### The Technology Adoption Cost Curve

**The Pattern:** New technologies start expensive and become affordable through scale:
- Mobile phones and computers followed this path
- EVs are following the same trajectory
- Early adopters "help pave the way for people that come later"

**Tesla's Strategy:** Explicitly started with expensive cars (Roadster, Model S/X at £90-100k) to fund development of affordable models (Model 3/Y), demonstrating the **deliberate path from premium to mass market**.

### The Trust-Building Interface Model (from Hive)

**Key Insight:** British consumers "quite like to know what's actually happening" rather than having systems take full control (contrasting with Nest's learning approach).

**The Progression:**
1. Give users full control with visibility (set specific temperatures/times)
2. Offer helpful automation: "You've left the house, would you like us to turn off heating?"
3. Build trust through repeated successful interactions
4. Gradually increase automation: "Would you like us to do this every day?"

**Application to EVs:** "You're still in control of the outcome, but we're just doing the charging at the time that's best for you and your car" - guaranteeing 80% charge by 7am while optimizing for cost and carbon.

### The Decentralized Energy System Transition

**The Old Model:** Centralized generation → transmission → distribution → consumption

**The New Model:** 
- Distributed generation (rooftop solar)
- Distributed storage (EV batteries, home batteries)
- Flexible demand response
- Dynamic pricing reflecting real-time supply/demand

**The Challenge:** "Our energy system has been set up with a kind of centralized much bigger central generation" and **market design hasn't kept up** with the technological reality. Some markets are "leapfrogging the UK's energy system because they've never had the legacy incumbent energy system in the first place."

## 4. Contrarian Takes

### "We Should Slow Down Power Decarbonization"

**Worthington's Argument:** Obsessing over getting the last 100g CO2/kWh out of electricity is counterproductive when:
- Electricity is already clean enough (~700g/kWh threshold) to make electrification of transport beneficial
- Pushing too hard raises electricity costs, undermining transport and heating electrification
- Strategic focus should be on **where decarbonization creates affordability wins** (transport) rather than diminishing returns (ultra-clean power)

**Howarth's Support:** Agrees there's risk in "push[ing] that too quickly, too hard" and notes "we're containing wind. You know, we're spending a lot of money doing that. We need to build out our grid more quickly."

### "Policy Rollback Would Be Counterproductive Even Though EVs Will Win Anyway"

**The Paradox:** Howarth argues that even though EVs are now competitive on merit ("you're not going to go back to your Blackberry or your Nokia when you can have an iPhone"), **maintaining policy is still critical** because:
- It provides consistency and confidence for charging infrastructure investors
- Watering down targets would undermine the £6 billion private investment in charging
- It's not about helping manufacturers in the short term, but about securing long-term jobs and investment

**The Implication:** Sometimes policy needs to stay in place not to force an outcome, but to maintain the conditions that make the market-driven outcome possible.

### "The Misinformation Is Traceable to Incumbents"

**Howarth's Observation:** Incumbent fossil fuel and automotive companies have financial incentive to "lobby or share information that causes fear and uncertainty" to slow the transition. She references research showing **misinformation campaigns traced back to communications companies funded by car manufacturers**.

**The Moneyball Analogy:** "They don't want you to be successful because you're disrupting their way of lives... at the point where they're trying to slow us down, you know, we've got something right cuz they're worried."

### For Businesses

**Strategic Opportunities:**
- Employee EV schemes offer tax advantages and help recruitment/retention
- Octopus EV has helped 7,500 businesses, raising £2 billion in funding
- Vehicle-to-grid can create new revenue streams from company fleet batteries
- Early adoption positions companies as climate leaders

### For Investors

**Bullish Signals:**
- **Public charging infrastructure** backed by £6 billion in private investment
- **Vehicle-to-grid technology** creating new business models
- **Battery recycling/repurposing** emerging as valuable sector
- **Smart energy management** (AI-driven forecasting, demand response)

**Bearish Signals for:**
- Legacy manufacturers lobbying against policy rather than investing in transition
- Companies not developing bidirectional charging capability
- Markets/countries watering down EV mandates

### For Policy Makers

**What's Working:**
- ZEV mandates creating investment confidence
- Time-of-use pricing enabled by smart meters
- Allowing innovations like curbside gullies for on-street charging

**What Needs Attention:**
- **Grid buildout** isn't keeping pace (paying to curtail wind)
- **Market design** still reflects centralized rather than distributed system
- **Energy pricing** in UK is highest in Europe, undermining electrification
- Focus on **skills and manufacturing subsidies** rather than watering down mandates
- Consider **reframing from "bans" to "market incentives"** to reduce political vulnerability

**Worthington's Recommendation:** Shift focus from power sector purity to transport electrification where climate, air quality, and affordability align.
