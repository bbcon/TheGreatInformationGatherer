# The World Decides: Clean Energy or Oil & Gas? - Damilola Ogunbiyi

## 1. Episode Overview

**Guest:** Damilola Ogunbiyi is the CEO and UN Special Representative for Sustainable Energy for All (SEforAll) and co-chair of UN Energy. This is her third appearance on Cleaning Up (previously episodes 62 and 124).

**Main Topic:** The future of global energy will be determined not by the US, China, and Europe—but by the "most of world" (the Global South, where 70% of the world's population lives). The central question: Will these countries buy lots of oil and gas with a bit of clean energy, or lots of clean energy with a bit of oil and gas?

**Key Thesis:** Despite ongoing global crises and distractions, the Global South—particularly Africa—is experiencing **organic, homegrown innovation** in clean energy adoption. The challenge isn't technology anymore; it's **finance innovation** and scaling successful models beyond the "pilot mentality."

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### The State of Energy Investment in Africa

**Host's framing:** Last time Ogunbiyi appeared (during the Russia-Ukraine energy price spike), she memorably said "just invest in something" because Africa was receiving only 1.5% of global energy investment.

**Guest's update:** Investment has doubled to **3% of global energy investment**—progress, but still inadequate. More significantly:
- **$2.2 trillion** was spent globally on renewable energy in the last year, exceeding fossil fuel investment for the first time
- **Utility-scale renewables are now cheaper** than the lowest-cost fossil fuel equivalent
- Even without considering climate, renewables are "just a good economic bet"

**The challenge of scale:** Africa needs roughly **20x its current installed capacity**. Doing development or energy infrastructure projects in Africa costs **seven times more** than in Europe—a fundamental injustice since the population is far more vulnerable.

**Supporting evidence:** 
- Nigeria alone traded about 400 megawatts of solar panel capacity with China
- People are organically choosing solar-plus-storage over diesel generators for self-generation
- This is happening **without government funding or subsidies**

### Mission 300: A Landmark Initiative

**The framework:** The World Bank and African Development Bank are leading **Mission 300**, aiming to connect **300 million Africans to electricity between now and 2030** (out of 560 million currently without access).

**Key mechanisms:**
- Every government must sign up to a **set of reforms**
- **$30-40 billion in concessional financing** will flow to the continent
- Focus on clean energy transition, integrated grids, and utility reform
- Described as "the largest public-private-philanthropic partnership" coming to Africa

**Significance:** This represents a shift from scattered pilots to coordinated, scaled action with institutional backing.

### The Mini-Grid Revolution and Market Consolidation

**Guest's insight:** The mini-grid sector is undergoing **consolidation**—similar to what happened in the US and Europe when electricity first emerged.

**The pattern emerging:**
- Countries like Nigeria with 200+ mini-grids won't have 200 separate owners
- Consolidation into **4-5 larger companies**, each becoming utilities with millions of customers
- This creates attractive customer bases for additional services: productive uses, cell tower connections, even AI servers

**The tech-energy convergence:** Tech companies are entering the energy space because they see **energy as an enabler** to sell multiple services—microloans, IT services, etc. This organic, market-driven development is creating what Ogunbiyi calls "a real marketplace" for the first time.

**Integration strategy:** Mini-grids are being designed with **standards to integrate with the main grid** when it arrives. Villages become towns, towns become cities—the infrastructure must be ready to connect.

### Policy Innovation: The Global South Leading

**Contrarian observation:** Ogunbiyi challenges assumptions about who's leading on policy innovation.

**Examples of bold Global South policies:**
- **Ethiopia:** Legislation not just encouraging EVs but **banning combustion engine buses**
- **Kenya and South Africa:** Any water heating systems in new builds **must be solarized** or building approval is denied
- These are regulations that "will never be passed in the global north"

**Host's reflection:** This represents a potential **leapfrog moment**—the Global South may be creating a modern, consumer-centered energy system without going through the centralized, patriarchal model that developed countries used.

### The Data Revolution

**The problem:** Traditional energy statistics miss massive amounts of activity—large-scale projects and utility-scale renewables are counted, but household solar adoption, e-bikes, EVs, and decentralized renewable energy (DRE) often aren't.

**Example - Pakistan:** The economy was growing but grid electricity demand was dropping—a conundrum. Investigation revealed **massive solar imports** not showing up in statistics. Satellite imagery showed incredible growth, with farmers using solar to pump water. The addition was equivalent to **50% of grid capacity in just 18 months**.

**SEforAll's approach:** Working to capture granular data—pinpointing on GIS maps exactly who lacks electricity, cross-referencing with cell phone ownership (indicating ability to pay), and creating **least-cost development pathways** for each country using AI modeling.

**Practical application:** This allows governments to understand that perhaps 50% of unelectrified population needs grid connection, 35% can use mini-grids, and 20% should receive solar home systems—rather than applying one-size-fits-all solutions.

### The Tier Framework Debate

**Guest's strong position:** "I don't believe in poor solutions for the most vulnerable people." 

**The controversy:** Ogunbiyi argues that **solar lanterns should not count as electricity access**—they're lighting, which is useful, but not electricity. With phones, internet, and modern life, "people just need more energy."

**The challenge:** When people get small solar systems, they often start businesses from home (especially women-led households), then need more capacity. The **cost of transitioning to larger systems** becomes a barrier.

**Host's experience:** When electrifying a neonatal unit in Bo, Sierra Leone, Liebreich insisted it should have **the same power reliability as Queen Charlotte's Hospital in London**. He faced pushback suggesting this was unrealistic for people earning $200/year. His response: "These are women giving birth to babies. The babies are unwell. Of course they should have the power supply as good as anybody else in the world."

### Whole-Economy Electrification

**The question:** Are Global South countries thinking about electrification of transport, heating, and industry—pushing out fossil fuels as developed countries are attempting?

**Guest's response:** Many countries are **ahead of the developed world** in some areas:
- Ethiopia's combustion engine bus ban
- Kenya and South Africa's solar water heating mandates
- These represent "audacity" and "strong political points"

**For least-developed countries:** Places like Sierra Leone don't have much energy to "transition out of"—instead, they're developing **green growth pathways** from the start, rather than building fossil fuel infrastructure and then having to transition away.

**The challenge:** Even with commitments, the **cost of capital and investment needed doesn't match**. Nigeria needs **$1.9 trillion** to reach net zero by 2060, with $400 billion above business-as-usual spending—meaning **$15 billion per year** starting now.

### The Finance Innovation Imperative

**Guest's central argument:** "We have all the technology we need to make sure anyone has access or gets to net zero. But what we don't have is enough **innovation in financing**."

**The cost of capital problem:**
- Local developers borrowing for projects face **25-30% interest rates**
- International companies with Series A/B/C funding get money at **2%**
- This creates unfairness and can crowd out local players—yet both are needed

**Emerging solutions:**
- **Solar-as-a-service** models allowing payment over 2-3 years rather than upfront costs
- **African sovereign wealth funds** investing in clean energy for the first time
- Nigeria's sovereign wealth fund developing a **decentralized renewable energy fund**
- Africa 50 (infrastructure fund from 30+ African countries, chaired by African Development Bank)
- **Africa Finance Corporation** (managing $12-15 billion, funding developers and large infrastructure)

**SEforAll's initiatives:**
- Training for pension funds and institutional investors to understand the sector
- Launching a **developer platform** answering due diligence questions upfront
- Creating country-specific investment decks (e.g., "If you want to invest in Mozambique, here's who to invest in for industry, DRE, or EVs")

**Host's insight from ClimateScope research:** One of the strongest indicators of clean energy investment flowing to a country is the **availability of local capital**. Capital formation locally is critical—overseas partners want to see a mosaic of providers, not just World Bank/IFC/AfDB.

### The Carbon Finance Opportunity

**The mechanism:** When generators are swapped for solar-plus-storage, the **emissions savings can be translated to carbon credits** with proper regulation.

**Recent progress:** Nigeria just passed a framework allowing this, with potential to move from voluntary markets to **Article 6 compliance markets**.

**The equity dimension:** Done properly, carbon credits benefit not just developers but also **communities**—representing another finance innovation pathway.

## 3. Frameworks & Mental Models

### The "Least-Cost Development Pathway" Model

**Framework:** Using AI and granular data to model the optimal mix of:
- Grid extension (typically ~50% of unelectrified population)
- Mini-grids (typically ~35%)
- Solar home systems (typically ~20%)

**Application:** Rather than master plans saying "in 10 years you'll get electricity," this allows real-time decisions based on actual electricity quantum needed in each location. If a community needs 200 kilowatts, extending the entire grid may not be cost-effective.

### The "Both/And" Grid Strategy

**Framework:** You need a grid AND you don't need to wait for the grid.

**Key principle:** Mini-grids should be built with **standards allowing grid integration** when it arrives. This isn't either/or—it's sequenced development that meets immediate needs while preparing for future integration.

**Application:** Utilities are now investing in DRE or partnering with decentralized renewable energy companies to build out—becoming "little discoms" that integrate over time.

### Power Pools as Continental Innovation

**Framework:** Regional power pools (Eastern, Southern, Western Africa) allowing electricity trading across borders.

**Significance:** Ogunbiyi argues this is **"one of the first things Africa can give to the rest of the world"**—Asia has tried for decades to create similar structures. It's one of the only ways to integrate large-scale renewables given intermittency.

**Application:** Part of Mission 300 strategy, enabling countries to share resources and balance renewable generation.

### The Energy Investment Calculation

**Host's framework:** If you have $1 million and need energy for a growing economy:
- **Buying fuel** (oil/gas to burn): One-off purchase, energy consumed in one year
- **Buying assets** (wind/solar/nuclear): Produces energy for 25+ years

**The math:** You get **10-15x more energy** (joules/megajoules/kilowatt-hours) by buying the asset versus buying fuel.

**The catch:** Requires finance. In year one, you only get 1/25th of the asset's lifetime output, so you need **patient capital** and low interest rates—which is exactly what's missing in the Global South.

## 4. Contrarian Takes

### Global South as Policy Leader, Not Follower

**Contrarian view:** "When sometimes people think about the global south, they don't really understand just the innovation they're adding into the spaces and the audacity they have."

**Reasoning:** Ethiopia banning combustion engines and Kenya/South Africa mandating solar water heating represent **stronger political commitments** than most developed countries have made. The assumption that policy innovation flows from North to South is outdated.

### Reject the "Appropriate Technology" Mindset

**Contrarian view:** "I don't believe in poor solutions for the most vulnerable people. I believe in solutions that are actually going to give them a better life."

**Reasoning:** Solar lanterns shouldn't count as electrification. The "tier framework" for electricity access needs revision. A neonatal unit in Sierra Leone deserves the same power reliability as one in London—full stop.

**Challenge to consensus:** Much development thinking accepts lower standards for poorer countries. Ogunbiyi and Liebreich both reject this as fundamentally unjust.

### Tech Companies as Energy Players

**Contrarian observation:** The energy sector is seeing **unexpected entrants**—tech companies viewing energy as an enabler for their services rather than the product itself.

**Reasoning:** Market clusters with communal energy are often run by tech companies offering microloans, IT services, etc. This wasn't predicted but is happening organically.

**Implication:** We may be missing other sectors that should be involved. Ogunbiyi asks: "Is there another sector we're missing that we should bring in?"

### End the Pilot Mentality

**Contrarian position:** "There's no time for pilots anymore. We need to scale and we need to know if we make mistakes it's just called lessons learned."

**Reasoning:** With only 5 years until 2030 SDG deadline, and insufficient resources, the continuous pilot-pilot-pilot approach is a luxury the Global South cannot afford.

**Challenge:** This goes against development sector norms of extensive testing and evaluation before scaling.

## 5. Practical Implications

### For Markets/Investing

**The consolidation opportunity:** Mini-grid sector consolidation creates **utility-scale investment opportunities** with millions of customers—much more attractive than scattered small projects.

**Local capital is key:** Investors should look for countries with **strong local capital markets and pension funds** willing to deploy domestically. This is a leading indicator of successful clean energy deployment.

**The developer platform:** SEforAll's upcoming platform will provide **pre-vetted due diligence** on developers by country and sector—reducing information barriers that prevent investment.

**Carbon finance:** With proper frameworks (like Nigeria's new one), **carbon credits from generator-to-solar swaps** create additional revenue streams for projects.

**The 3% problem:** Africa receiving only 3% of global energy investment (up from 1.5%) means there's **enormous room for growth**—but also that current levels are grossly inadequate for the scale of opportunity and need.

### For Understanding Macro

**The 70% question:** Energy analysts obsess over US, China, and Europe, but **70% of the world's population lives elsewhere**. The majority of new energy demand growth is in "most of world."

**The decisive question:** Will the Global South buy lots of oil and gas with a bit of clean energy, or lots of clean energy with a bit of oil and gas? **This will determine the global energy system's future.**

**Interconnection matters:** The US snowstorm diverting LNG exports and affecting European gas prices demonstrates how **everything links to energy** and we're all interconnected.

**Data gaps:** Official statistics **dramatically undercount** clean energy deployment in the Global South—household solar, e-bikes, EVs, and DRE often don't appear in data, creating a false picture.

**The leapfrog is real:** Countries are moving from 2G to 4G connectivity, skipping 3G. Similarly, they may **skip centralized fossil fuel systems** and build consumer-centered clean energy systems from the start.

### Actionable Takeaways

**For policymakers:**
- **Set bold standards** (like Ethiopia's combustion engine ban or Kenya's solar water heating mandate)—the Global South can lead on policy
- Focus on **least-cost development pathways** using data and AI rather than one-size-fits-all approaches
- Ensure mini-grid standards allow **future grid integration**
- Develop **carbon credit frameworks** to monetize emissions reductions

**For investors:**
- Look for **consolidation plays** in the mini-grid sector
- Prioritize countries with **strong local capital markets**
- Consider the **tech-energy convergence**—companies using energy as an enabler for other services
- Use emerging platforms (like SEforAll's) for **pre-vetted due diligence**

**For development organizations:**
- **End the pilot mentality**—focus on scaling what works
- Invest in **granular data collection** to understand true energy access and usage
- Support **finance innovation** more than technology innovation (the tech exists)
- Train **local institutional investors** (pension funds, sovereign wealth funds) to understand the sector

**For companies:**
- **Solar-as-a-service** models work better than requiring upfront payment
- Design systems that can **scale up** as customer needs grow (especially for women-led businesses)
- Partner with **local developers** rather than crowding them out, despite access to cheaper capital

**For understanding progress:**
- Don't rely solely on official statistics—look for **satellite data, import statistics, and on-the-ground reports**
- Recognize that **1 light bulb + phone charger ≠ electricity access**
- Track **power reliability** not just connection rates
- Understand that energy access affects **2/3 of the 169 SDGs**—it's the "red thread" holding development together
