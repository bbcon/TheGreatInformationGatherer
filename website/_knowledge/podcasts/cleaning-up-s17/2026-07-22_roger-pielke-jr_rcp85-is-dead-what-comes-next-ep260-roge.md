# RCP 8.5 Is Dead: What Comes Next? - A Conversation with Roger Pielke Jr.

## 1. Episode Overview

**Guest Background:** Professor Roger Pielke Jr. is a senior fellow at the American Enterprise Institute (formerly tenured professor at University of Colorado Boulder), expert on science-policy interfaces, leading researcher on disaster costs and hurricane impacts, and author of "The Honest Broker" book and influential Substack. He's been a controversial figure for challenging the climate science community's use of extreme scenarios.

**Main Topic:** The climate science community has officially declared RCP 8.5 - the worst-case emissions scenario that has dominated climate research for 15 years - as "implausible." This represents a major shift in how we understand climate futures and their policy implications.

**Key Thesis:** While climate change remains serious and requires urgent action, the scientific community has been using an implausible worst-case scenario (RCP 8.5) that has distorted research, policy, and public understanding. The self-correction is overdue but demonstrates science working as it should.

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### The Honest Broker Framework

**The Question:** What roles should scientists play in policy debates?

**Pielke's Perspective:** Scientists have choices in how they relate to society: they can be advocates, retreat to ivory towers, serve as "honest brokers" providing a range of options, or participate in advisory processes like the IPCC. **The key insight is that "honest brokering is a group activity"** - it's something done collectively through respectful debate and disagreement.

**Current Relevance:** This framework is particularly germane given the Trump administration's executive orders on "gold standard science" and attempts to reverse the endangerment finding. Pielke notes these are mostly "for show" and won't create lasting policy change, which requires Congressional action. The administration appears driven more by "vengeance" against experts than coherent policy.

### Understanding Climate Scenarios

**The Framework:** Climate models don't start with physics - they start with **socioeconomic projections**: population, economic size, energy systems, and technologies. These determine CO2 emissions, which then drive climate change projections.

**RCP 8.5 Explained:** 
- RCP = Representative Concentration Pathway
- The "8.5" refers to radiative forcing (watts per square meter) in 2100
- It's a **pathway to 2100**, not just a temperature target
- Requires CO2 concentrations reaching ~1,100 ppm (vs. 430 ppm today, 280 ppm pre-industrial)
- Demands burning **7-9 times current coal consumption**
- Equivalent to building **30,000-40,000 new coal plants** by 2100

**Why It Matters:** Despite being labeled extreme, RCP 8.5 became characterized as "business as usual" and **dominated climate research** - appearing in ~105,000 papers (including 3,000 published in 2026 alone), policy documents, banking regulations, stress tests, and media coverage.

### The Implausibility of RCP 8.5

**Key Evidence:**
1. **Learning-by-doing theory failure:** The scenarios assumed coal would become cheaper and more dominant through learning curves - the same mechanism that has actually made solar, wind, and batteries cheaper
2. **Resource constraints:** There isn't enough recoverable coal to fuel the scenario
3. **Economic alternatives:** Renewables, nuclear, and other options weren't properly modeled
4. **Demographic impossibility:** The "high" scenario assumes 14 billion people by 2100, which demographers consider implausible

**Historical Context:** Justin Richie and Hadi Dowlatabadi identified these problems in 2017, but the scenario continued to dominate. Pielke characterizes this as **"a single point of failure across thousands of different scenarios."**

**The Correction:** In April 2026, Detlef van Vuuren (who originally launched the RCPs in 2011 stating they must be "plausible") led a paper declaring RCP 8.5 implausible. Pielke emphasizes this represents **science self-correcting**, which builds rather than undermines public trust.

### Real-World Policy Implications

**Concrete Examples:**
- **San Francisco Airport:** Planning for sea level rise adaptation requires focusing on plausible scenarios, not worst-case 8.5
- **London flood protection:** Host Michael Liebreich notes that if RCP 8.5 were plausible, Transport for London would need to start expanding the Thames Barrier immediately - a massive resource allocation decision
- **Financial regulation:** The Network for Greening the Financial System (NGFS) has based stress tests on 8.5

**The Challenge:** Pielke argues there's **no immediate policy change needed** because "the pace of mitigation will be much more a function of economics, technology, public opinion" than climate scenarios. The focus should be on **growing clean energy faster than energy demand growth** - a multi-decadal effort rather than quick fixes.

### The New "High" Scenario

**What's Coming:** The new high scenario has forcing of ~6.7 watts/m² (not 8.5), translating to approximately **3.0-3.5°C warming by 2100** (vs. 4.7-5.4°C under RCP 8.5).

**Critical Flaw:** This scenario was **generated before plausibility testing** - the same mistake made 20 years ago. It's based on 14 billion people, which is implausible. Pielke suggests that with proper demographic adjustments, "high becomes a lot more like the medium scenario."

**The Risk:** Despite being labeled as requiring active policy choices to increase emissions (not business-as-usual), there's **"enormous risk it'll just be treated as a reference scenario"** - exactly what happened with RCP 8.5.

### Tipping Points and Abrupt Climate Change

**Pielke's Position:** Tipping points are **real and concerning**, providing "another background reason why aggressive mitigation makes sense." However, they shouldn't drive the politics of climate action.

**Key Distinctions:**
- Historically, climate has moved abruptly **without human influence** (on decadal timescales)
- Human forcing makes such events **more likely in unquantifiable ways**
- Examples: 1870s El Niño (killed ~4% of global population), 1970s harvest failures
- **Benefits of mitigation:** "We're just provoking the system a little bit less"

**On Specific Tipping Points:**
- **Amazon tundra flip:** Could happen, but takes hundreds to thousands of years
- **Ice sheet collapse:** A23A iceberg took 40 years to melt (1,000 gigatons); Greenland/Antarctica are millions of gigatons
- **AMOC shutdown:** Science shows 30-50% slowdown by century's end, not collapse within decades
- Most tipping points have long **"commitment times"** before impacts manifest

**The Research Gap:** Pielke uses a powerful analogy: studying milk boiling at setting 10 tells us nothing about setting 5 due to non-linearity. **We've wasted a decade researching extreme scenarios instead of the pathways we're actually on.**

### Hurricane Research and Disaster Costs

**The Normalization Method:** Pielke pioneered asking: "If past storms hit today's population and wealth, what would damages be?" The 1926 hurricane could cause **>$400 billion in 2026 dollars**.

**Key Finding:** There is **no trend in US hurricane frequency or intensity** in the landfall record - confirmed by NOAA, IPCC, and matching the economic normalization.

**On Claims of Worsening Storms:**
- **Faster intensification, northward tracking, more rainfall:** These are hypotheses with at most "medium confidence" from IPCC
- **Higher proportion of intense storms:** True since 1970, but caused by **fewer total storms**, not more intense ones - "clutching at straws"
- **Why hurricanes are poor climate indicators:** Only 60-80 globally per year with enormous variability, unlike temperature/precipitation with continuous global measurements

## 3. Frameworks & Mental Models

### The Plausibility Vacuum

**Concept:** Climate scenarios have been generated in a "plausibility vacuum" - numbers are chosen first, then modelers work backward to figure out what would be required to reach them.

**Better Approach:** 
1. Test demographic projections against demographer consensus
2. Evaluate energy system assumptions against technological and economic realities
3. Consider resource constraints
4. Only then generate forcing pathways

**Application:** Any scenario assuming 14 billion people or 7x coal consumption should be immediately flagged for plausibility review.

### Temperature Targets as Policy Objects

**The Problem:** Pielke argues **"there's no knob in the policy control room where you can dial it to 1.7 or 2 or 1.5."** Temperature targets don't function as actionable policy objectives.

**Alternative Framing:** Focus on **growing clean energy faster than energy demand growth** as a multi-decadal sustained effort, rather than searching for quick targets or actions.

### Science Self-Correction and Public Trust

**Counter-Intuitive Insight:** Scientists often fear that admitting errors will undermine trust. **The opposite is true** - self-correction builds confidence, while refusing to acknowledge obvious problems destroys it.

**Application:** When science corrects course (as with RCP 8.5), it should be celebrated and communicated clearly, not hidden or defended.

## 4. Contrarian Takes

### On Fear-Based Climate Communication

**The Claim:** Many believe that extreme scenarios and tipping point fears will motivate climate action.

**Pielke's Counter:** **"It not only does it not work, it can actually backfire"** by causing people to lose faith in science. The RCP 8.5 story demonstrates this - exaggeration became obvious and undermined credibility.

**Supporting Logic:** If activists, journalists, and scientists continue using scenarios now declared implausible, it damages trust in all climate science, not just the scenarios.

### On Chris Wright as Energy Secretary

**Conventional View:** Wright is a fossil fuel executive unsuited for the role.

**Pielke's Take:** Wright "would be a great secretary of energy under any president, Democrat or Republican" because he **"knows an enormous amount about energy."** His views on climate science proper are "pretty much in the mainstream" - his Liberty Energy reports cited the IPCC extensively.

**Disagreement:** Pielke does challenge Wright's claim that coal will dominate for the rest of his life, noting renewables overtook coal in electricity generation last year.

### On the Trump Administration's Science Policy

**Appearance:** Coordinated attack on climate science and scientific institutions.

**Reality:** Pielke characterizes it as **"a campaign of vengeance"** rather than coherent policy. Example: targeting NCAR (National Center for Atmospheric Research) for elimination stems from a political dispute between Colorado's governor and Trump, not policy thinking.

**Implication:** These actions will last only as long as the administration - lasting policy requires Congressional action.

### On Mitigation Sensitivity to Uncertainty

**Conventional View:** We need precise knowledge of tipping points and climate sensitivity to determine mitigation urgency.

**Pielke's Position:** **"The challenge of mitigation is insensitive to those sorts of questions."** We should pursue aggressive mitigation regardless, because:
- It reduces risk of abrupt changes (even if unquantifiable)
- Energy affordability and technology drive the transition more than climate science
- Fundamental ignorance about the future is unavoidable

## 5. Practical Implications

### For Climate Research

**Immediate Needs:**
1. **Plausibility testing framework:** Establish formal processes before scenarios are adopted
2. **Research reallocation:** Focus on pathways we're actually on (RCP 4.5, 3.6, 2.9) rather than extreme scenarios
3. **Demographic updates:** Incorporate current population projections
4. **Technology realism:** Model actual energy transition dynamics, including learning curves for renewables

**The Lost Decade:** From ~2017 (Richie-Dowlatabadi) to 2026, the community could have been researching plausible futures instead of defending the indefensible.

### For Policy and Regulation

**What Changes:**
- **Infrastructure planning:** Sea level rise, flood protection should use new "high" scenario (~3-3.5°C) not RCP 8.5 (4.7-5.4°C)
- **Financial stress testing:** Banking regulations and NGFS frameworks need updating
- **Cost-benefit analyses:** Trillions in policy decisions have been based on implausible scenarios

**What Doesn't Change:**
- **Mitigation urgency:** Even 3-3.5°C is serious; aggressive action remains justified
- **Adaptation needs:** Still significant, just calibrated to plausible rather than extreme scenarios
- **Multi-decadal commitment:** This remains a long-term challenge requiring sustained effort

### For Communicators and Journalists

**Red Flags to Watch:**
- Any study using RCP 8.5 or SSP5-8.5 as "business as usual" (especially post-2026)
- Claims about the new "high" scenario being where we're headed
- Tipping point claims with specific near-term timelines
- Hurricane trends that don't account for normalization

**Better Practices:**
- Ask which scenario underpins any climate impact story
- Distinguish between commitment time and impact time for tipping points
- Recognize that **"follow the science"** is problematic when it masks value choices
- Understand that scientists can play different legitimate roles (advocate vs. honest broker)

### For Understanding Disaster Costs

**The Normalization Principle:** Rising disaster costs primarily reflect **more people and wealth in harm's way**, not necessarily worsening physical phenomena.

**Application:** When seeing claims about increasing hurricane damages:
1. Check if they're normalized for population/wealth
2. Compare to geophysical record of storm frequency/intensity
3. Look at landfall data specifically (where damage occurs)
4. Consider building code improvements

### For Energy Transition Strategy

**Key Insight:** The transition will be driven by **"economics, technology, public opinion"** more than climate scenarios. 

**Actionable Focus:**
- Grow clean energy faster than energy demand growth
- Prioritize energy affordability (compatible with aggressive mitigation)
- Think multi-decadal, not quick fixes
- Nuclear is "having a moment" with hyperscaler backing
