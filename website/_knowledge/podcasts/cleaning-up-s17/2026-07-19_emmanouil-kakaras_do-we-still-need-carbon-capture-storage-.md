# Summary: Do We Still Need Carbon Capture & Storage? | Ep250: Emmanouil Kakaras

## 1. Episode Overview

**Guest Background:**
Emmanouil Kakaras is a technology evangelist and former Executive Vice President for Green Transition at Mitsubishi Heavy Industries (MHI), with 30 years of experience in energy engineering. He holds multiple patents in power-to-fuel technologies and serves as a university professor in Athens. His career uniquely bridges academia and industry, having been "plucked" from his professorship by Hitachi (later acquired by MHI) to work on energy transition technologies.

**Main Topics:**
- Carbon capture and storage (CCS) technology and its necessity
- Decarbonization pathways for hard-to-abate sectors
- Hydrogen and ammonia combustion in gas turbines
- Green steel production and direct reduced iron (DRI)
- Power-to-X technologies and synthetic fuels
- Industrial heat pumps and energy efficiency

**Key Thesis:**
Despite opposition from some climate advocates, **CCS is essential for decarbonizing hard-to-abate sectors** like cement and steel. The energy transition requires a portfolio approach combining electrification, zero-carbon fuels, and end-of-pipe carbon capture—not ideological commitment to single solutions.

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### The Inevitability of Carbon Capture & Storage

**The Question:** Why do we need CCS when renewable energy and electrification are advancing rapidly?

**Kakaras's Perspective:**
Drawing on historical parallels, Kakaras argues that CCS opposition mirrors the resistance to sulfur scrubbers in the 1980s. He recalls: *"As I am in this business quite a long time, I remember same people hating the desulfurization plant... nobody was believing that we will going to retrofit all these coal plants... And at the end, because it's an end-of-pipe technology and because there was a societal commitment for that, it becomes the standard state-of-the-art technology."*

**Key Insights:**
- **Hard-to-abate sectors** (cement, steel, certain chemical processes) have process emissions that cannot be eliminated through fuel switching alone
- CCS is already proven at scale—MHI's Petronova project in Texas captures 1.5 million tons of CO2 annually from coal plants
- The **Padeswood cement project** in the UK (800,000 tons/year) and **Ravenna project** in Italy demonstrate commercial viability
- MHI's **KS-21 amine solvent** technology achieves efficient CO2 capture with lower energy penalties for regeneration

**Supporting Evidence:**
- Post-combustion capture works for both process emissions (like cement calcination) and combustion emissions
- Japan's ambitious national CCS program is testing various configurations in real conditions
- The technology integrates with existing infrastructure without requiring complete industrial redesign

### Advanced Gas Turbine Technology & Decarbonization

**The Question:** How can fossil fuel-based gas turbines contribute to decarbonization?

**Kakaras's Perspective:**
MHI has developed what they claim are the world's best gas turbines, achieving **1,700°C combustion temperatures** while maintaining low NOx emissions through sophisticated combustion control and special alloys/coatings.

**Key Mechanisms:**
- **Higher temperatures = higher Carnot efficiency**, but create thermal NOx challenges
- MHI uses "highly sophisticated" combustion systems to control nitrogen oxide formation
- Turbines are being adapted for **hydrogen and ammonia combustion** without efficiency compromises
- The approach is **fuel-flexible**—turbines can switch between natural gas, hydrogen, ammonia, and syngas

**Practical Applications:**
- Combined cycle plants already achieved significant CO2 reduction through efficiency gains
- **Dual-path decarbonization**: either remove CO2 from flue gas (CCS) or burn zero-carbon fuels
- Industrial gas turbines have used off-gases from steel and chemical processes for decades

### The Hydrogen and Ammonia Reality Check

**The Question:** What role will hydrogen and ammonia actually play in the energy transition?

**Kakaras's Perspective:**
While MHI has developed hydrogen-fired turbines and ammonia combustion technology, Kakaras is **pragmatic rather than evangelical** about these fuels. The fundamental challenge: *"We have the technologies for the hydrogen use, but we don't have the hydrogen."*

**On Ammonia:**
- Japan's interest stems from being an **energy importer** seeking carbon-free energy carriers
- Ammonia (NH3) presents severe technical challenges—14 of 17 grams is nitrogen, creating fuel NOx on top of thermal NOx
- **Recent breakthrough**: MHI can now burn ammonia in gas turbines without cracking it first
- Economics remain challenging—Japan's ammonia imports fell short of targets due to price
- Best applications: **fertilizers first**, possibly shipping, and only lastly back-to-power

**On Hydrogen:**
- Round-trip efficiency for green ammonia-to-power is approximately **20%** of input electricity
- More viable as **seasonal storage** than frequent cycling
- The discussion is "not one size fits all"—hydrogen will find specific niches, not universal application

**Framework Introduced:**
Kakaras suggests comparing zero-carbon fuels to **biodiesel on a CO2 avoidance cost basis**—if synthetic fuels are cheaper than biodiesel per ton of CO2 avoided, they deserve market space.

### Green Steel: Pragmatism Over Purity

**The Question:** Should green steel production focus exclusively on hydrogen-based direct reduction?

**Kakaras's Perspective:**
The steel industry is pursuing **flexible, forward-compatible solutions** rather than betting everything on hydrogen availability.

**Key Insights:**
- **Direct Reduced Iron (DRI)** technology (via Primetals/Midrex license) can start with natural gas and gradually switch to hydrogen
- Natural gas DRI is **not a stranded asset**—it remains viable even with hydrogen availability
- Some carbon is needed for reduction purposes anyway
- The approach combines well with CCS for natural gas-based processes

**Supporting Technologies:**
- Electric arc furnaces for recycled steel (electrification pathway)
- R&D on metal oxide cycles—using metal oxide reduction as energy storage and rapid oxidation as power generation
- Interest in **electrolytic iron production** (Boston Metals, Fortescue processes)

**Practical Implication:**
*"It's always the same discussion about hydrogen. We have the technologies for the hydrogen use, but we don't have the hydrogen. So, it's a question of availability."*

### Industrial Electrification & Heat Pumps

**The Question:** How far can electrification extend into industrial processes?

**Kakaras's Perspective:**
Kakaras is a "great believer in electrification," surprising for someone from a turbine manufacturer. MHI's **Turboden subsidiary** built the world's largest heat pump—**12 MW producing steam at 180°C** with a coefficient of performance (COP) of 2.4.

**Key Insights:**
- In industrial applications, **COP of 2 is "fantastic"** because it competes with gas boilers, not domestic heating standards
- The Finland project (wood pulp industry) represents **circular economy at its best**—recycling waste heat within the factory
- Industrial-scale heat pumps can now produce steam, something considered impossible a decade ago
- Applications extend beyond heat pumps to **Carnot batteries** (high-temperature thermal storage)

**Technology Portfolio:**
- Organic Rankine Cycle (ORC) for geothermal (including 300 MW Fervo project in Utah)
- ORC has "very poor thermodynamic efficiency" but is "highly economic" because it exploits zero-cost waste heat
- Heat batteries for steel industry using electricity stored as heat in inert materials

### Power-to-X: Finding the Viable Niches

**The Question:** Which power-to-X applications make economic sense?

**Kakaras's Perspective:**
Power-to-X requires **abundant renewable electricity** as a precondition. The most promising applications combine hydrogen with **biogenic CO2**.

**Viable Pathways:**
1. **Methanol production**—the simplest molecule, already demonstrated at RWE's Niederaussem plant (2015-2016)
2. **Co-electrolysis** of CO2 and steam using solid oxide electrolyzers, producing syngas (CO + H2)
3. **Yield enhancement** for biodigesters—combining vented CO2 with electrolytic hydrogen
4. Applications that are cheaper than biodiesel on a **CO2 avoidance cost basis**

**Technology Developments:**
- MHI's solid oxide co-electrolyzers can process CO2 and steam together
- Syngas production opens both methanol and Fischer-Tropsch pathways
- Uncertainty remains about which synthetic fuel pathway will dominate

**Kakaras's Caution:**
Synthetic methanol can contain "90% water in some cases"—not exactly the fuel desired. The economics depend heavily on carbon pricing and comparison to alternatives.

## 3. Frameworks & Mental Models

### The CO2 Avoidance Cost Ladder

**Framework:** Evaluate all decarbonization solutions on **dollars per ton of CO2 avoided or removed**.

**Application:**
- Compare synthetic fuels to biodiesel—if cheaper per ton CO2 avoided, they deserve market space
- Prioritize solutions by cost-effectiveness rather than political acceptability
- Recognize that "e-jet fuels at 12 times the cost of kerosene is not going to be a thing"

**Kakaras's Insight:** *"We really need to go back to value for money tests and the value for money in this space is in terms of the climate solutions has got to be dollars per ton of CO2 avoided or absorbed or removed."*

### Technology Portfolio Approach

**Framework:** No single technology solves all decarbonization challenges—success requires matching technologies to specific applications.

**Application:**
- **Electrification** where technically and economically feasible
- **Zero-carbon fuels** where electrification is impractical
- **CCS** for process emissions and hard-to-abate sectors
- **Biogenic carbon** combined with hydrogen for specific synthetic fuel applications

**Key Principle:** "It's not one size fits all"—different sectors and applications require different solutions.

### Forward Compatibility in Technology Development

**Framework:** Develop technologies that can start with available resources and upgrade as better options become available.

**Application:**
- DRI plants starting with natural gas but capable of switching to hydrogen
- Gas turbines designed for fuel flexibility (natural gas, hydrogen, ammonia, syngas)
- Infrastructure that doesn't become stranded as the energy system evolves

**Strategic Value:** Reduces risk and enables immediate emissions reductions while maintaining long-term decarbonization pathways.

## 4. Contrarian Takes

### CCS is Essential, Not Optional

**Contrarian View:** While many climate advocates oppose CCS as prolonging fossil fuel use, Kakaras argues it's unavoidable for hard-to-abate sectors.

**Reasoning:**
- Process emissions from cement calcination cannot be eliminated by fuel switching
- Historical precedent: desulfurization was similarly opposed but became standard
- Some industrial processes will always generate CO2 regardless of energy source
- The alternative—not decarbonizing these sectors—is worse

**Quote:** *"Some people really hate CCS, carbon capture and storage, and I just say get over it because we're going to need it."*

### Hydrogen Won't Dominate, Despite the Hype

**Contrarian View:** Despite developing hydrogen turbines, Kakaras doesn't believe in a broad hydrogen economy.

**Reasoning:**
- Technology exists, but **hydrogen availability is the constraint**
- Round-trip efficiency for many hydrogen pathways is prohibitively low
- Economics favor electrification in most applications
- Hydrogen will find specific niches, not universal application

**Nuance:** This comes from someone whose company has invested heavily in hydrogen technology—it's not anti-hydrogen ideology but economic realism.

### Ammonia's Future is Uncertain

**Contrarian View:** Japan's enthusiasm for ammonia may not translate to global adoption.

**Reasoning:**
- Japan's ammonia strategy reflects their specific situation as energy importers
- Recent import volumes fell short due to price sensitivity
- Technical challenges (NOx control) are severe
- Better applications exist (fertilizers, possibly shipping) before back-to-power

**Implication:** Ammonia may remain a niche solution rather than a major energy carrier.

### Electrification Can Go Further Than Expected

**Contrarian View:** Coming from a gas turbine manufacturer, Kakaras's enthusiasm for electrification is surprising.

**Reasoning:**
- Industrial heat pumps can now produce steam—previously thought impossible
- Many "impossible to electrify" processes will yield to engineering R&D
- *"How many researchers, how many engineers... so much will yield to research over the next decade, two decades"*
- Fundamental thermodynamic limits exist, but many perceived limits are merely current technical constraints

### Syngas Deserves Reconsideration

**Contrarian View:** Old-fashioned syngas (town gas) might have a role in the future energy system.

**Reasoning:**
- MHI turbines have burned industrial off-gases for 30+ years
- Co-electrolysis can produce syngas from biogenic CO2 and renewable electricity
- Syngas is "very well known" technology with established applications
- Could serve as energy storage and fuel simultaneously

**Quote:** *"Syngas... has been around for how many years now, more than a century. Maybe has also a position in the carbon free [future]."*

## 5. Practical Implications

### For Industrial Decarbonization

**Cement Industry:**
- Post-combustion capture is the only viable near-term solution
- MHI's KS-21 amine technology captures both process and combustion emissions
- Padeswood project (800,000 tons/year) demonstrates commercial scale
- Electrification of cement kilns remains experimental—CCS is state-of-the-art

**Steel Industry:**
- Start DRI with natural gas, maintain hydrogen compatibility
- Combine with CCS for natural gas-based processes
- Electric arc furnaces for recycling
- Don't wait for hydrogen availability to begin decarbonization

**Chemical Industry:**
- Utilize biogenic CO2 from existing biodigesters (currently vented)
- Combine with electrolytic hydrogen for yield enhancement
- Consider methanol as building block for chemicals

### For Energy System Planning

**Gas Turbines:**
- Maintain fuel flexibility—design for natural gas, hydrogen, ammonia, and syngas
- Combined cycle efficiency gains already delivered significant CO2 reduction
- Pair with CCS where zero-carbon fuels aren't economically viable
- Consider syngas from co-electrolysis as storage medium

**Heat Management:**
- Industrial heat pumps viable up to 180°C steam production
- COP of 2+ makes them competitive with gas boilers
- Waste heat recovery can achieve circular economy within facilities
- Geothermal with ORC for 24/7 carbon-free baseload

**Data Centers:**
- Integrated solutions (power, cooling, energy management) reduce complexity
- Direct chip cooling technologies emerging
- Energy management software as important as hardware
- Gensets, batteries, and turbines all play roles

### For Technology Investment

**High Priority:**
- **CCS for hard-to-abate sectors**—proven, necessary, scaling
- **Industrial heat pumps**—economic today, large addressable market
- **Fuel-flexible turbines**—hedge against fuel uncertainty
- **Co-electrolysis**—enables multiple synthetic fuel pathways

**Medium Priority:**
- **Hydrogen combustion**—technology ready, awaiting fuel availability
- **Methanol synthesis**—demonstrated but needs carbon pricing
- **DRI with hydrogen capability**—start with natural gas, upgrade later

**Speculative/Long-term:**
- **Ammonia combustion**—technical challenges remain, economics uncertain
- **Metal oxide cycles**—interesting R&D, low technology readiness
- **Electrolytic iron production**—potentially transformative, early stage

### For Policy and Carbon Pricing

**Key Insight:** Much of the technology portfolio depends on **carbon pricing or equivalent mechanisms**.

**Implications:**
- Without carbon price, many solutions won't be economic
- CO2 avoidance cost should guide policy priorities
- Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR) credits provide alternative to ETS pricing
- Value-for-money testing essential—avoid politically popular but expensive solutions

**Kakaras's Warning:** *"Provided that we keep our appetite for decarbonization. That's a driver that is not negotiable or you cannot make discounts."*

### Actionable Takeaways

1. **For industrial facilities:** Implement CCS where process emissions exist; don't wait for perfect zero-carbon fuel solutions

2. **For energy planners:** Design systems with fuel flexibility; avoid betting on single fuel pathways

3. **For investors:** Evaluate power-to-X projects against biodiesel on CO2 avoidance cost basis

4. **For steel producers:** Begin DRI transition with natural gas while maintaining hydrogen compatibility

5. **For policymakers:** Establish carbon pricing that makes CCS and synthetic fuels competitive; use CO2 avoidance cost as primary metric

6. **For technology developers:** Focus on forward-compatible solutions that work with today's resources but can upgrade
