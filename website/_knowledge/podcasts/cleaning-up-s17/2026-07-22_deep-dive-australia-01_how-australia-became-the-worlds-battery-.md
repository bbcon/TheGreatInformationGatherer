# How Australia Became The World's Battery Champion | Deep Dive Australia 01: Darren Miller

## 1. Episode Overview

**Guest Background:** Darren Miller is the CEO of the Australian Renewable Energy Agency (ARENA), a position he's held for 8 years. Prior to ARENA, he spent 6-7 years in the renewable energy space as an investor, consultant, and operator in rooftop solar, batteries, and electricity retail. ARENA is Australia's federal government agency responsible for funding new renewable energy, electrification, and energy efficiency projects.

**Main Topics:** Australia's leadership in solar and battery deployment, ARENA's funding model and approach, green steel opportunities, sustainable aviation fuel challenges, and Australia's clean energy transition.

**Key Thesis:** Australia has emerged as the world's per capita leader in battery deployment and rooftop solar, positioning itself to potentially become a renewable energy superpower through ultra-low-cost solar ($20/MWh target) and innovative grid technologies, though significant challenges remain in transportation electrification and industrial decarbonization.

## 2. Major Themes Discussed

### Australia's Battery Revolution

**The Scale of Achievement:**
Miller reveals stunning statistics: **350,000 home batteries installed in the past year alone** under the "cheaper home batteries program" - representing **3.5% of all 10 million Australian households buying a battery in a single year**. Miller confidently states: "I'm pretty sure that Australia is the number one battery market per capita on the planet by a long way."

Beyond residential, Australia ranks **third globally in absolute battery installations** (behind only China and the US) - remarkable for a country of 28 million people. Miller notes Australia's per capita battery installations are "almost double the next best in line."

**Three Battery Scales:**
1. **Home batteries:** Driven by a 30% government rebate program, leveraging the existing 30%+ rooftop solar penetration
2. **Large-scale grid batteries:** Starting with the famous 2016 Elon Musk/Mike Cannon-Brookes bet (build in 90 days or it's free), which "called the bluff of the renewable energy causes blackout whiners"
3. **Community batteries:** Mid-scale neighborhood batteries (shipping container-sized) that provide **more cost-effective storage per unit energy** and create portfolio effects, as not every home needs battery access simultaneously

### Grid-Forming Technology: ARENA's Strategic Intervention

**The Problem Identified:**
Miller explains a critical technical challenge: As coal and gas plants (with their spinning mass providing natural 50Hz frequency signals) are removed, **inverter-based renewable technology needs something to provide the grid signal**. Initially, all batteries were being installed in "grid-following" mode because it was "cheapest and easiest."

**ARENA's Solution:**
In 2022, ARENA put forward $100 million (matched with another $60 million from government) in a **reverse auction** requiring battery projects to enable **grid-forming capabilities**. Eight projects signed up, five are likely to be built, and Miller considers this a standout success: "That's caused the market to adopt this technology... exactly the kind of model that we want to see at ARENA."

**The Impact:**
Now "the vast majority of big batteries going into the market... are all going in with grid-forming capabilities." Only one technical challenge remains: **protection grade fault current**, which ARENA is working with universities to solve in lab environments before the grid operator (AEMO) will trust the devices.

### Solar Innovation and the $20/MWh Target

**Australia's Solar Heritage:**
The University of New South Wales (UNSW), led by Professor Martin Green, invented the **PERC cell (passivated emitter rear contact conductor)**, which powered up to 85% of world solar production. The technology is now moving to **TOPCon, also a UNSW invention**. However, Miller notes the patents expired before commercialization, so "it wasn't able to be protected" - the technology ended up in Chinese modules with no licensing revenue to Australia.

**ARENA's Current Solar Strategy:**
Miller articulates an ambitious goal: **"I am a true believer that Australia will be able to produce solar energy at a levelized cost of energy of $20 [Australian] or under a megawatt hour"** (approximately $15 US).

The dual-track approach:
1. **Efficiency improvements:** ARENA funds the Australian Centre for Advanced Photovoltaics (A-CAP) with over $100 million over 14 years, targeting **>30% efficiency** (deliberately chosen because it requires unlocking tandem cell technology). Researchers believe they can reach **40% efficiency by 2040**.

2. **Deployment cost reduction:** The **"Mega Demand" program** provides $45 million to Fortescue to allocate 500 MW of their 1.5 GW Pilbara solar portfolio for experimenting with **robotics, AI, and prefabrication**. From 117 global applications, ARENA is deploying innovative technologies with 2-3 EPCs and developers, making "Australia a giant experimental sandbox of solar innovation."

Miller argues: "We shouldn't underestimate what efficiency does for the economics of solar. It is an unlock for the cost right throughout the supply chain downstream of the module."

### Green Steel: Australia's Industrial Decarbonization Challenge

**The Opportunity:**
Steel-making represents roughly **10% of world greenhouse gas emissions**. Australia has the advantage of **very low-grade Pilbara hematite ores** and potentially ultra-cheap renewable electricity. The challenge: these ores "don't play nicely in a direct reduced iron process."

**ARENA's Approach:**
Working with "the very biggest steel producers and miners and industrial companies" to develop processes that can **upgrade Pilbara ores for electric arc furnaces**, most likely using green hydrogen if costs can be reduced sufficiently (hence ARENA's support for companies like Hysata developing cheaper electrolyzers).

**The Two-Step Question:**
Miller separates the challenge into:
1. **Can the ores be processed in a low-carbon way?** (The technology question)
2. **Where should this happen?** (The economics question)

On the second question, Miller is candid about uncertainty: "It might be that we prove the technology in Australia... but that the likes of China or the Middle East take those ores and produce them in a low carbon way." The determining factor: **whether Australian renewable electricity can be cheaper than China's**.

Miller's confidence on electricity costs: "I actually do believe that with the work that I'm seeing happening in Australia... we will get our electricity core electricity price from solar down below $20 Australian a megawatt hour. So $15 US. I think we can do that."

However, he adds a crucial caveat: "The other part of it is the capital expenditure on the actual plant, the green steel plant, which we have to make sure we're cost-competitive globally, otherwise we will have wasted our cost advantage on the electrons because we haven't got an efficient plant."

### Hydrogen: From Hype to Reality

**The Funding Rollercoaster:**
ARENA has funded about **60 hydrogen projects totaling ~$300 million** since 2017. The **Hydrogen Headstart program** was created in response to the US Inflation Reduction Act, initially with $2 billion, expanded to $4 billion for **production credit subsidies** (no taxpayer risk until hydrogen is actually produced and used).

Recently, **$1 billion was clawed back** from the program in the latest budget. Miller explains: "Hydrogen had its hype cycle. It hasn't performed as people wanted and expected, and so there's been a moderation of ambition."

**ARENA's Pragmatic Position:**
Miller states: "I think we always had a middle of the road kind of mentality to it that we thought it would be needed for very hard to abate sectors and decarbonization into industry." ARENA never funded road transport hydrogen applications, but did fund a gas injection project ("we just wanted to make some hydrogen to get our hands dirty. We wouldn't probably wouldn't be doing that again").

Liebreich interjects his view that blending hydrogen into gas networks is "the stupidest idea from stupidville because you take this incredibly difficult expensive thing that you've made and you then reduce it to the value of its heat content only."

**Most Promising Use:**
Both agree: **green iron production**. Miller: "We think that the most promising use for hydrogen especially in an Australian context will be green iron production in the future."

**Blue vs. Green Hydrogen Debate:**
Liebreich challenges Miller with his thesis that **blue hydrogen (from natural gas with carbon capture) will remain cheaper than green hydrogen** due to the energy intensity of water electrolysis. This creates a dilemma for ARENA, whose mandate is "renewable" energy.

Miller reveals ARENA is exploring **turquoise hydrogen (methane pyrolysis)** - decomposing methane without oxygen. They're "doing that work right now" to determine if it's eligible under their act. The logic: "We're supporting a downstream electrification opportunity and displacing fossil fuels downstream."

### Vehicle-to-Grid: The Next Frontier

**The Program:**
Miller announces a **$13.6 million program funding 1,000 EVs with vehicle-to-grid technology**, launched with innovative electricity retailer Amber. This is one of Miller's current favorites: "I don't see other parts of the world putting forward the correct vehicle-to-grid standards the way we're doing it in Australia right now."

**The Barrier Being Addressed:**
The key challenge is **battery warranty concerns** from vehicle manufacturers (OEMs). If a vehicle has an 80,000-mile warranty but the battery is used for home energy with zero miles driven, how does the warranty work?

**BYD has stepped forward** as the first manufacturer to warrant their vehicles for this program, with ARENA looking to expand to other manufacturers.

**Surprising Early Evidence:**
Miller shares insight from Amber's CEO: "It may actually be helping the life of the battery by cycling it short amounts actually improves the quality of the battery over time."

**Why Australia?**
The opportunity is particularly strong in Australia given **30%+ rooftop solar penetration** and rapidly growing home battery adoption. Car batteries (60-80 kWh) are significantly larger than typical home batteries, creating substantial potential for grid services.

### Transportation Electrification: Australia's Lagging Sector

**The Reality Check:**
Liebreich challenges Miller on Australia's **relatively poor EV adoption** - around 10% before the Hormuz crisis, now 15%, which is "below the world average."

**Miller's Defense:**
He notes Australia also has **22% penetration including plug-in hybrids**, which people choose due to "the distances we travel" in a country with "very sparse population, but large land mass."

**The Hormuz Crisis Effect:**
Miller provides fascinating evidence of plug-in hybrid behavior: During the crisis, **EV charging network utilization "went through the roof"** as plug-in hybrid owners who normally used petrol suddenly switched to charging. This proves plug-in hybrids provide **resilience** - even if not always used electrically, they *could* be.

**Policy Changes:**
The current government has introduced **New Vehicle Efficiency Standards** creating signals for manufacturers to increase EV sales. However, Australia remains "world champions" in an unwanted category: **diesel subsidies and per capita diesel use**.

**The Diesel Subsidy Issue:**
Originally justified because fuel excise tax funded roads, so off-road users (miners, agriculture) got rebates. However, Miller reveals: "That system stopped in 1992. So the fuel tax is general taxation" - yet the rebates continue. There's now debate about switching to a **road user charge based on kilometers traveled**, though it's "quite a contested space."

### Sustainable Aviation Fuel: The Cost Challenge

**The New Priority:**
After the Hormuz crisis, the government gave ARENA **$1.1 billion for sustainable aviation fuel (SAF)** development, partially offsetting the hydrogen funding cut.

**The Cost Reality:**
Miller is frank about the challenge: Current technologies cost **$800-$1,000 per ton of CO2 abatement**. Australia has "huge feedstocks" (used cooking oils, tallow, canola, sugarcane, bagasse, forestry waste) that are currently "sent overseas for processing and then we buy back the sustainable aviation fuel."

**The Live Debate:**
The government wants **sovereign capability** for fuel security, but the question is "how are we going to divvy up the subsidy between governments and the taxpayer or the consumer paying extra on their air ticket."

**Liebreich's Intervention:**
He advocates against **blending mandates**, preferring **dual auctions** (one to buy from producers, one to sell to airlines) for price transparency and the ability to kill unsuccessful programs. "The big problem with blending mandates, they're very difficult to kill."

## 3. Frameworks & Mental Models

### ARENA's Investment Philosophy: "Benevolent Equity Investor"

Miller describes ARENA's approach: "I like to think of ARENA as a benevolent equity investor in that we have the mindset of an equity investor" - conducting thorough diligence on teams, project definitions, and finances - "but ultimately, we want to give our money to that project and not see that money come back because... it is uncommercial."

**Key Principles:**
- **Project-focused, not company-focused:** ARENA funds specific experiments or deployments, not general working capital
- **Co-investment requirement:** Target of **1:1 matching** - "a dollar from ARENA, a dollar from third-party investors"
- **Innovation requirement:** Must be testing something new, not just funding business-as-usual
- **Knowledge sharing mandate:** Projects must "open up as much as possible" about learnings (while respecting IP)
- **Non-dilutive funding:** Money doesn't come back, providing crucial support without equity dilution

**The "Fund of Funds" Solution:**
For smaller companies struggling with ARENA's heavy process, they've developed a **re-granting model**: giving $10 million to the Australian Manufacturing Growth Centre (AMGC) to distribute as $500,000 grants, or funding Energy Lab incubator for startups.

### The "Prove and Step Away" Model

Miller articulates ARENA's success metric: "When we see a problem and we solve it with money and a program, we like to think we can just step away and the market takes over from there."

**Example:** The grid-forming battery program. After proving the business case with targeted funding, "the vast majority of big batteries going into the market... are all going in with grid-forming capabilities" without further ARENA intervention.

### The Two-Track Technology Development Approach

Demonstrated in solar:
1. **Upstream R&D:** University research on efficiency improvements (UNSW's A-CAP program)
2. **Downstream deployment innovation:** Real-world experimentation with installation methods, robotics, AI (Fortescue's Mega Demand program)

Miller: "These two things should come together within the next few years to prove we can manufacture electrons cleanly in Australia at under $20 a megawatt hour."

### Separating Technology from Economics in Industrial Strategy

For green steel, Miller explicitly separates:
1. **Technical feasibility:** Can we process Pilbara ores in a low-carbon way?
2. **Economic location:** Where can this be done most cost-effectively?

This framework acknowledges that **proving technology in Australia doesn't guarantee Australian production** - it might be more economical in China or the Middle East. The honest assessment prevents wishful thinking in industrial policy.

## 4. Contrarian Takes

### On Hydrogen IP and Open Source Benefits

When Liebreich questions whether Australia was "naive" letting PERC cell technology end up in Chinese modules without licensing revenue, Miller offers a contrarian defense:

**"Having this proliferate... is the real benefit for the globe, for Australia from a climate perspective... it's been so open source... taken up by Chinese manufacturers to produce it at very low cost... we've all benefited from extremely low module prices and the decarbonization benefit."**

This challenges the Mariana Mazzucato "entrepreneurial state" thesis that governments should capture returns from publicly-funded innovation. Miller argues the **climate and cost benefits of rapid proliferation exceed potential licensing revenue**.

### On Plug-in Hybrids as Resilience Technology

While acknowledging that plug-in hybrids are "never plugged in" (a common criticism), Miller reframes them as **resilience infrastructure**. The Hormuz crisis proved owners *will* switch to electric mode when needed, and some will stick with charging afterward. This challenges the binary "pure EV or nothing" perspective.

### On Blue Hydrogen vs. Green Hydrogen

Liebreich's contrarian position (which Miller doesn't fully embrace but engages with): **Blue hydrogen will remain cheaper than green** due to fundamental energy intensity of electrolysis. This challenges the renewable energy community's preference for green hydrogen and raises questions about ARENA's mandate constraints.

### On Community vs. Home Batteries

The **community battery model** challenges the assumption that distributed home batteries are optimal. Miller argues for **portfolio effects and cost-effectiveness per unit energy** at neighborhood scale, though he notes "it's not kind of one or the other" - both have roles.

### On Australia's Manufacturing Competitiveness

Miller offers a refreshingly honest assessment: "I'm not yet convinced we can do the midstream [green steel processing] as cheaply because we're an expensive country to do business in." This contrasts with more optimistic narratives about Australia becoming a renewable energy manufacturing superpower.

## 5. Practical Implications

### For Energy Markets

**Electricity Price Trajectory Shift:**
Miller identifies a **"new phenomenon"** - after years of rising electricity prices, they're "finally... coming down because of the role that batteries are playing displacing gas in our system." Since gas sets prices, battery penetration creates a **structural downward pressure on wholesale prices**.

**Implication:** Markets with high gas price-setting and increasing battery deployment should expect similar dynamics.

### For Grid Planning

**The Grid-Forming Imperative:**
As grids approach high renewable penetration (South Australia at 75%), **grid-forming capability becomes non-negotiable**. ARENA's intervention proves that **targeted subsidies can shift entire market behavior** - now standard practice in Australia.

**Implication:** Grid operators globally should identify similar technical barriers and consider strategic interventions before they become crises.

### For Industrial Decarbonization

**The Electricity Cost Threshold:**
For green steel to work in Australia, renewable electricity must be **cheaper than China's**. Miller's $20/MWh target provides a benchmark. But electricity is only half the equation - **capital costs of processing plants** must also be competitive.

**Implication:** Countries considering green industrial strategies must honestly assess both energy costs AND capital/operating cost competitiveness, not just assume cheap renewables guarantee success.

### For Hydrogen Strategy

**Focus on Hard-to-Abate Sectors:**
ARENA's experience suggests **avoiding hydrogen for applications with better alternatives** (like road transport or gas blending) and concentrating on **genuine hard-to-abate uses** like green steel.

**Implication:** Hydrogen strategies should be ruthlessly prioritized based on lack of alternatives, not breadth of applications.

### For EV Adoption

**The Warranty Innovation Requirement:**
Vehicle-to-grid deployment requires **rethinking warranty structures** from mileage-based to cycle-based or chemistry-based metrics. BYD's willingness to pioneer this creates competitive advantage.

**Implication:** OEMs that solve warranty concerns for V2G will access new revenue streams and customer value propositions.

### For Technology Funding Agencies

**The Re-granting Model:**
ARENA's solution to the "too small to fund directly" problem - **giving money to intermediaries** like AMGC or Energy Lab - provides a template for agencies struggling to support early-stage companies without excessive bureaucracy.

**Implication:** Large government funding bodies should develop **tiered funding mechanisms** rather than trying to make one process fit all scales.

### For Climate Policy

**The Dual Auction Advantage:**
Liebreich's advocacy for **dual auctions over blending mandates** for SAF offers a framework for other hard-to-abate sectors: maximize price transparency and maintain ability to terminate unsuccessful programs.

**Implication:** Policy mechanisms should be designed with **clear exit criteria** and **transparent cost revelation**.
