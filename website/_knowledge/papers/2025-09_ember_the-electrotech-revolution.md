## 1. Research Question & Contribution

The deck is Ember Futures' first annual "state of the transition" report, and its stated purpose is to propose a third lens for understanding the energy system beyond the two that currently dominate: the **incumbent view** (fossil-centric, slow, business-as-usual, epitomized by EIA-style primary-energy charts that show renewables as a thin sliver on top of a still-growing fossil base) and the **climate view** (emissions-centric, policy-driven, epitomized by IPCC pathways that require near-vertical emissions cuts to hit temperature targets). Both, the authors argue, are poor descriptions of what is actually happening.

The proposed **electrotech view** reframes the transition as a technology revolution rather than a fuel-switching or moral project. "Electrotech" is defined as the family of technologies that generate, connect, and use electrons — solar PV, wind, batteries, EVs, heat pumps, power electronics, HVDC transmission, smart meters/grids — unified by the fact that they are manufactured products subject to Wright's Law learning curves and exponential S-curve adoption, unlike fossil fuels, which are extracted commodities subject to depletion economics. The contribution is a reframing/synthesis rather than new primary data: nearly every chart is sourced from IEA, Ember's own datasets, BNEF, IRENA, RMI, Rystad, or academic literature, but organized around a single thesis.

**Core thesis:** Electrotech has crossed a threshold of cost and manufacturing scale where it is now cheaper than fossil incumbents in most applications. Because it benefits from learning curves (getting cheaper with scale) rather than depletion curves (getting more expensive with extraction), and because it is currently growing exponentially on S-curves, the trajectory is now essentially locked in regardless of climate policy. "If current exponentials hold for five more years, global fossil demand will fall off its plateau."

## 2. Methodology

This is a synthesis/data-visualization report, not an empirical study. Its method is:
- **Cross-source aggregation:** pulling deployment, cost, and trade data from ~15 distinct sources (Ember, IEA, IRENA, BNEF, Rystad, RMI, IIASA, Comtrade, Global Energy Monitor, World Bank, Energy Institute, etc.) into a single consistent narrative.
- **Historical framing devices:** borrowing Carlota Perez's "technology wave" framework (steam/rail → steel/electricity → oil/mass production → digital → electrotech as the "6th wave") and Ian Morris's energy-capture-per-capita framework (foragers → farmers → fossils → photovoltaics) to situate electrotech within long-run economic history.
- **S-curve/learning-curve extrapolation:** several forward-looking charts (pp. 73, 79-80, 96) are Ember Futures' own extrapolations of historical learning rates and growth rates (solar 15-18% growth, 20-30% learning rate; wind 8-13% growth, 12-17% learning rate; batteries 19-22% growth, 19-29% learning rate) under "Fast" and "Faster" scenarios — these are the only genuinely novel modelling outputs in the deck.
- **Case-study evidence:** country and sector case studies (China, emerging-market EV leapfrogging, Africa solar imports, UK electricity tax structure) are used as illustrative proof-points rather than a representative sample.

## 3. Main Findings

**Costs have collapsed and crossed over.** Since 1980, solar module prices have fallen 99.6%, wind installation costs 80% (since 1984), battery cell prices 99% (since 1991). Wind LCOE has fallen 58% since 2014, solar 77%, battery costs 84%. Electrotech is now cheaper than fossil alternatives across generation, EVs, and heat pumps on both LCOE and total-cost-of-ownership bases (p.17, p.67).

**Growth has been, and continues to be, exponential.** Solar capacity has doubled roughly every three years for three decades; battery storage has been nearly doubling annually since 2020 (p.12). Manufacturing capacity for solar (1,600 GW) and batteries (13 TWh) already exceeds even the IEA's Net Zero demand projections through 2028-2030 (p.18) — i.e., the supply-side bottleneck has already been solved.

**Fossil fuel demand is peaking sector-by-sector and region-by-region, not all at once.** Two-thirds of the world's population already lives in a country or sector past peak fossil demand for electricity or final energy (p.39). Peaks have cascaded: industrial fossil demand peaked in 2014, buildings in 2018, road transport in 2019. China — which drove 95% of net global fossil growth since 2018 (p.41) — shows early signs of its own fossil electricity plateau in H1 2025, which the authors argue implies a *global* peak given China's outsized share of incremental demand.

**Electrotech is three times more efficient than the fossil system it replaces**, because fossil combustion wastes roughly two-thirds of primary energy as heat (worth an estimated $4.6 trillion/year globally), while electrotech converts electrons to useful work directly (solar-to-electricity ~100% vs. fossil thermal 30-40%; EVs 80-90% vs. ICE 25-40%; heat pumps 300-400% vs. gas boilers 85%) (pp.45-51).

**China is the dominant electrostate.** It leads on patents (cleantech patent applications now ~5x the rest of the world combined, p.89), manufacturing (batteries, solar), exports (solar PV, batteries, EVs to emerging markets and the EU, p.70), and deployment. Chinese electrotech leaders (CATL, BYD, LONGi, XPeng, Nio) disproportionately trace back to founders who cut their teeth in digital tech / consumer electronics manufacturing (p.86) — the report's framing is that electrotech is literally "the child of digital tech," sharing components (batteries, processors, inverters) and inheriting its cost-decline dynamics.

**Emerging markets are leapfrogging, not following the West's path.** 63% of emerging-market populations are already ahead of the US in solar generation share; 25% are ahead in electrification share (p.28). Nepal, Ethiopia, and Thailand show EV sales shares rising faster than China's did at the same stage (p.77). This is framed as analogous to mobile-phone leapfrogging of landlines.

**Renewables + electrification could triple their share of the energy system** using only currently economic technology — from ~20% of final energy today to ~75%+ technically/economically electrifiable (heating, road transport, most industrial process heat), leaving only hard-to-abate segments (aviation, shipping, high-temperature industrial heat, chemicals feedstock) outside near-term reach (p.32).

**Energy security, not climate, is now the primary driver.** Three-quarters of the world's population lives in fossil-fuel-importing countries; a quarter spend over 5% of GDP on fossil imports (p.62). Renewables are available to every country (92% of the world's population has "replete" or better renewable resource, i.e., >10x their energy demand, p.63) and, once installed, remove ongoing import dependency — the report frames this as a shift "from fossil import dependency, to electrotech import dependency [during buildout], to full circular energy independence" (p.68).

## 4. Mechanisms & Interpretation

The report's causal chain rests on three "fundamental drivers" that it argues explain *why* the trend is structural rather than cyclical or policy-contingent (Chapter 4, pp.43-71):

- **Physics:** electrotech has an inherent thermodynamic efficiency advantage (2-4x depending on end use) that no policy choice can undo — it is a law of nature, not a subsidy effect.
- **Economics:** electrotech is manufactured, modular, and small-unit (a solar panel costs ~$100 and deploys in 1-6 months vs. a coal plant at ~$3bn and 4-6 years), which enables Wright's-Law learning curves; fossil fuels are extracted and face depletion economics where costs rise as reserves are exhausted. The report explicitly contrasts "learning beats digging" (p.53).
- **Geopolitics:** because renewable resource is locally available everywhere, electrotech converts an import dependency (fossil fuels, which must be "bought again every day") into a one-time capital deployment that then yields ~30 years of energy independence (the solar panel "period of freedom" framing, p.66).

A secondary interpretive frame is the **"virtuous spiral"** (p.75): cheaper renewables → more electrification → more demand to justify flexibility/storage investment → smarter grids → higher renewables penetration → repeat. This is offered as the mechanism by which early gains compound rather than plateau.

The authors also address *why* incumbents resist despite the economics: fossil fuel rents (~$1.8-2.4 trillion/year), fossil fuel subsidies (rising to ~$700bn by 2023), and US oil & gas lobbying (~$150mn/year and rising) create strong incentives for incumbent resistance even as the underlying technology economics have already flipped (p.102) — echoing a "profitable status quo blocks efficient change" political-economy argument rather than a technology or cost argument.

## 5. Limitations & Caveats

- **This is advocacy-adjacent research from a mission-driven think tank**, not neutral academic output. Ember's stated vision is "a world with a safe climate, powered by a clean, electrified energy system for all" — the report should be read as a well-sourced brief for that constituency, not a disinterested forecast. Charts are consistently framed to support the thesis (e.g., "CCS is about to take off!" mocked as a recurring false narrative on p.101, wind/solar skeptics similarly dismissed) rather than steelmanning counter-cases.
- **Forward projections (2025-2050) are extrapolations of historical learning/growth rates**, explicitly labeled "Fast" and "Faster" scenarios — there is no "Slow" or policy-reversal downside case shown, which likely understates the real distribution of outcomes given the political backlash dynamics documented elsewhere in this knowledge base (see the Cleaning Up S16/S17 podcast summaries on Trump-era rollback, UK affordability backlash, etc.).
- **The "final 10%" / hard-to-abate problem is under-treated.** The deck's own data shows aviation, shipping, steel, cement, and chemicals remaining largely unelectrified even in the 2025 "electrification potential" chart (p.32) — these are acknowledged but not analyzed in depth, consistent with the broader "90/10 rule" theme found across the Cleaning Up podcast's Season 16 guests (Ritchie, Turner, Liebreich), who argue the last-mile cost curve is fundamentally different from the first 90%.
- **China-dependency risk is stated but not stress-tested.** The report celebrates China's electrotech dominance (patents, manufacturing, exports) but does not model what happens to the global buildout trajectory under a serious US-China decoupling or supply-chain disruption scenario, despite flagging (elsewhere, e.g. the Cleaning Up podcast) that concentrated import dependency on Chinese solar panels can itself be a security vulnerability (a tension explicitly raised by Arunabha Ghosh's "India's renewable imports are MORE concentrated than oil imports" argument in Ep226).
- **No discussion of grid integration costs or curtailment economics at higher penetrations** — the deck stops at "70-80% penetration is within reach" (p.31) without engaging with the well-documented rising system-cost curve beyond that point (a major theme of the parallel Cleaning Up podcast literature on the "last mile problem").

## 6. Implications

**For macro/markets:**
- Electrotech investment is now ~2x fossil investment globally ($1.8-2.0 trillion/year vs. ~$1.0-1.1 trillion), and solar capex alone now exceeds oil capex (p.14) — a genuine capital-allocation regime shift, not a marginal trend.
- Refining margins face structural pressure as gasoline/fuel-oil/other-products demand (42%+13%+2% of a typical barrel's yield) starts to decline while jet/naphtha/LPG grow — only complex refineries can rebalance output; simpler ones face margin compression (p.81).
- A "battle of overcapacities" is emerging between LNG (new US supply chasing shrinking addressable demand) and solar PV (Chinese manufacturing capacity far exceeding even aggressive demand forecasts) — both industries will be pushing into new markets to absorb excess capacity through the rest of the decade (p.82).
- Oil supply-cost-curve analysis shows petrostate fiscal breakevens (Saudi Arabia $98/bbl, Iraq $84, Kuwait $88, UAE $51) are already above or near current price ranges — even modest demand declines disproportionately squeeze the highest-cost marginal producers (US shale, deepwater) first (p.93).
- Ports, terminals, and shipping tied to fossil fuel volumes (45% of global shipping mass, ~$3 trillion/year in value, p.94) represent a stranding-risk asset class as volumes decline.

**For policy:**
- The deck's explicit policy prescription (Chapter 6, p.109) is to focus government effort on removing deployment bottlenecks (connection queues: 1,600 GW of solar and 1,000+ GW of wind stuck in queues, p.103) rather than on subsidizing unproven technologies (CCS, biofuels, and green hydrogen are scored as failing on physics/economics/geopolitics tests versus electrotech, p.100).
- It also argues for taxing what you want to phase out rather than the reverse — several EU countries currently tax electricity more heavily than gas (Denmark, Germany, Netherlands, Belgium among the worst offenders), which the authors argue actively slows electrification (p.108).
