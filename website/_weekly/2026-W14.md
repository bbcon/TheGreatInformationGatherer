The week of March 30 began with oil markets extending their historic rally as the Iran conflict entered its fifth week, while global equities approached correction territory amid mounting stagflation concerns. Markets whipsawed on conflicting diplomatic signals midweek before settling into a pattern of energy-driven inflation pressures overwhelming traditional safe-haven dynamics.

**Historic Oil Rally Drives Stagflation Fears**

Energy markets dominated the week's narrative as crude oil maintained unprecedented gains despite brief diplomatic hope.

- Brent crude fluctuated between $107-116/barrel, with the month-to-date gain of 60% representing the largest monthly increase on record
- WTI held above $101/barrel with the Brent-WTI spread widening to approximately $15
- The Strait of Hormuz closure continued disrupting 10 million barrels per day of supply, forcing only trickle traffic through the critical chokepoint
- US gasoline breached the psychologically important $4.00/gallon threshold on Tuesday, reaching $4.02 - the first time above $4 since August 2022
- Supply disruption risks expanded beyond Hormuz as Houthis targeted Red Sea shipping routes, with Saudi Arabia's Yanbu port (7 million bpd capacity) potentially forcing African rerouting at $15-20/barrel premium

The conflict escalated despite diplomatic signals, with Iran striking a Kuwaiti tanker off Dubai on Tuesday just hours after reports of potential US exit strategies. Power outages in eastern Iran following explosions suggested ground reality diverged sharply from diplomatic rhetoric, while analysts maintained a 40% probability of $200 oil if Hormuz closure extends through June.

**Global Equity Carnage Exceeds COVID Losses**

Equity markets suffered their worst monthly destruction on record, surpassing even the March 2020 COVID crash.

- Global equities destroyed $11-12 trillion in market value during March, with the MSCI All-Country World Index falling 9.4% from peak - just 0.6% from technical correction territory
- Asian markets bore the brunt of selling pressure due to energy import vulnerability, with the Nikkei falling 4.5% on Monday reflecting Japan's 90% Middle East oil dependency
- South Korea's Kospi declined 3.5% and finished the month down 17% - a dramatic reversal from being the best global performer pre-war to the worst performer
- China's CSI 300 tested its 200-day moving average at 4,500 on Monday for the first time since Liberation Day
- The Nasdaq 100 entered bear territory, falling 11% from peak, while the VIX sustained above 30 for the first time since the Liberation Day spike to 50

Technology sectors faced additional headwinds beyond energy concerns, with memory chips particularly vulnerable to Google's breakthrough Turbo Quant technology promising 6x memory reduction efficiency.

**Central Banks Face Impossible Policy Trade-offs**

Monetary authorities confronted mounting inflation pressures while growth risks emerged, creating an unprecedented policy dilemma.

- Eurozone CPI jumped to 2.5% year-over-year in March - the largest increase since 2022, with Germany accelerating faster than expected at 2.6%
- UK inflation expectations reached 4.5% on 2-year zero-coupon swaps, 250 basis points above the 2% target
- Rate cuts were effectively priced out for 2025, with the ECB facing pressure for two 25bp hikes while the Bank of England confronted similar tightening demands
- Fed Chair Powell attempted to calm markets on Tuesday by describing energy-driven inflation pass-through as potentially "transitory," helping bond markets stabilize despite March CPI previewing at 3.4%

Asian central banks faced particularly acute constraints, unable to raise rates due to currency and growth concerns while importing higher energy costs. The Reserve Bank of India implemented $100 million daily position limits for banks in rupee defense measures, highlighting the impossible trade-offs between currency stability and growth support.

**China Shows Resilience Amid Regional Vulnerability**

Chinese economic data provided a rare bright spot while broader Asia struggled with energy import dependency.

- China Manufacturing PMI beat expectations at 50.4 versus 50.1 expected - the highest reading in 12 months
- Non-Manufacturing PMI reached 50.1 versus 49.9 expected, suggesting economic resilience despite energy disruption
- Chinese equities remained relatively flat while regional peers declined, with Moutai gaining 4% on Monday following its first price hike in three years
- BYD initially fell 5% on Monday after reporting a 38% Q4 profit plunge, but recovered to flat as management warned of a "brutal knockout stage" in EV sector competition

However, global long-only funds turned neutral on China equities in February for the first time since 2022, reflecting lingering structural concerns despite near-term resilience.

**Commodities Beyond Oil Face Supply Shocks**

Energy wasn't the only commodity experiencing dramatic supply disruptions during the week.

- Aluminum surged 5-6% on Monday after Iranian strikes damaged Gulf smelters including Emirates Global Aluminum and Aluminium Bahrain facilities
- The damaged facilities represented 9% of global aluminum production, with Chinese producers like Chalco rallying 7-9%
- Japan aluminum premiums reached decade highs due to supply constraints
- Gold maintained its position above $4,500/ounce for three consecutive sessions, remaining well above fair value estimates of $2,900 as one of the few safe havens functioning effectively

**Week Ahead**

Key developments to monitor include:

- US March employment report and wage growth data for inflation implications
- Further diplomatic developments in Iran conflict and potential Hormuz reopening progress
- China Q1 GDP preliminary estimates and March trade balance
- ECB policy meeting minutes and potential hawkish guidance
- Corporate earnings season beginning with major energy and financial sector results

The week established a clear stagflationary backdrop as energy supply shocks drove inflation expectations higher while growth concerns mounted. Markets appeared to be transitioning from the disinflationary regime of recent years toward a more challenging environment where central banks must choose between fighting inflation and supporting growth.
